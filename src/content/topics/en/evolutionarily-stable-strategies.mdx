In a model population, animals contest a resource. Hawks fight; doves withdraw from a hawk and share with another dove. When hawks are rare, aggression can pay. When hawks are common and injuries are costly, a dove may do better by avoiding fights. A behaviour's success depends on the population it meets.

An **evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS)** resists invasion by sufficiently rare alternatives under the model's payoff and population assumptions. It is a stronger condition than simply being a Nash equilibrium. If an alternative ties against the resident strategy, you must also compare their performance in encounters involving the alternative; checking only the resident population can miss the decisive condition.

Building on game theory, natural selection and cooperation, you will run a Hawk–Dove invasion lab and derive the standard mixed result: when resource value V is positive and below injury cost C, the equilibrium hawk proportion is V/C. You will distinguish that population mixture from an individual randomising, then examine frequency-dependent selection and cooperation. Stability does not imply an efficient or desirable outcome. Nor does every evolutionary process settle into one: cycles, changing environments and the absence of an ESS are possibilities the model must leave open.
