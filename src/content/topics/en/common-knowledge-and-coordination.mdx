Two teammates receive the same meeting time in separate messages. Each knows when to arrive, but neither knows whether the other received the message. A shared announcement can change their expectations even though the time stays the same: they can now see that both have been informed.

**Mutual knowledge** means that everyone knows a fact. **Common knowledge** adds successive levels: everyone knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on. That recursive definition is an idealisation. The meeting announcement approaches it only if attendance, visibility and attention are themselves understood by the group.

Building on Nash equilibrium, critical mass and game theory, the course traces how beliefs about other people's beliefs affect coordination. An interactive coordinator lets you compare private and public signals before examining collective action and cascades. You will ask what a signal actually establishes, who receives it and what each participant expects others to do. Shared knowledge can help people coordinate, but conflicting incentives, mistrust or an unreliable channel can still prevent agreement.
