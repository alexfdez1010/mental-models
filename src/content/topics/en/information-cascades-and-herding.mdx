You receive a weak signal that restaurant A is better, but the two diners ahead of you choose B. If their choices appear to reflect better information, following them can make sense. The next diner then sees three choices for B, without knowing that yours added no independent evidence.

An **information cascade** occurs when people following earlier actions stop letting their own private signals change their choices. Later observers see agreement, but cannot tell how much independent information supports it. Such a cascade can settle on the right option or the wrong one and can be vulnerable to new public evidence.

This expert course builds on Bayesian updating, common knowledge and critical mass. You'll work through an urn example, trace what each choice reveals, and distinguish information-based herding from reputational conformity and genuine shared evidence. You'll then examine designs that preserve independent judgments before group discussion. A queue or a popular book alone does not prove a cascade: quality, convenience and common information may explain the same pattern. The model earns its place when you can identify what people observe and what their actions stop revealing.
