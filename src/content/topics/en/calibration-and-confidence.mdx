You label 100 forecasts as 70% likely. If about 70 occur, that group of forecasts is well calibrated. If only 40 occur, the problem is visible in the record even if every forecast felt convincing when you made it. One successful prediction cannot establish calibration; you need a collection of resolved forecasts.

**Calibration** means that stated probabilities correspond to observed frequencies. It is one part of forecast quality. Always repeating the base rate can be calibrated while failing to distinguish easier cases from harder ones. **Resolution** captures that useful differentiation. Good forecasting needs both, along with enough observations to assess them fairly.

Building on probability and Bayesian updating, the course uses an interactive calibration lab to compare confidence with results. You will read reliability curves, distinguish overestimation from excessive precision and score binary forecasts with the **Brier score** and log score. You will also practise expressing estimates as intervals and checking their coverage. The record gives you feedback about particular questions and conditions; it does not produce an exact, permanent measure of how trustworthy every future judgment will be.
