In one hour, Ana can prepare four reports or two diagrams. Ben can prepare two reports or one and a half diagrams. Ana is faster at both tasks. Yet one diagram costs her two reports, while it costs Ben only four thirds of a report. Ben gives up fewer reports to make a diagram.

That lower **opportunity cost** is his **comparative advantage**. Ana's higher output is an **absolute advantage**, a different comparison. In this example, shifting diagram work towards Ben and report work towards Ana can increase what they produce together. A suitable exchange can let both benefit; who receives those gains depends on the terms.

Building on opportunity cost and supply and demand, you will calculate relative costs in two-good examples, explore possible specialisation in an interactive tool and apply the reasoning to delegation and trade. The arithmetic rests on assumptions about productivity and feasible alternatives. Coordination costs, retraining, changing demand and the distribution of gains can alter a practical decision. “I am faster at this” is relevant information, but it does not settle which task is the best use of your time.
