An experiment costs \$100 if it fails and returns a net \$900 if it succeeds. With a 10% success probability, its expected net value is zero: \$90 from the success branch minus \$90 from the failure branch. The large upside alone does not make it a good bet. You also need the probabilities, the price and the ability to absorb the loss.

**Asymmetry** means that the possible gains and losses differ in size. **Optionality** gives you a choice you can exercise when it helps and leave unused when it does not. A pilot project, for instance, may let you test demand before committing to a full launch. You pay for information and flexibility, and you still need a plan for acting on what you learn.

The course develops these ideas through asymmetric bets, convex and concave payoff curves, Jensen's inequality and barbell strategies. The payoff explorer lets you change probabilities and amounts to see why winning frequently and finishing ahead are different measures. Building on fat tails and margins of safety, you will examine the costs of keeping options open, the danger of hidden downside and the limits imposed by correlated failures and ruin. A capped loss is useful only if that cap is real and affordable.
