A search method works well when good answers tend to lie near other good answers. Give it a problem where neighbouring answers tell you nothing about one another, and that useful shortcut may disappear. Its advantage came from a feature of the problems it was built to solve.

The **No Free Lunch theorem** makes this idea precise for certain mathematical settings. When performance is averaged uniformly over all possible objective functions under the theorem's assumptions, search methods have the same average performance. Doing well on a relevant class of problems requires a useful match between the method and that class.

This beginner course makes the reasoning visible with a small example you can count. You'll separate the formal result from broader lessons about choosing tools, learn why it does **not** make all methods equally useful in practice, and ask what assumptions support a proposed shortcut. Real problems are rarely drawn uniformly from every imaginable possibility. Their structure is exactly what lets a well-chosen method help.
