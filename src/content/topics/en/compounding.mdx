Start with 100 and add 10 each year: after two years you have 120. Instead, grow the current total by 10% each year: it becomes 110, then 121. The extra 1 appears because the second year's growth also acts on the first year's gain.

That is **compounding**: changes accumulate on a base that previous changes have already altered. For a constant rate, the formula multiplies the starting amount by the same growth factor each period. The rate, the number of periods and whether gains remain in the system all matter. A smooth exponential curve shows the assumptions working together; it does not promise that a real process will keep that rate forever.

This beginner course starts from addition and multiplication, then builds the formula and the **Rule of 72**, an approximation for doubling time. Interactive curves and saver comparisons let you vary contributions, timing and growth. You will also examine losses: a 50% fall requires a 100% gain to recover. Skills and knowledge can reinforce later learning, but applying a fixed financial rate to them is an analogy to test, rather than a measured law.
