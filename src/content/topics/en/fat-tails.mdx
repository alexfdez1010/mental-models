Nine projects each cost \$10,000. A tenth costs \$200,000. The median cost is \$10,000, but the mean is \$29,000. Both numbers are correct and answer different questions. A single large result has changed the total enough that a plan based only on the typical project would miss much of the spending.

A **fat-tailed distribution** gives extreme outcomes more weight than a normal-distribution model would suggest. One unusual observation does not establish a fat tail, and different tail shapes have different consequences. Some have a well-defined mean and variance; others do not. Even when those quantities exist, estimating them from a short record can be difficult.

Building on thinking in probabilities, you will compare normal and power-law models, vary a tail in an interactive sampler and examine how extreme draws change totals. The course connects those observations to black swans, the turkey problem and irreversible losses. You will distinguish an event missing from the record from one that cannot occur, while checking which distribution is justified by the evidence. The practical aim is to test how much a decision relies on a reassuring average or a fragile tail estimate, rather than treating every rare outcome as predictable after giving it a name.
