A team discusses a project deadline. The first person says six weeks; after the discussion, everyone offers a similar estimate. Now imagine collecting estimates privately before anyone speaks. Differences become visible, and the team can investigate the assumptions behind them. The procedure changes what you can check before a shared number takes over.

The **bias blind spot** is the tendency to see your own judgments as less biased than other people's. Recognising the name of a bias does not establish that you have avoided it. **Debiasing** means trying to reduce systematic errors through how you reason or how the decision is organised, then evaluating whether the change helps.

Building on confirmation bias and calibration, you will compare considering the opposite, the outside view, pre-mortems, checklists, independent estimates, red teams and pre-commitment. A debiasing bench illustrates how these tools address particular problems; its displayed scores are modelled examples, not measured evidence of their effectiveness. Different decisions need different procedures. A checklist can omit the critical issue, disagreement can be uninformative and an expensive review may cost more than a small decision warrants. You will practise choosing a check for an identifiable failure mode and using feedback to assess it.
