In a meeting, each person praises a proposal. Afterwards, several admit privately that they expected it to fail. Their public statements gave everyone else a misleading picture of support, and that apparent support made speaking up harder.

**Preference falsification** is expressing a public preference that differs from the one you privately hold because of the consequences of revealing it. The model developed by Timur Kuran helps explain how social or professional costs can sustain apparent agreement, and how that agreement can change quickly when the cost of dissent falls.

Building on incentives, information cascades and common knowledge, you'll separate public statements from private beliefs, follow the thresholds that can trigger a cascade, and examine how persistent silence damages the information available to a group. You'll also practise the model's most important limit: silence does not reveal what someone believes. A hidden majority is one possibility; genuine agreement, indifference and a mixture of views are others. Use the model to improve how opinions are gathered, rather than to assume that everyone secretly agrees with you.
