Two colleagues can help each other meet deadlines. Helping costs time today, while reliable help in return makes later work easier. If they will never work together again, that future benefit disappears. If they expect many more projects, today's choice can affect a valuable relationship.

The **evolution of cooperation** asks how helping behaviours can persist when some participants gain by taking benefits without contributing. The answer depends on the interaction: its payoffs, how often partners meet again, what they can observe and how mistakes are handled. Natural selection can favour cooperation under some conditions; it does not require every organism or every relationship to be cooperative.

Building on natural selection and Nash equilibrium, you will play an iterated prisoner's dilemma and compare evolving populations of strategies. The course examines **tit-for-tat**, generous and contrite variants, and **win-stay, lose-shift**, then extends the analysis to reputation, kin selection, Hamilton's rule and networks. Noise can turn retaliation into a cycle of mistaken punishment, while a strategy that succeeds against one population may fail against another. You will use those differences to ask which conditions support cooperation, keeping an evolutionary explanation separate from a claim about what people ought to do.
