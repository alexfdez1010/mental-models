Two used cars look alike. One is reliable; the other needs expensive repairs. Their owners know the difference, but you cannot check it before buying. You offer a price that reflects the average quality. The owner of the reliable car refuses; the owner of the faulty one accepts. Your sensible offer has changed the selection of cars you can buy.

**Adverse selection** is this sorting effect: hidden information about quality or risk influences who enters a deal. If better-quality sellers withdraw, buyers may lower their offers again, pushing more of those sellers out. The course works through that mechanism with numbers before applying it to insurance, lending, hiring and online marketplaces. The question is close to the [winner's curse](/winners-curse): what does getting the deal reveal about it?

You will compare **signalling**, where the informed side provides evidence, with **screening**, where the less informed side offers choices that reveal differences. Inspections, warranties and reputation can help, but each has costs and conditions. Hidden information does not make every market collapse. You will also separate adverse selection from **moral hazard**, the [principal–agent problem](/principal-agent-problem) of hidden actions after an agreement, so that the remedy addresses the actual problem.
