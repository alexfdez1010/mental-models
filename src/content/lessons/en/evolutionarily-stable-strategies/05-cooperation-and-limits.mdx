You have spent four lessons learning to distrust a comforting story. A behaviour is *not* stable because it looks clever, or because it "helps the species," or because a documentary narrator said so in a hushed voice. It is stable only if it survives the **invasion test**: a strategy $I$ is an ESS when, for every mutant $J$, either $E(I,I) > E(J,I)$, or $E(I,I) = E(J,I)$ with $E(I,J) > E(J,J)$.

This final teaching lesson turns that lens on the most emotionally loaded question in all of evolutionary game theory — **can cooperation be stable?** — and then, having earned the right, catalogues honestly everywhere the model quietly lies to you.

## Repetition changes the game: the iterated prisoner's dilemma

In the **one-shot prisoner's dilemma**, defection dominates. Whatever your partner does, you earn more by defecting, so a population of cooperators is trivially invaded — cooperation is not an ESS, full stop.

For the constructed example, the same pair continues after each round with probability $w=3/4$, independently of the actions and previous history. A policy specifies an action after each possible history. Score it using the **normalized expected total**

$$F=(1-w)\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}w^r u_r.$$

Here $u_r$ is its expected round payoff conditional on the interaction reaching that round. The factor $w^r$ weights survival; the common positive factor $1-w$ normalizes the score. This is **not** the arithmetic mean of the per-round payoffs in a realised match of random length. Holding w fixed, the normalization preserves payoff inequalities. We must also state which policies and mixtures are allowed.

The famous conditional strategy is **Tit-for-Tat (TFT)**: cooperate on the first move, then copy whatever your opponent did last round. Nice (never defects first), retaliatory (punishes defection immediately), and forgiving (returns to cooperation the moment the opponent does).

Think of TFT as a **thermostat for trust**: it starts warm and responds to the opponent's last action. The analogy describes its rule, not its stability. To test that, we need a complete payoff criterion and an explicit catalogue of alternatives.

<Callout variant="info" title="The payoffs we'll reuse">
We use the standard dilemma per round: mutual cooperation pays **3** each, mutual defection **1** each, and the tempted defector gets **5** while the exploited cooperator (the "sucker") gets **0**. So the ranking is Temptation (5) > Reward (3) > Punishment (1) > Sucker (0). Over a repeated game these per-round payoffs accumulate, weighted by how long the relationship lasts.
</Callout>

## A complete score and two catalogues

**AllD** always defects; **AllC** always cooperates. TFT cooperates first and then copies. A policy governs the entire match. A mixed policy can draw among these rules before each independent pairing; it does not change rule halfway through the match.

Two TFT policies always cooperate, so $F(T, T)=3$. AllD against TFT scores five first and one thereafter:

$$F(D, T)=(1-w)5+w\cdot1=2.$$

TFT against AllD scores zero first and one thereafter:

$$F(T, D)=(1-w)0+w\cdot1=3/4.$$

The tail weights sum to w because $\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}(1-w)w^r=w$. By contrast, AllD against AllC always scores five and AllC against AllD always scores zero. The complete account is:

### First allow only AllD and TFT

The catalogue includes these two policies and all their mixtures. Against resident AllD, TFT scores $3/4<1$; against resident TFT, AllD scores $2<3$. Both residents are strict best replies to themselves within this catalogue. Check every mixed mutant too: let q be its probability of choosing AllD and $\varepsilon$ its population share.

- Resident AllD, alternative $q<1$:

$$\Delta=(1-q)\left[\frac14-\frac54\varepsilon(1-q)\right]>0\quad\text{when }0<\varepsilon<1/5.$$

- Resident TFT, alternative $q>0$:

$$\Delta=q\left[1-\frac54\varepsilon q\right]>0\quad\text{when }0<\varepsilon<4/5.$$

The factors supply positive barriers for **every** different mixed alternative. This space has two pure ESSs. That still does not specify a trajectory between them.

### Add AllC without changing payoffs or w

TFT and AllC score three in all their mutual encounters. Therefore,

$$F(T, T)=F(C, T)=3,\qquad F(T, C)=F(C, C)=3.$$

The first tie is not rescued by the second: **TFT is not ESS in the expanded catalogue**. There is no weaker category called “almost a strict ESS” doing the work here. AllC prevents the definition from being satisfied. T/C encounters are neutral on this face of the game, but neutrality does not force AllC to accumulate or predict takeover.

AllD remains ESS against every mixture in the three-policy catalogue. For a mutant with probabilities $(d, t, c)$ of choosing AllD/TFT/AllC, define $s=t+c>0$. Its comparison with resident AllD gives

$$\Delta=\frac t4+c+\varepsilon s\left(c-\frac{5t}{4}\right)\ge\frac{s(1-5\varepsilon)}4>0\quad\text{when }0<\varepsilon<1/5.$$

The bound uses $t/4+c\ge s/4$, $c-5t/4\ge-5s/4$ and $s\le1$. It covers every mixture different from AllD within these three policies. It does not certify resistance against every conceivable history-dependent policy.

### Not every amount of AllC opens the door to AllD

Define a cooperative policy R that, before each match, chooses AllC with probability c and TFT with $1-c$. Against an unperturbed R population, AllD scores $2+3c$ while R scores three. AllD has a strict advantage in that limiting comparison if $c>1/3$; at equality the second calculation still matters.

When a share $\varepsilon$ uses AllD, the complete R-minus-AllD score difference is

$$\Delta=1-3c+\frac{\varepsilon(9c-5)}4.$$

At $c=1/3$ it equals $-\varepsilon/2$, not zero at positive frequency. And an arbitrary positive share of AllC does not guarantee an AllD advantage. These cases keep the same one-percent mutant share:

## Replicator dynamics: the engine that does the moving

Now add a **separate update model**. Under continuous finite-strategy replicator dynamics, payoff differences determine changes in population shares. This is a specified differential equation, not the definition of ESS and not an exact description of the shared lab's finite Euler steps.

> **A strategy's share grows in proportion to how far above the population average its fitness is.** Fitter-than-average expands; below-average shrinks; exactly-average holds.

For two strategies with shares $p$ and $1-p$ and fitnesses $W_A$ and $W_B$:

$$\frac{dp}{dt} = p\,(1-p)\,(W_A - W_B).$$

Read it left to right and it almost narrates itself. The $p(1-p)$ term is just "you can only change if both types are actually present" (nothing to select between if everyone is already A or already B). The $(W_A - W_B)$ term is the steering wheel: **positive** where A beats B, so A's share rises; **negative** where A loses, so it falls; **zero** where they tie, which is a rest point. It's compound interest for behaviour — winners get proportionally more of next generation's "capital."

For the binary AllD/TFT catalogue above, let $x$ be the AllD population share. Then $W_D=2-x$ and $W_T=3-9x/4$, so

$$\dot x=x(1-x)(5x/4-1).$$

The rest points are $0,4/5,1$. For $0<x<4/5$, x falls; for $4/5<x<1$, x rises. The endpoints attract from their respective sides, and the interior point separates the basins. “Closest valley” is not the rule: a start at x=3/4 is closer to one than zero, but lies below the threshold and moves toward zero in this chosen model. The boundary states themselves remain fixed.

The corresponding [replicator theorem](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14.126-game-theory-spring-2024/mit14_126_s24_lecture_slides_evolutionary_game_theory.pdf) connects ESS with **local** asymptotic stability under its stated model. We use that conditional result, not a definition equating ESS with any observed convergence. A finite animation, one initial state or a particular numerical integrator does not test every alternative or establish a global trajectory. No claim about perpetual lizard cycles follows from this equation.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The one sentence to keep">
A static invasion test and a population-update rule answer different questions. Under the specified replicator model, ESS has a local stability theorem. It does not follow that every initial population arrives at an ESS or that a settled animation has proved one.
</Callout>

## Where the model lies: the honest catalogue

You now have a powerful tool. Powerful tools earn respect precisely because their owners know where they break. Here is the unflattering, load-bearing list of what ESS does **not** tell you.

### 1. Stability is not optimality

An ESS has local resistance to rare alternatives under the specified game. **That property does not maximise group welfare.** Hawk–Dove supplies a numerical comparison.

Recall from Lesson 2, with value $V = 6$ and cost $C = 10$, the mixed ESS is $p^* = V/C = 6/10 = 0.6$ hawks. At that equilibrium, every individual — hawk or dove — earns the same expected payoff, and that payoff works out to $V(C - V) / (2C)$:

$$\frac{V(C-V)}{2C} = \frac{6 \times (10 - 6)}{2 \times 10} = \frac{6 \times 4}{20} = \frac{24}{20} = 1.2.$$

Now imagine a population of **all Doves**. They share resources peacefully, and each earns $V/2 = 6/2 = 3$. That is **more than double** the ESS payoff of 1.2. Everyone would be richer in the all-Dove world.

| World | Composition | Payoff per individual |
|---|---|---|
| All-Dove utopia | 0% hawks | 3.0 |
| The ESS | 60% hawks | 1.2 |

All-Dove fails the invasion test: a rare Hawk initially earns $V=6$ against Doves while they earn three. The mixed policy passes the all-alternative ESS test and pays 1.2. These are different criteria: resistance to an alternative and payoff across whole population arrangements. The calculation does not establish inevitable conflict, genetic optimisation or arrival from every initial state.

### 2. Not every game has an ESS — and some have several

The answer can be zero, one or several. The specified zero-sum rock–paper–scissors game has no ESS, whereas our Hawk–Dove game has a mixed ESS. The restricted AllD/TFT catalogue above has two pure ESSs. Counts must come from the actual test and strategy space, not from the name “repeated dilemma,”a beats-loop sketch or a claim that all coordination games behave alike.

### 3. The classic model assumes an infinite, well-mixed population of unrelated strangers

The tidy algebra of $E(I,I) > E(J,I)$ rides on three assumptions that real populations routinely break:

- **Infinite population.** The continuum calculation uses exact expectations at arbitrarily small positive frequencies. A finite stochastic population can lose or fix a type through random sampling even when payoff selection favours another result. Such a process must be specified; neutrality alone does not force AllC to accumulate or a mutant to take over.
- **Unrelated players.** ESS payoffs assume you meet strangers. Add **kin structure** and the effective payoffs change: helping a relative propagates shared genes, so cooperation can pay when **Hamilton's rule** $rB > C$ holds (relatedness $r$ times benefit $B$ to the recipient exceeds cost $C$ to the actor) — even where the stranger-only ESS says defect.
- **Well-mixed encounters.** ESS assumes everyone is equally likely to meet everyone. Real interactions have **spatial or network structure** — who meets whom is lumpy. On a lattice, **cooperators can cluster** and shelter each other, surviving where a well-mixed model says they'd be exploited to extinction.

Each of these is not a footnote; each rewrites the payoff matrix.

### 4. It's a static snapshot of a moving world

ESS asks whether a declared resident resists every allowed rare alternative in a fixed game. It does not directly give arrival, path or speed. Changing environments can alter payoffs, matching or the available strategies; a result proved for one fixed game then needs to be checked again.

### 5. Loose narration is the biggest practical trap

The most common abuse of this whole field is not a math error — it's **storytelling**. "That behaviour is evolutionarily stable" is trivially easy to *assert* and genuinely hard to *earn*. Just-so stories breed like rabbits: any trait can be dressed up as adaptive after the fact. The discipline that separates science from narration is mechanical — **name the strategy set, write the payoffs $E(\cdot,\cdot)$, and actually run the invasion test.** If you haven't done those three things, you're telling a campfire story, not making a claim.

### 6. The naturalistic fallacy

Finally, the ethical guardrail carried over from the cooperation course: **an ESS is a local resistance property, not a verdict about what is good, fair or right.** Stable fighting (Hawk–Dove), stable deception, and stable defection (the all-AllD world) are *all* ESSs. Being evolutionarily stable confers exactly zero moral endorsement. "It's natural" and "it's stable" are descriptions; "it's good" is a separate claim you have to argue on its own terms.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Practitioner's checklist — before you claim an ESS">
To earn the phrase "this is an ESS," specify all of it:

- **(a)** the **strategy set** — what alternatives are on the table;
- **(b)** the **payoff function** E(A,B) — the numbers, not a vibe;
- **(c)** best-reply-to-itself — check E(I,I) >= E(J,I) for every mutant J;
- **(d)** the **tie-breaker** where E(I,I) = E(J,I) — check E(I,J) > E(J,J);
- **(e)** require a positive small-frequency interval for every different allowed policy; investigate any population trajectory with a separate update model.

And keep in mind the count: **zero, one, or many** ESSs may exist. If you skipped (a)–(b), you never had a claim — just a story.
</Callout>
