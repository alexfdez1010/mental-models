Imagine a constructed contest over a resource. One policy fights; another displays and retreats from a fight. It is tempting to ask which behaviour is best. The answer can depend on the opponents: a fighter meeting mostly retreaters has a different score from a fighter meeting mostly fighters.

The question for this course is narrower: once a resident strategy is common, does it out-score every different alternative introduced at a sufficiently small positive frequency? A stable-looking ratio in an animal census would not answer that question by itself. We need a strategy set, a payoff rule and assumptions about who meets whom.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The one-sentence version">
An **evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS)** scores strictly more than every different mutant strategy when that alternative is introduced at a sufficiently small positive frequency, under the declared game and matching assumptions. It is a local resistance property; reaching it or eliminating a mutant requires an additional population model.
</Callout>

## The move that makes it work

A **Nash equilibrium** is a payoff condition: no player gains by changing strategy alone while the other strategies are fixed. Its definition does not require a player to compute or describe how play reached the equilibrium. ESS adds a different question about a symmetric population game: does the resident retain a strict payoff advantage after a small positive share of alternatives is introduced?

Maynard Smith and Price's [1973 model of animal conflict](https://doi.org/10.1038/246015a0) made population strategies central to this question. We will calculate expected payoffs under specified encounters. If we later turn a payoff advantage into a growth rule, that rule will be another assumption to examine, not something the definition supplies for free.

## Compare the encounters

Here is the **Hawk–Dove game** as a stipulated example, not a measurement of animal reproduction. The resource is worth $V=4$ payoff points; losing a fight costs $C=12$. A **Hawk** fights and a **Dove** retreats from a fight. Two Hawks each have an equal chance of winning V or paying C; two Doves share V. These assumptions give row payoffs $\begin{pmatrix}-4&4\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$.

Compare the two policies against **the same opponent distribution**. Against a Hawk, Hawk minus Dove is −4−0=−4 points. Against a Dove it is 4−2=2 points. Weight those two differences by the opponent shares. The calculator starts with ninety percent Hawk opponents; it is an expected-payoff calculation, not a population-update simulation.

## Why this earns a place in the latticework

The ESS looks like a narrow piece of theoretical biology. It is actually one of the most portable tools you can carry, for three reasons.

- **It makes a resistance claim testable.** Declare the alternatives, their payoffs and the encounter rule. The test then applies even without deliberating agents; applying it to a market or social norm requires defending those ingredients.
- **It allows mixed answers.** A mixed resident may resist every alternative, but equal observed frequencies or equal-payoff curves alone do not establish that result.
- **It separates resistance from welfare.** An ESS can pay less than another population arrangement. Neither a desirable outcome nor an undesirable one is guaranteed to be reached.

## The map of the course

Five teaching lessons build the model from the definition up to its limits, then one exam locks it in:

1. **The Invasion Test** — build the opponent population, prove resistance to every different mixed alternative at small positive frequency, and distinguish a local barrier from a global prediction.
2. **Hawk–Dove: The Flagship Game** — the full worked model. Why pure Hawk is invadable whenever C > V, why the ESS is the *mixed* proportion V/C, and the real numbers behind the fighting-vs-displaying trade-off.
3. **ESS and Nash: The Extra Bar** — ESS implies symmetric Nash; a tie needs another comparison. Individual randomisation and populations of pure types can share aggregate frequencies without having identical individual or inheritance mechanisms.
4. **Frequency-Dependent Selection** — payoffs can depend on population composition. The course discusses sex ratios, scale-eating fish and rock–paper–scissors analogies; frequency dependence alone does not establish ESS or a particular trajectory.
5. **Cooperation, Waste, and Where the Model Lies** — declare how repeated payoffs are scored and which policies are allowed. Adding Always Cooperate (AllC) changes the verdict for reciprocal Tit-for-Tat (TFT); a separate replicator model illustrates local dynamics and the limits of the static test.

Then a **Final Exam** — graded, one question at a time, one-way: once you answer, it locks. No back button, no retries, 70% to pass.

### How to use this course

One rule does most of the work: **guess before you peek.** Commit to an answer on every exercise *before* you reveal the explanation — the small sting of being wrong is what welds the idea into memory. Return to the fixed matrix and reconstruct a weighted payoff without looking at the answer. Then ask which assumptions and which alternatives your calculation actually covers.
