An ESS is a local payoff-resistance property in a declared game: every different alternative scores strictly less at sufficiently small positive mutant share. The course distinguishes that test from arrival or fixation, works through Hawk–Dove and Nash, and makes the strategy catalogue explicit when comparing repeated-game policies. This final exam sweeps all five lessons at once. It is one-way: answer, and you have committed.

In the two-action games, p and q are individual probabilities of A; epsilon is the mutant population share. The opponent distribution is z=(1−epsilon)p+epsilon q. Repeated-game stems distinguish the binary AllD/TFT catalogue from the ternary catalogue adding AllC; their mixtures are allowed. F is a normalized expected score, not the arithmetic average of a realised match.

## Key Takeaways

<Callout variant="success" title="What an ESS really claims">
An evolutionarily stable strategy is the move that, once common, no rare mutant can profitably invade — a claim about **resistance**, not virtue. The whole test lives in one inequality: $E(I,I) > E(J,I)$, or a tie there settled by $E(I,J) > E(J,J)$. In Hawk–Dove that logic gives pure Hawk when $V > C$ and the mixed $p^* = V/C$ when $C > V$ — a mix everyone would gladly trade away, since it pays $V(C-V)/(2C)$ against all-Dove's $V/2$. ESS refines Nash: every ESS is Nash, every strict Nash is an ESS, but weak equilibria and mixed strategies fall between. Frequency dependence alone establishes neither ESS nor a trajectory; even the zero-sum rock–paper–scissors example requires specified payoffs and dynamics. And the honest fine print matters most: stability is not optimality, some games have no ESS while others have several, the model assumes an infinite well-mixed population of strangers, and "it evolved" never means "it is good." Apply the invasion test only after stating the strategy space, payoff rule and encounter assumptions; examine population dynamics separately.
</Callout>
