So far you have two tools. The **invasion test** tells you when a strategy $I$ is an **ESS**: for every mutant $J$, either $E(I,I) > E(J,I)$, or the payoffs tie against the resident ($E(I,I) = E(J,I)$) *and* $I$ wins the tie-breaker $E(I,J) > E(J,J)$. And the **Hawk–Dove** game handed you a mixed ESS at $p^* = V/C$. This lesson connects that machinery to the most famous idea in game theory — the **Nash equilibrium** — and shows exactly where the two concepts agree, where they part ways, and why the difference is not a technicality but the whole point.

The headline: **an ESS meets the symmetric Nash condition and also passes the required tie-breaker.** Both are payoff conditions. A tie against unperturbed residents does not establish neutrality once mutants encounter one another.

## First, what a symmetric Nash equilibrium actually says

Before we can say "ESS refines Nash," pin down the Nash side in the exact language we've been using. In a **symmetric one-population game**, a strategy $I$ is a **Nash equilibrium (NE)** if it is a **best reply to itself**:

$$E(I,I) \ge E(J,I) \quad \text{for all } J.$$

Read it aloud: against an all-I opponent population, no alternative J earns strictly more than I. This is a static payoff comparison. It neither requires a player to compute the best reply nor specifies a learning or reproduction process. Nash describes the absence of a profitable unilateral deviation at that profile, not a route to it.

Notice the inequality is **weak** ($\ge$, not $>$). That single choice of symbol is where all the drama lives. A **strict** Nash equilibrium uses $>$: every deviation does *strictly worse*. A **weak** Nash equilibrium allows ties: some deviation does *exactly as well*. Hold that distinction — it returns with a vengeance.

<Callout variant="info" title="Best reply to itself">
A symmetric NE is a strategy that is among the best replies to itself. If another strategy scored strictly more against it, the Nash condition would fail. That payoff comparison does not establish what any player or population will actually do next.
</Callout>

## ESS ⊆ Nash: every stable strategy is an equilibrium

Now the first big claim, and it's cleaner than you'd expect: **every ESS is a Nash equilibrium.** Set-wise, $\text{ESS} \subseteq \text{NE}$.

Why must this be true? Look at the two ways a strategy $I$ can pass the invasion test against a mutant $J$:

1. **Condition 1 fires:** $E(I,I) > E(J,I)$. That already gives you $E(I,I) \ge E(J,I)$ — with room to spare.
2. **Condition 1 ties, Condition 2 fires:** here $E(I,I) = E(J,I)$, which *also* gives you $E(I,I) \ge E(J,I)$ — with equality.

Either branch delivers $E(I,I) \ge E(J,I)$. Since $I$ has to beat *every* mutant $J$ to be an ESS, it satisfies $E(I,I) \ge E(J,I)$ for **all** $J$ — and that is precisely the Nash condition. So an ESS is automatically a best reply to itself.

If I is not a best reply to itself, there is a J with $E(J, I)>E(I, I)$. Expected payoffs vary continuously with the mutant share, so J retains a payoff advantage for sufficiently small positive shares. That rules out ESS. It does not establish fixation or a particular population path.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The logic in one line">
Not a best reply to itself → an alternative has a payoff advantage at sufficiently small positive frequency → not ESS. Therefore ESS implies symmetric Nash.
</Callout>

## A Nash equilibrium that is NOT an ESS

The converse can fail. A symmetric Nash strategy may tie against the unperturbed resident and then lose the positive-frequency comparison. The extra ESS condition checks those encounters; it does not require a story about rationality or neutral drift.

Let's build the smallest possible counterexample and grind through the arithmetic. Two strategies, $A$ and $B$. Payoffs to the **row** player:

| | vs A | vs B |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | 1 | 1 |
| **B** | 1 | 2 |

Read the table the same way every time: the row is *you*, the column is your opponent, the cell is *your* payoff. So $E(A,A)=1$, $E(A,B)=1$, $E(B,A)=1$, $E(B,B)=2$.

**Step 1 — Is $A$ a Nash equilibrium?** Compare $A$ against the other pure alternative, $B$, when the resident is $A$:

- $E(A,A) = 1$
- $E(B,A) = 1$

They **tie**. No strategy earns strictly more than $A$ against an $A$-crowd, so $A$ is a best reply to itself. **$A$ is a (weak) Nash equilibrium.** A rational player in an all-$A$ world has no strict reason to switch — deviating to $B$ pays exactly the same 1.

**Step 2 — Is $A$ an ESS?** Condition 1 already failed (it's a tie, not a strict win), so everything rides on **Condition 2**: we need $E(A,B) > E(B,B)$.

- $E(A,B) = 1$
- $E(B,B) = 2$
- Is $1 > 2$? **No.** Condition 2 **fails.**

So **$A$ is NOT an ESS**, even though it is a genuine Nash equilibrium.

Against a fixed all-A opponent, any mixture of A and B averages one and one, so it also scores one. This is linearity in the individual policy with the opponent held fixed. In the ESS comparison the opponent mixture also changes with q.

Introduce a fraction $\varepsilon>0$ of pure-B mutants. Every A earns $W_A=1$. A B earns $W_B=(1-\varepsilon)1+\varepsilon2=1+\varepsilon$. Thus B already has a strict payoff advantage at every positive share; it does not first need to drift until it is “common enough.” At $\varepsilon=1/100$, the scores are 1 and 1.01. For an arbitrary mutant choosing A with probability $q<1$, the complete score is $W_J=1+\varepsilon(1-q)^2>1=W_A$. This covers every different mixed alternative; its quadratic dependence on q includes the changing opponent population. Turning the advantage into growth or fixation would require another model.

## Strict Nash ⇒ ESS: the shortcut, and where the ties live

We've seen a weak Nash equilibrium fail to be an ESS. The mirror-image fact is a genuinely useful shortcut: **every strict Nash equilibrium is an ESS.**

Suppose $I$ is a **strict** Nash equilibrium — meaning $E(I,I) > E(J,I)$ for *every* $J \ne I$. But look: that inequality **is** ESS Condition 1. It holds for all mutants, so $I$ passes the invasion test outright — Condition 2 never even has to be consulted. **Strict NE → Condition 1 for all $J$ → ESS.** Done.

So where do the *interesting* ESSs live — the ones that need Condition 2 at all? Exactly where strategies **tie** against the resident. If $E(I,I) = E(J,I)$ for some mutant $J$, then $I$ is not a strict best reply to itself, so the strict-NE shortcut doesn't apply, and stability has to be rescued by the tie-breaker $E(I,J) > E(J,J)$. In those cases **Condition 2 is doing all the stability work.**

And the headline example of "strategies that tie against the resident" is the **mixed ESS**. Here's the key structural fact, worth carving into stone:

<Callout variant="info" title="Why a mixed ESS is never a strict Nash equilibrium">At a Nash equilibrium that mixes at least two actions, every pure action in its support earns the same payoff against the equilibrium. No action outside the support may earn more. Support equality is necessary, not sufficient on its own. Each supported pure action ties the genuinely mixed resident, so the profile is not strict Nash. A mixed ESS must also pass the tie-breaker against every different alternative.</Callout>

Bring back **Hawk–Dove**. Its mixed ESS $p^* = V/C$ — play Hawk with probability $V/C$ — is a **non-strict, mixed Nash equilibrium**. At that mix, pure Hawk and pure Dove earn *exactly the same* expected payoff against the equilibrium population (that's why nobody is tempted to become all-Hawk or all-Dove). Because those payoffs tie, $p^*$ flunks the strict test; it qualifies as an ESS **only through Condition 2**, which checks that the resident mix does better against a rare mutant than the mutant does against itself. The tie-breaker isn't decoration in Hawk–Dove — it's the entire reason the mix is stable.

## The two faces of a mixed strategy

An aggregate fraction of Hawk actions can arise in two different ways. The distinction matters because the action distribution an opponent meets does not identify an individual policy or an inheritance mechanism.

**Face 1 — the randomising individual.** Every animal is a single **mixed player**: an identical stochastic gambler who, in each contest, plays Hawk with probability $p^* = V/C$ and Dove otherwise. Everyone is the same; each individual carries a coin and flips it. Variation lives *within* each animal, across its many encounters.

**Face 2 — the stable polymorphism.** Nobody randomises at all. The population is **split into pure types**: a fraction $p^*$ are lifelong pure Hawks, the remaining $1 - p^*$ are lifelong pure Doves. Each individual is deterministic; the *population* is the mixture. Variation lives *between* animals, not within them.

Under uniform independent matching, these pictures can give a random opponent the same action distribution and the same population mean payoff. Individual payoffs and responses to a new strategy need not coincide, however: a pure specialist is not the same policy as a randomising resident. Inheritance and dynamics also need separate assumptions. Observing the aggregate fraction alone does not establish either mechanism or ESS.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Same frequencies, different biology">
Ask whether variation occurs within individuals across encounters or between different individuals. Shared aggregate frequencies do not answer that question, establish genetic inheritance or certify the same invasion dynamics.
</Callout>

To distinguish the two pictures, observe repeated actions by identified individuals and investigate how policies are transmitted. A population snapshot can show a ratio; it cannot tell you that individuals randomise, that their types are genetic, or that the ratio is evolutionarily stable.

<details>
<summary>Check the claim “it is Nash, so it is stable”</summary>

Does the strategy strictly beat every different alternative against itself? If so, strict Nash implies ESS. For each tied alternative, instead check $E(I, J)>E(J, J)$. In the counterexample, $W_A=1$ and $W_B=1+\varepsilon$ at positive pure-B share. No neutral-drift story is required.

</details>

<Callout variant="success" title="When to use this">
When someone infers evolutionary stability from Nash, ask for the declared alternative strategies and every required tie-breaker. A weak Nash may pass or fail ESS. When someone identifies an individual mechanism from a mixed population frequency, ask what repeated-individual observations or transmission evidence supports it.
</Callout>

<Callout variant="info" title="One loose thread for later">The standard zero-sum **rock–paper–scissors** game has a uniform mixed Nash that is not ESS. This is a statement about specified payoffs. A trajectory additionally requires an update rule and an initial state: even under its usual replicator model, the exact centre is stationary. No perpetual cycle of a real animal population follows from the label alone.</Callout>
