A newcomer earns exactly what the residents earn when it meets a resident. Is the population safe? That sentence leaves out the encounters that decide the answer: newcomers can also meet newcomers.

An **evolutionarily stable strategy**, or **ESS**, passes a particular resistance test. Every different strategy must score strictly less than the resident when introduced at a sufficiently small positive frequency. This is a statement about a declared game and its alternatives. It does not, by itself, tell a story about how a population arrives there.

## Build the population before comparing scores

Think of a resistance test on a sample: specify what you put into it before reading the result. Here the sample is a population, and the reading is an expected payoff. The analogy stops there. A payoff calculation does not specify births, learning, random deaths or a future trajectory.

We use a **symmetric two-player game**. Both players have actions A and B and the same payoff rule: your score depends on your action and your opponent's action. A row-payoff matrix records your score, not the combined score of both players. All new numbers in this lesson are **stipulated payoff points per encounter**, not measured offspring counts.

An individual can use a **mixed strategy**: choose A with a fixed probability and B otherwise. Write:

- $p$: probability that a **resident individual** chooses A;
- $q$: probability that a **mutant individual** chooses A;
- $\varepsilon$: **share of individuals** carrying the mutant strategy.

A pure A strategy has probability one; pure B has probability zero. A genuine alternative has $q\ne p$. The model assumes a continuum population and **uniform, independent matching**. Each encounter draws an opponent from the current population. It does not condition on family, location or a finite population's sampling history.

The probability that this opponent chooses A is therefore

$$z=(1-\varepsilon)p+\varepsilon q.$$

For example, residents always choose A ($p=1$), mutants always choose B ($q=0$), and mutants are one tenth of the population ($\varepsilon=1/10$). Then $z=(9/10)\times1+(1/10)\times0=9/10$. A mutant meets a resident with probability nine tenths and another mutant with probability one tenth. Calling it “rare” does not erase that second possibility.

For row payoffs $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$, an individual choosing A with probability $x$ against opponent probability $z$ earns

$$U(x, z)=xz\, a+x(1-z)b+(1-x)z\, c+(1-x)(1-z)d.$$

The four terms are the four encounters, each multiplied by its probability. Compare **the same opponent population** for both individuals:

$$W_I=U(p, z),\qquad W_J=U(q, z),\qquad \Delta=W_I-W_J.$$

A common mistake gives the resident an all-resident opponent and the mutant a mixed opponent. That is two different games disguised as one comparison.

### When to use this setup

Use it when actions and payoffs can be specified and the matching assumptions are defensible. A census showing a stable-looking ratio does not supply these ingredients or establish an ESS. Likewise, identical aggregate action frequencies need not mean identical individual payoffs or inheritance mechanisms.

A wins the small-perturbation comparison and loses the half-mutant comparison. There is no contradiction: ESS is **local resistance to rare alternatives**, not resistance to every possible replacement of the population.

But checking pure B alone has not proved ESS. What if the mutant randomises? Against any $q<1$, the full calculation gives

$$\Delta=(1-q)\big[1-3\varepsilon(1-q)\big].$$

For every real $q$ in $[0,1)$, the first factor is positive. If $0<\varepsilon<1/3$, the bracket is also positive because $1-q\le1$. Thus A beats **every different mixed policy** throughout this positive interval. That factorisation earns the ESS claim. A list of a few successful mutant tests would not.

Resident B also passes: for every alternative $q>0$,

$$\Delta=q(2-3\varepsilon q)>0\quad\text{when }0<\varepsilon<2/3.$$

This one game therefore has two pure ESSs. Neither claim says which one a population will reach.

### What the quantifiers require

Formally, **for every** alternative $q\ne p$, **there exists** a positive barrier $\bar\varepsilon_q$ such that $\Delta>0$ for **every** $0<\varepsilon<\bar\varepsilon_q$. The barrier may depend on the mutant. Our example supplies a single sufficient barrier that works for all its alternatives.

The comparison uses positive $\varepsilon$. Merely evaluating $\varepsilon=0$, or saying “take the limit,” can hide the sign that decides a tie.

For an arbitrary mutant $q<1$, not just pure B, the exact difference is

$$\Delta=(2-k)\varepsilon(1-q)^2.$$

- **k = 0:** every different mutant scores less at every positive frequency. A is an ESS despite the initial tie.
- **k = 2:** every entry of the matrix is two. All policies tie against all populations. Every policy is a symmetric Nash equilibrium, but none is ESS. There is no strictly favoured mutant here.
- **k = 3:** every different mutant scores more at every positive frequency. It does not need to remain neutral until it becomes “common enough.” Its advantage can be tiny, but the sign is already negative for the resident.

**Neutrality** means equal scores in the specified comparison. A tie against all-A opponents is not the same as a tie once mutants are part of the population. **Drift** is random change arising from a specified finite-population sampling process; it is not a consequence supplied by this continuum payoff calculation. Even genuine neutrality does not guarantee accumulation or takeover.

### When the tie matters

Check it whenever an alternative matches the incumbent against incumbent opponents. The plausible shortcut “selection has nothing to act on” throws away exactly the encounters the tie-breaker examines.

## Derive the two conditions

Let $E(I, J)$ mean the expected score of strategy I against strategy J. It already averages over any random actions inside either strategy. With mutant share $\varepsilon$, bilinearity of expected payoffs gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta={}&(1-\varepsilon)\big[E(I, I)-E(J, I)\big]\\
&+\varepsilon\big[E(I, J)-E(J, J)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

Call the two brackets $\alpha$ and $\beta$. These are **payoff differences**, not probabilities.

If $\alpha>0$, then $(1-\varepsilon)\alpha+\varepsilon\beta$ stays positive for sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$. Even a negative $\beta$ cannot overturn a strict initial advantage immediately. If $\alpha=0$, the difference is exactly $\varepsilon\beta$, so it is strictly positive precisely when $\beta>0$. If $\alpha<0$, sufficiently rare mutants already have the payoff advantage.

In the first A/B example, $\alpha=4-3=1$ and $\beta=0-2=-2$. At $\varepsilon=1/10$, the formula gives $(9/10)\times1+(1/10)\times(-2)=7/10$, reproducing the table. The second bracket can be negative while a strict first advantage still protects a small interval.

Therefore I is ESS exactly when, **for every alternative J**, either

$$E(I, I)>E(J, I),$$

or

$$E(I, I)=E(J, I)\quad\text{and}\quad E(I, J)>E(J, J).$$

This is why “best reply to itself” is necessary. A **symmetric Nash equilibrium** has $E(I, I)\ge E(J, I)$ for every J. Nash permits a tie; ESS also checks what that tied alternative does in encounters involving itself. A **strict** symmetric Nash equilibrium passes the first condition for every different alternative, so it is ESS. Neither definition requires a player to calculate, and neither is an update algorithm.

Those examples suggest resistance, but the proof must cover every $q\ne1/2$. Substitution gives

$$\boxed{\Delta=4\varepsilon(q-1/2)^2>0.}$$

The square is positive for every different real q, and so is $\varepsilon$. Thus the mixed resident is ESS. At $q=p$ the difference is zero because there is **no different strategy to test**.

## An intersection is a candidate, not a certificate

For a general two-action matrix, let $k=a-b-c+d$. The difference between choosing A and B against opponent probability z is $kz+b-d$. An **interior** resident p that makes both actions tie satisfies $kp+b-d=0$. In that case,

$$\Delta=-k\varepsilon(q-p)^2.$$

This identity explains the contrasting mixtures. Our first matrix has $k=3$ and an interior symmetric Nash strategy $p=2/3$: both actions score $8/3$ against it. But its gap is **negative**, $-3\varepsilon(q-2/3)^2$, for every different mutant. The transfer matrix has $k=-4$, so its gap is positive. The lines intersect in both games; their geometry does not make both intersections ESS.

In **Hawk–Dove**, with $C>V>0$, the coefficient is $k=-C/2$. At $p=V/C$ the gap is $C\varepsilon(q-p)^2/2>0$ for every alternative. That supplies the positive-frequency justification behind the familiar mixed ESS; equal payoffs alone did not supply it.

The one-shot prisoner's dilemma offers the strict counterpart. With cooperation/defection row payoffs $\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\5&1\end{pmatrix}$, resident defection has $p=0$. Any different mutant cooperates with $q>0$, giving $\Delta=q(1+\varepsilon q)>0$. The claim covers all mixed alternatives in that two-action game, not every policy in a repeated game.

### When to use the conclusion

Use an ESS claim only with its strategy space, payoff rule and matching assumptions attached. To predict motion, add and justify an update model. To predict finite-population loss or fixation, add a stochastic population model. A deterministic payoff advantage is neither a guarantee about one individual's realised success nor a genetic diagnosis.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Keep the test and the trajectory separate">
For specified finite-strategy replicator dynamics, ESS has a mathematical connection to **local** asymptotic stability. That is a theorem under stated dynamics, not the definition of ESS or a guarantee of global arrival. A finite animation that settles does not verify the universal invasion test.
</Callout>
