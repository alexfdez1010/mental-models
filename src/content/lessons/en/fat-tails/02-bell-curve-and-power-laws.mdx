An instrument records a very large load. One analyst says, “That was impossible.” Another says, “Then it must be a power law.” Both have skipped a step: specify a model, calculate what it predicts, and check what the observation can actually establish.

This lesson compares mathematical models with given parameters. It does not estimate a distribution for a real instrument, market or population. Keep that distinction in view: a correct calculation inside a model is still conditional on choosing an adequate model.

## A normal model specifies probabilities, not a boundary

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="A specified normal model gives a very small chance to an event that occurs. What follows by logic alone?"
  options={[{"text":"Its parameters must have been estimated without error."},{"text":"The observation establishes a Pareto law for this process."},{"text":"The event was possible under the stated normal model.","correct":true},{"text":"Every future observation will exceed the same threshold."}]}
  explanation="Positive probability permits an event without making it typical. One observation alone does not identify the model or eliminate other explanations."
  pretest={true}
/>

The **normal distribution**, also called the **Gaussian distribution**, is a continuous probability model with mean $\mu$ and positive standard deviation $\sigma$. Its density is

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).$$

A **density** is not the probability of an exact value: probabilities are areas over intervals. Standardize a value with $Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma$. Under the specified model, $Z$ has the standard normal distribution. A threshold three standard deviations above the mean is $Z>3$; an equally extreme value on either side is $|Z|>3$.

Those are different events. Symmetry makes the two-sided probability twice the upper-tail probability for a positive threshold. For example, $P(Z>3)\approx0.001349898$ whereas $P(|Z|>3)\approx0.002699796$. The second event also includes the lower tail, $Z<-3$.

### When to use this calculation

Use it when a normal model with these parameters is the hypothesis you want to examine. State which tail you counted. Do not turn one realization into a theorem about the population that generated it.

## A bell-shaped limit concerns standardized averages

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="Collecting many independent observations makes which object approximately normal under the stated central-limit assumptions?"
  options={[{"text":"The distribution of the largest raw observation."},{"text":"The population distribution of each original observation."},{"text":"The next observed value, regardless of its original law."},{"text":"The distribution of the standardized sample mean.","correct":true}]}
  explanation="The theorem concerns a centered and scaled mean across repeated samples. It does not change the distribution that generates each raw observation."
  pretest={true}
/>

One version of the **central limit theorem** concerns independent, identically distributed observations with finite mean and finite positive variance. For the sample mean $\bar X_n$, it states that

$$\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}$$

approaches a standard normal distribution as the sample size grows. The original observations need not be normal. Their distribution does not become normal because more of them were recorded, and a finite sample size does not make the approximation exact.

Consider independent fair zero-or-one draws. Each has mean $1/2$ and standard deviation $1/2$. The **standard error** is the standard deviation of their sample mean across repeated samples. Here it is $1/(2\sqrt n)$:

| Sample size | Standard error in proportion units | Same quantity in percentage points |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1/2 | 50 |
| 4 | 1/4 | 25 |
| 16 | 1/8 | 12.5 |

Quadrupling independent sample size halves this standard error. It does not promise that the next realized mean will be closer to the population mean. At four draws, the mean still has only five possible values; it is not an exactly continuous bell curve.

Compare that with making four or sixteen perfect copies of **one** draw. Their average remains the original zero-or-one value, so its standard deviation stays at fifty percentage points. More rows have not supplied more independent information.

### When this limit helps

It can support an approximation for averages when its assumptions and approximation error are defensible. It cannot justify a normal model for the largest raw observation, remove dependence, or supply finite variance when the population lacks it.

## Define a Pareto tail before comparing exponents

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="Two Pareto models share their cutoff. At the same threshold above it, which survival exponent gives more probability above the threshold?"
  options={[{"text":"The smaller exponent.","correct":true},{"text":"The larger exponent."},{"text":"Either exponent gives exactly the same probability."},{"text":"The answer depends only on how many draws were recorded."}]}
  explanation="At a common cutoff and an above-cutoff threshold, the ratio raised to the exponent lies between zero and one. A smaller positive exponent therefore leaves a larger survival probability."
  pretest={true}
/>

A continuous **Pareto model** with lower cutoff $x_m>0$ and survival exponent $\alpha>0$ specifies

$$P(X>x)=\left(\frac{x_m}{x}\right)^\alpha,\qquad x\ge x_m.$$

The **survival probability** counts outcomes above a threshold. Below the cutoff, every outcome is at least $x_m$, so survival is one. For this continuous model, changing $>$ to $\ge$ at one point does not alter its probability. That equality need not hold in a discrete model with point masses.

Work a complete comparison with the same cutoff, $x_m=1$, and the same threshold, $x=4$. Exponent two gives $(1/4)^2=1/16$; exponent four gives $(1/4)^4=1/256$. The first probability is sixteen times the second. A smaller survival exponent gives a larger upper-tail probability when the cutoff is held fixed and the threshold is above it.

These ratios come from the declared models, not from observed ticket frequencies. Both models have unbounded support. Even exponent four remains a power-law tail; it does not turn into a normal distribution.

Different sources sometimes use $\alpha$ for the exponent of the **density** instead. Here differentiating the distribution gives

$$f(x)=\frac{\alpha x_m^\alpha}{x^{\alpha+1}}.$$

Thus survival exponent two corresponds to density exponent three. Read the definition beside a reported exponent before comparing it with this lesson.

### When this comparison is valid

It compares specified Pareto models at a common cutoff and threshold. Different cutoffs can change the ordering at a chosen numerical threshold. Neither a single large observation nor an economic label establishes these parameters or the Pareto family.

## Heavy tails do not always remove the mean

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="The given continuous Pareto model has survival exponent one point two. Which description is correct?"
  options={[{"text":"Both population moments are finite because every sample is finite."},{"text":"Its population mean is finite but its variance is infinite.","correct":true},{"text":"Its mean is infinite because its variance is infinite."},{"text":"Its sample mean cannot be calculated until the population mean exists."}]}
  explanation="The survival exponent is above one but not above two. A finite sample mean and a population expectation are different objects."
  pretest={true}
/>

For the Pareto convention just defined, the mean is finite when $\alpha>1$:

$$E[X]=\frac{\alpha x_m}{\alpha-1}.$$

The variance is finite only when $\alpha>2$, in which case

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{\alpha x_m^2}{(\alpha-1)^2(\alpha-2)}.$$

For $0<\alpha\le1$, the mean is infinite, so the ordinary variance about a finite population mean is undefined. For $1<\alpha\le2$, the mean is finite but the variance is infinite. These thresholds describe the ideal model with an unlimited upper tail. A specified finite upper cutoff changes its moments.

| Survival exponent, cutoff one | Mean | Variance |
|---|---:|---|
| 0.8 | Infinite | Not finite |
| 1.2 | 6 | Infinite |
| 2 | 2 | Infinite |
| 4 | 4/3 | 2/9 |

For exponent $1.2=6/5$, the mean calculation is $(6/5)/(1/5)=6$. Independent draws from this model have a finite absolute mean, so the law of large numbers applies to their sample mean even though the variance is infinite. A slowly settling estimate in a short record is not a proof that the population mean does not exist.

Finite does not mean evenly contributed. In that same model, the probability above thirty-two is $32^{-6/5}=1/64$. Yet this region contributes half the finite mean: its expected contribution is three of the total six load units. The share of the mean contributed above a threshold $t\ge x_m$ is $(x_m/t)^{\alpha-1}$ when $\alpha>1$. This is a ratio of expectations, not a promise about the largest draw in one finite sample.

### When moments matter

Check the moment your proposed method actually requires. A finite mean can support a law-of-large-numbers claim under independence while failing the finite-variance assumption of the central limit theorem stated above. “Fat-tailed” alone does not settle either question.

## Separate scale invariance from a universal eighty-twenty split

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="One dataset has an eighty-twenty concentration summary. Does that alone determine the top four percent’s contribution?"
  options={[{"text":"Yes: every concentration summary must repeat inside itself."},{"text":"Yes: every right-skewed finite dataset has the same ranking."},{"text":"No: the split inside the leading group is additional information.","correct":true},{"text":"No: a share of effects cannot be calculated for any finite dataset."}]}
  explanation="The nesting calculation needs the same split inside the first group. An aggregate summary does not specify that internal allocation."
  pretest={true}
/>

In the specified Pareto model, for a multiplier $c\ge1$ and threshold $t\ge x_m$,

$$P(X>ct\mid X>t)=\frac{P(X>ct)}{P(X>t)}=c^{-\alpha}.$$

With cutoff one, exponent two and $t=2$, the unconditional probability above four is $1/16$. Conditioning on already exceeding two changes the denominator to $1/4$, so the conditional probability is $(1/16)/(1/4)=1/4$. That conditional probability is the same as the original probability above twice the cutoff. This is the model's scale relationship, not a claim that finite samples reproduce identical shapes at every zoom.

Concentration introduces another quantity: the **share of the total model mean** contributed by the largest fraction $p$ of observations. When the Pareto mean is finite, that share is $p^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha}$. For exponent two, the top quarter contributes one half of the mean total; the top sixteenth contributes one quarter. The model has a power-law tail without an eighty-twenty split.

If a constructed ranking stipulates that the top twenty percent contribute eighty percent, **and the same split repeats inside that group**, then the top four percent contribute sixty-four percent: $0.2\times0.2=0.04$ and $0.8\times0.8=0.64$. The second stipulation is doing work. One eighty-twenty summary in a finite dataset does not prove it.

### When to use the scaling relationship

Use a specified tail model to calculate conditional exceedances or concentration, keeping their denominators distinct. Do not convert an empirical concentration summary into a guaranteed recursion, or a positive probability into an appointment on a calendar.

## Transfer the calculation, then audit the model choice

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="At the common threshold of six, compare the given exponent-two models with cutoffs one and three. Which has the greater survival probability?"
  options={[{"text":"The model with cutoff one, because its support starts earlier."},{"text":"The model with cutoff three, because the threshold is a smaller multiple of its cutoff.","correct":true},{"text":"They must agree because their exponents are equal."},{"text":"Neither probability exists above a finite threshold."}]}
  explanation="With the exponent fixed, the ratio of cutoff to threshold also matters. The calculations give one thirty-sixth versus one quarter."
  pretest={true}
/>

An independent constructed case has cutoff three load units and survival exponent two. Its upper-tail probabilities are

$$P(X>6)=(3/6)^2=1/4,\qquad P(X>12)=(3/12)^2=1/16.$$

Given that a load already exceeds six, its probability of exceeding twelve is $(1/16)/(1/4)=1/4$. Copying the old cutoff one would instead give $P(X>6)=1/36$, a different calculation for a different model.

For independent draws with a fixed exceedance probability $p>0$, the chance of at least one exceedance in $n$ trials is $1-(1-p)^n$. With $p=1/4$ and four draws, this is $1-(3/4)^4=175/256$, about sixty-eight percent. It is not certainty within those four trials. Independence and an unchanged probability are additional assumptions; a marginal probability alone does not specify a sequence of events.

The harder question is whether the model describes a real process. A fitted line on logarithmic axes, a large mean-to-median gap, or one extreme observation does not by itself establish a power law. A lognormal or a truncated model can offer competing explanations. A serious empirical analysis must specify the fitted range, estimate parameters appropriately, check fit, compare alternatives and inspect how observations were obtained. This workshop has done none of those empirical steps for a real dataset.

Even a model not rejected by a chosen fit test has not been proved uniquely true. Use the diagnosis to decide what evidence or sensitivity analysis is needed, rather than replacing one automatic default with another.

### When to repeat the audit

Repeat it after a change in scale, cutoff, observation window, selection, dependence or possible physical bound. The operational question is which conclusions survive those changes, given a stated decision and its consequences. A tail label supplies no universal policy by itself.

### Sources and scope

The [NIST normal-distribution reference](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3661.htm) specifies the normal model; the central-limit statement here explicitly adds the independent, identical-distribution and finite-variance assumptions used in this lesson. [SciPy's Pareto definition](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.pareto.html) gives the continuous density and cutoff convention. The moment, conditional-probability and constructed finite calculations above can be reproduced from those definitions.

Clauset, Shalizi and Newman's [Power-law distributions in empirical data](https://arxiv.org/abs/0706.1062) explains why visual fitting alone cannot establish a power law and why alternative models matter. Selected introduction, definitions and model-checking passages informed this lesson; no empirical fit or statistical test from that paper has been executed here.
