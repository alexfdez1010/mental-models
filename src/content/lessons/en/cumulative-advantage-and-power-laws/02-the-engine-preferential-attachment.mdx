“More begets more” leaves important details unwritten. More of what? Does the current state change a probability, an absolute gain or a percentage growth rate? How long does the process run? Different answers describe different models.

This lesson makes one rule precise. It also tests a tempting shortcut: multiplying two quantities by the same factor does not enlarge their ratio. The later workshop will extend the finite random rule to three draws and audit its complete distribution.

## Make the update rule explicit

**Reinforcement** means that the current outcome feeds back into the rule for future gains. In a **preferential allocation rule**, a larger current count can receive a larger chance of the next unit. A network model must additionally specify which nodes enter and where new links may attach. A social claim about recognition needs its own evidence. Sharing a verbal pattern does not make these complete models identical.

The **Matthew effect** concerns scientific recognition. In his [1968 account](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.159.3810.56), Robert K. Merton discussed disproportionate credit for eminent scientists in collaborations with less-known colleagues or in independent multiple discoveries. That account describes a social pattern; its prevalence and causes in a particular setting require evidence from that setting.

Our constructed urn starts with **one red ball and one blue ball**. At each draw:

1. Select one of the currently present balls uniformly at random.
2. Record its colour.
3. Return that ball and add **one new ball of the same colour**.

Returning matters: the selected ball is not removed. Adding matters: the total grows from two balls to three, then four. Colours do not enter or disappear. A ball is not a newly arriving network node.

Write R for a red observation and B for a blue observation. After the first R the urn holds 2R/1B, so the next red chance is 2/3. After the first B it holds 1R/2B, so the next red chance is 1/3. Both are conditional probabilities: each refers to a specified history.

### When to specify a rule

Specify it before using a simulation to explain a distribution. A plot can show what one program did. Whether it represents the intended process depends on its state, update equation, randomness and starting conditions.

## Calculate the paths before reading a winner

  options={[{"text":"Yes, because listing four paths gives each one a quarter of the probability"},{"text":"No; multiply each branch’s conditional probability before comparing paths","correct":true},{"text":"No; only the path that occurs has any probability"}]}
  explanation={"A catalogue of possibilities does not by itself assign their probabilities. The rule does that."}

For RR, multiply the chance of first R by the chance of second R after that history:

$$P(RR)=\frac12\times\frac23=\frac13.$$

For RB, the first chance is still 1/2, but only one of the next three balls is blue:

$$P(RB)=\frac12\times\frac13=\frac16.$$

The other two paths follow by exchanging the colour names.

The probabilities sum to 1. A first red lead does not even guarantee that the next observation is red: RB has probability 1/6. Nor do two red observations produce an all-red urn; the initial blue ball remains.

### When the first gain can matter

It matters here because it changes the composition used at the next draw. To explain another system, verify its corresponding state-to-allocation connection. A gap in final counts alone does not establish that the connection existed.

## Conditional changes can leave the marginal unchanged

Before seeing the first draw, what is the chance that the **second** draw is red? There are two relevant paths: RR and BR. Add their probabilities:

$$P(\text{second R})=\frac13+\frac16=\frac12.$$

The same result follows by weighting the two first-draw histories:

$$\frac12\times\frac23+\frac12\times\frac13=\frac12.$$

The **marginal probability** ignores the first colour; the **conditional probability** names it. Reinforcement changes the second-red chance after a specified first outcome without changing its marginal in this symmetric setup.

For a baseline rule that returns the ball **without adding another**, the urn stays 1R/1B. Each two-draw colour path then has probability 1/4. The chance of matching colours is 1/2 in that baseline, compared with 1/3+1/3=2/3 under reinforcement. We have compared a defined event across two complete rules, not declared that every individual run becomes more concentrated.

### When an average hides a difference

The equal marginal does not make the joint laws equal. The matching-colour event differs even though the chance of red on each individual draw is the same. Name the statistic or event you want before saying that two processes behave alike.

## Equal percentage growth preserves a ratio

Now set the urn aside. In a separate deterministic example, two quantities contain 1100 and 1000 units. Multiply **both** by 1.10 once.

This does not deny reinforcement. It distinguishes two claims: a larger quantity can have a larger **absolute** increase while its **relative** lead stays constant. If a story shows a changing ratio, it needs a rule beyond a shared percentage multiplier. Neither that ratio nor a few simulated piles proves a power-law distribution.

### When the distinction is useful

Use it when someone moves from “the bigger account gained more units” to “its share must grow”, or from “the rule rewards current size” to “one winner is inevitable”. Write the quantities and calculate the comparison actually being claimed.
