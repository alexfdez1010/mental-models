A red ball is drawn first. It is returned with another red ball, so red now leads. Someone watching declares: “The winner is settled.” Another person says: “Then the average must rise too.” Neither statement follows from the update alone.

We will calculate an entire small model, not select an impressive run. The questions are specific: **what is the probability of each recorded path, what count does it produce, and which comparison actually changes?**

## Fix the rule and the thing you count

The constructed urn begins with **one red ball and one blue ball**. We make exactly **three draws**. Each draw selects uniformly among all balls currently present. Record the colour and return the selected ball. Compare two rules:

- **Return only:** add nothing; the urn stays one red and one blue.
- **Return and reinforce:** add one new ball of the selected colour after returning it.

There are no new colours, differences in quality or strategic choices. Write **R** for a red observation and **B** for a blue one. A **path** is the full three-letter record, such as RBB. Define **K** as the number of red observations in those three draws. K can be 0, 1, 2 or 3.

K counts recorded draws. It does not count the red balls physically left in the urn, measure an article’s quality or name a winner in a real market.

### When a finite audit helps

Use one when a verbal mechanism is being asked to carry a precise conclusion. Write a small complete case first. It can expose an invalid inference without pretending to reproduce the scale or complexity of the world.

## Multiply the changing branch probabilities

For the reinforced path RRR, the first red chance is 1/2. Returning red and adding another leaves two red among three balls. Repeating red leaves three red among four before the last draw:

$$P(RRR)=\frac12\times\frac23\times\frac34=\frac14.$$

For RBB, the first R still has chance 1/2. There is then one blue among three balls. After B is returned and reinforced, there are two red and two blue:

$$P(RBB)=\frac12\times\frac13\times\frac24=\frac1{12}.$$

The first red lead becomes a tie after the second draw and a blue lead after the third. Conditional on first R, the chance of the two following blue observations is (1/3)(2/4)=1/6. The first gain changes chances; it does not lock every subsequent outcome.

### When to condition on a history

Condition when the question tells you what has already happened. If the history is not supplied, combine its possible values with their probabilities. Reusing the most vivid branch answers a different question.

## Give paths their actual weights

There are eight colour strings. They are equally likely in the symmetric return-only baseline: each draw has red and blue probability 1/2, so every string has probability 1/8. Reinforcement changes that law.

Why can an eight-row catalogue have unequal probabilities? Its rows combine different elementary histories. Temporarily label individual balls, retaining their order and appending each new ball. Under reinforcement the three index choices have sizes 2, 3 and 4. Each complete labelled index history has probability **1/(2×3×4)=1/24**. Six histories give RRR, six give BBB and two give each mixed string. Thus 6/24=1/4 while 2/24=1/12.

Under return only there are 2×2×2=8 equally likely labelled histories. The denominators differ because the two rules define different sample spaces. We have not paired “the same luck” across the two models.

Now group paths by their red count. For K=1, the reinforced paths RBB, BRB and BBR each contribute 1/12, so their sum is **3/12=1/4**. In the baseline they contribute **3/8**.

### When counting rows is legitimate

Counting favourable rows and dividing by all rows works only after equal probability for those elementary rows has been justified. Here it works for labelled index histories. It generally does not work for the displayed colour strings.

## Equal means can hide different laws

The **expected count** is the probability-weighted average of K across possible complete runs. It does not have to be an integer:

$$E[K]=\sum_{k=0}^{3} kP(K=k).$$

For the baseline:

$$E[K]=\frac{0\times1+1\times3+2\times3+3\times1}{8}=\frac{12}{8}=\frac32.$$

For reinforcement:

$$E[K]=\frac{0+1+2+3}{4}=\frac32.$$

Both means are **1.5 red observations per run in expectation**. Every actual run still contains an integer number of red observations. Reinforcement has changed the complete law while preserving this mean. A useful average and an incomplete description can be the same number.

### When a mean is not the full question

Use the mean for a question about a weighted count. Use the relevant event or spread measure for a different question. Equal means alone do not establish equal exposure to a selected event, and a changed exposure does not make the mean arithmetically wrong.

## Define the event before calling it concentration

Choose the event **all three recorded colours agree**. It consists of RRR or BBB, so:

- Baseline: 1/8+1/8=1/4.
- Reinforced: 1/4+1/4=1/2.

This event becomes twice as likely. It is a precise finite statement. It is not a general theorem that every inequality measure rises, that a power law has formed, or that a monopoly is inevitable.

The physical urn still contains both initial colours. After three reinforced draws, its red share is **(1+K)/5**. If K=3, it contains four red and one blue ball: red share **4/5**, while all three recorded observations were red. In the baseline the physical red share stays **1/2** for every K.

### When to name a metric first

Do it before comparing rules or interventions. “More concentrated” can mean several things. An event probability, a variance, a largest share and a distributional tail are different quantities and need their own calculations.

## Transfer to a different starting urn

Now start with **one red ball and two blue balls**. Keep the same three-draw horizon and each of the two update rules. The change is the initial composition, not an unannounced change to the selection law.

Before opening the solution, calculate reinforced RRR and BBB, the law of K, its mean, and the all-same event. Check whether the eight colour strings are equally likely even in the return-only baseline.
