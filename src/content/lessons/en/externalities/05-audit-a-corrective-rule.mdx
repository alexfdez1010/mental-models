Two workshops are told to cut the same number of emissions. One can make the next cut cheaply; the other would consume more resources. Should an equal rule ask both for the same effort? Before answering, write down what is being minimised. Equal emissions, equal costs and equal harm are different targets.

## Declare the unit, the boundary and the objective

**Case card.** A and B are fictional workshops operating for one period at fixed output. Each would emit four physical units of a fictional pollutant without reduction. Each may reduce an integer amount from zero to four. Production benefits are the same in every plan, so they cancel when comparing plans. There is no option to close, change output or adopt another technology.

All resource costs and harms use the same cardinal scale of fictional **credits**, written **cr**. They are known stipulated quantities, not prices or estimates from a real place. Initially, every remaining emitted unit causes **4.5 cr of harm during the period**, from either source. Marginal reduction cost means the extra resources needed for the next one-unit cut; cumulative cost adds all preceding steps.

| Units reduced | Next-step cost A | Cumulative A | Next-step cost B | Cumulative B |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | — | 0 | — | 0 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | 3 | 3 |
| 2 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 8 |
| 3 | 4 | 7 | 7 | 15 |
| 4 | 6 | 13 | 9 | 24 |

For example, A reducing three units consumes $1+2+4=7$ cr, not four credits: four is only the last step. Think of climbing a staircase: the third step is not the entire height climbed. The limit of that analogy is that these steps represent resources, not physical elevation.

<Callout variant="info" title="Who is inside the accounts?">
Include both workshops, affected outsiders and a passive public fund. The fund receives fees and does not rebate them to firms. Receiving money does not repair physical harm or imply compensation to victims. There are no monitoring, administration, collection or transaction costs, evasion, income effects or behavioural changes beyond the stated choices. Add accounts with equal weights. This defines an efficiency measure for this example; it does not settle distributional fairness.
</Callout>

### When to use this setup

Before comparing remedies, state the feasible actions and whose gains and losses count. If output can fall, benefits change or monitoring uses resources, this particular account is incomplete. Do not import a result derived under fixed output into that different problem.

## Compare all feasible plans

Let $a$ and $b$ be the units A and B reduce. Remaining emissions are $4-a$ and $4-b$. Write $C_A(a)$ and $C_B(b)$ for cumulative resource costs. The combined cost to minimise is:

$$T(a,b)=C_A(a)+C_B(b)+4.5(4-a)+4.5(4-b)$$

There are five choices for each workshop: $5\times5=25$ feasible plans. The table gives the combined cost in cr for every one. A row fixes A’s reduction; a column fixes B’s.

| A reduces ↓ / B reduces → | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 36 | 34.5 | 35 | 37.5 | 42 |
| 1 | 32.5 | 31 | 31.5 | 34 | 38.5 |
| 2 | 30 | 28.5 | 29 | 31.5 | 36 |
| 3 | 29.5 | 28 | 28.5 | 31 | 35.5 |
| 4 | 31 | 29.5 | 30 | 32.5 | 37 |

The unique lowest entry is **28 cr at (3,1)**: A spends seven and B spends three; their remaining emissions are one and three. Resource cost $7+3=10$, remaining harm $4.5\times4=18$, combined cost $10+18=28$. Full reduction costs 37 cr and leaves no harm. Eliminating all harm is therefore not the cost-minimising plan under this declared objective.

Now impose a different task: reduce **exactly four units in total**. Only five entries remain feasible:

| Reduction A/B | Resource cost | Remaining harm | Combined cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 / 4 | 24 | 18 | 42 |
| 1 / 3 | 16 | 18 | 34 |
| 2 / 2 | 11 | 18 | 29 |
| 3 / 1 | 10 | 18 | 28 |
| 4 / 0 | 13 | 18 | 31 |

The equal standard (2,2) and plan (3,1) achieve the same reduction and harm, but the standard uses one more credit of resources. Here the cheapest four-unit allocation is also the best among all twenty-five. That coincidence uses the common damage; we will test it after changing one assumption.

### When the objective matters

A cost-effective plan spends the least resources for a specified target. A jointly efficient plan minimises resources plus harm over the entire feasible set here. Neither phrase means “fair to every group” or “safe in every real setting.”

## Derive the private response to a fee

Charge each workshop **4.5 cr per unit still emitted**. Each minimises its cumulative reduction cost plus its payment to the fund. It does not include the outsiders’ harm in that private objective. For a reduction $n$, private cost is $C_i(n)+4.5(4-n)$.

| Units reduced | A: resources | A: fee | A: private total | B: resources | B: fee | B: private total |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 18 | 18 | 0 | 18 | 18 |
| 1 | 1 | 13.5 | 14.5 | 3 | 13.5 | 16.5 |
| 2 | 3 | 9 | 12 | 8 | 9 | 17 |
| 3 | 7 | 4.5 | 11.5 | 15 | 4.5 | 19.5 |
| 4 | 13 | 0 | 13 | 24 | 0 | 24 |

A’s unique private minimum is 11.5 at three cuts; B’s is 16.5 at one. The fee implements (3,1), without assuming the regulator commands those actions. Another way to reconstruct the choice: make a cut when its marginal resource cost is below the fee avoided. A accepts the steps costing 1, 2 and 4, but not 6; B accepts 3 but not 5. Strictly increasing step costs make this marginal comparison agree with the full private table.

At equality, making or skipping that step has the same private cost; keep both best responses. A lower positive fee need not change any integer action: on a **0.5 cr fee card**, even A’s first cut costs more than the payment avoided. Both workshops still reduce zero units.

### When a fee implements a plan

Check the actual private objective, all permitted quantities and ties. Do not infer a strictly smaller quantity merely from a smaller-than-damage positive fee. The size and precision of the action grid matter.

## Keep the fund in the ledger

In the implemented plan, A pays 4.5 and B pays 13.5: the fund receives 18. These payments move purchasing power within our boundary. They are not another eighteen credits of resources consumed.

| Included account | Change relative to common production benefits, cr |
|---|---:|
| A | -11.5 |
| B | -16.5 |
| Affected outsiders | -18 |
| Fund receipts | +18 |
| Sum | -28 |

Equivalently, add only the resources and harm to obtain a positive cost total. Use the builder to assemble those three terms. The initial empty sum is incomplete, not a plan with no harm.

### When transfers stop cancelling in this account

A recipient outside the chosen boundary, unequal distributional weights or extra resource effects require a different calculation. EPA’s economic-analysis guidance distinguishes transfers from real costs and stresses those boundary and behavioural qualifications. Cancellation in a total does not erase who loses or receives money.

## Transfer: harm now depends on the source

**New card.** Change only B’s harm per remaining emitted unit to **7.5 cr**. A stays at 4.5; the technologies, one-period output and other assumptions stay fixed. Before opening the solution, calculate the combined cost if each workshop reduces three units. Use the original cumulative costs and count each source’s remaining unit separately.

The unique minimum over all twenty-five plans is now **34 at (3,3)**: resources 22 and harm 12. The old fee still induces (3,1), because firms’ costs and the fee have not changed; that plan now costs $10+27=37$. Source-specific fees 4.5 for A and 7.5 for B induce (3,3) without ties. Payments sum 12, separately from the 22 resources and 12 harm.

If instead the task still requires exactly four total cuts, the cheapest resource allocation remains (3,1), but the least resources-plus-harm allocation among those five is **(2,2), costing 35**. It spends 11 resources and leaves $2\times4.5+2\times7.5=24$ harm. The cheaper 10-resource allocation leaves 27 harm and costs 37. Equal total emissions no longer imply equal damage.

### When equal quantity is insufficient

If location changes harm, minimising only reduction resources at a fixed quantity can miss the joint objective. Fowlie and Muller distinguish these objectives in their published model; this finite case is our own construction, not their data or a replication of their quadratic model.

## Test the restriction to one uniform fee

Could another uniform fee implement (3,3)? For B to accept its third cut, the fee must be at least 7. But at that price A strictly prefers its fourth cut, which costs 6. Thus no nonnegative uniform fee gives (3,3) as a pair of best responses, even allowing every tie.

A fee of **5.5 cr** induces (3,2) uniquely: A accepts steps costing 1, 2 and 4; B accepts 3 and 5. Its resource cost is $7+8=15$; harm is $4.5+2\times7.5=19.5$; combined cost is **34.5**. The fee payment is $5.5\times3=16.5$, outside that combined total. Here A’s private cost is 12.5 and B’s is 19: $12.5+19=31.5$. The full loss account is $31.5+19.5-16.5=34.5$ after including the fund receipt; private costs alone no longer equal combined cost.

<details>
<summary>Why this covers every nonnegative uniform fee</summary>

A private best response can change only when the fee crosses a marginal step cost: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 9. Between successive thresholds it is constant. At each threshold both adjacent quantities for that workshop tie. Enumerating these intervals and thresholds gives these reachable profiles:

| Uniform fee, cr per emitted unit | Best responses A/B in open intervals | New-damage combined cost |
|---|---|---:|
| $0\le t<1$ | 0 / 0 | 48 |
| $1<t<2$ | 1 / 0 | 44.5 |
| $2<t<3$ | 2 / 0 | 42 |
| $3<t<4$ | 2 / 1 | 37.5 |
| $4<t<5$ | 3 / 1 | 37 |
| $5<t<6$ | 3 / 2 | 34.5 |
| $6<t<7$ | 4 / 2 | 36 |
| $7<t<9$ | 4 / 3 | 35.5 |
| $t>9$ | 4 / 4 | 37 |

At the eight thresholds, take both adjacent table profiles; no two marginal step costs coincide here. For example, at 5, B may reduce one or two; at 6, A may reduce three or four. Both responses minimise that firm’s private cost. They do not all minimise combined cost. The best combined cost reachable under any uniform fee is 34.5, at (3,2); an interior fee 5.5 avoids the threshold ambiguity.

</details>

### When not to turn this into a policy slogan

The half-credit gap is proved only for these known discrete technologies, damages and private objectives. Actual uncertainty, collection costs, monitoring limits, other incentives or variable output can change the comparison. A permit market is another mechanism: we have not simulated trading or shown that a market converges to a price.

## Know what would require a revision

Treat the model like an itemised work order: if an omitted activity becomes necessary, add it before comparing totals. Suppose administering source-specific fees consumed one extra credit while administering the uniform fee consumed none, with all actions otherwise fixed. The stated costs would become 35 versus 34.5; the earlier ranking would reverse. That is a new stipulated case, not evidence of real administrative costs.

### When to report a limit instead of a recommendation

If the boundary, costs or behavioural responses are unknown, report which comparison is missing. An exact minimum inside a stipulated model is not a measured best policy for a real community.
