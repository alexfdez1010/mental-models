This is the final exam for **Externalities**. It pulls together everything: the
precise definition (a real cost or benefit on a **third party**, outside the price),
the master split of **private vs. social cost** and **private vs. social benefit**,
how unpriced negative effects can lead to **over-production** under the competitive benchmark and how **positive** outside benefits can change the best feasible quantity, **deadweight loss** in both directions, the
worked marginal-cost numbers, and the whole repair kit — **Pigouvian taxes and
subsidies**, **cap-and-trade**, **regulation**, and the **Coase theorem** — with
where each one fails. Several questions look easy until you spot the trap: mislabeling
a price-mediated effect as an externality, forgetting the positive half, blessing the
market's quantity by using private instead of social cost, or reaching for one
universal cure.

The workshop questions also distinguish resource cost, remaining harm and transferred payments. Their complete numerical card is below.

## Workshop card

Constructed one-period case with fixed output and equal production benefits across plans. Each workshop initially emits 4 physical units of the same fictional pollutant and may reduce an integer amount from 0 to 4. All amounts use fictional credits (cr). There are no administration costs, evasion, output changes or income effects. Include workshops, affected outsiders and a passive fund: payments to the fund have matching receipts. Add accounts with equal weights; this is not a measure of distributive fairness.

| Reduction per workshop | Marginal A | Cumulative A | Marginal B | Cumulative B |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | — | 0 | — | 0 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | 3 | 3 |
| 2 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 8 |
| 3 | 4 | 7 | 7 | 15 |
| 4 | 6 | 13 | 9 | 24 |

**Main card:** each remaining unit causes 4.5 cr harm, from either A or B. The main fee is 4.5 cr per remaining unit: A reduces 3 and B reduces 1; resources total 10, four units remain and 18 cr is paid to the fund. The fixed total reduction in the allocation question is 4 units.

**Higher-B-damage card:** change only B’s harm per remaining unit to 7.5 cr; A stays at 4.5. Technologies and all other assumptions remain fixed. Each firm minimises resources plus its own payments; the combined objective adds resources and harm without adding the fund transfer again.

The course’s curve questions use their competitive benchmark; workshop quantities are not real estimates.

<Callout variant="success" title="Key takeaways">
An **externality** is a real cost or benefit that lands on a **third party** outside
the transaction and escapes the price — so the private account can omit outside costs or benefits that belong in the complete account, and the market can land on a quantity that fails the complete-account comparison.
For a **negative** production externality in the unit catalogue, add marginal harm to private resources. Compare every feasible cumulative total and retain ties: an omitted cost can change the optimum, but need not force a strict output gap. The avoidable loss is the best complete total minus the total at the quantity assessed. A **positive** externality adds outside benefit to the complete account. Compare all feasible totals: net welfare can be forgone, but ties and capacity matter. Total outside benefit and avoidable loss are different amounts. Corrective tools can change the incentive to account for a spillover; check their assumptions, resource costs and remaining harm: **Pigouvian taxes and subsidies** price it per unit, **cap-and-trade**
fixes the quantity and floats the price, **regulation** mandates the outcome, and the
**Coase theorem** shows clear rights plus cheap bargaining can fix it privately — until
the parties get too many and the rights too fuzzy, which is every big externality.
