A sale can benefit its buyer and seller while leaving someone else with a cost. To assess the result, keep all three accounts open. Then check every feasible quantity: a missing cost does not, by itself, tell you how many units to remove.

Our case uses invented **display panels** and **fictional credits**, abbreviated **cr**. The credits put the stipulated valuations, resources and inconvenience on one common scale. They are classroom data, not estimates of anyone’s actual losses.

A **negative externality** is a cost an activity imposes on others that the relevant decision-makers do not fully take into account. Here, making panels causes a nuisance for neighbours. We stipulate its cost; the neighbours are outside the sale but inside our evaluation.

**Marginal** means the change from adding one more unit. The **marginal private cost**, or **MPC**, is the producer’s extra resource cost. The **marginal social cost**, or **MSC**, adds the extra harm to outsiders. In this model, MSC = MPC + marginal external harm. “Social” names the boundary of the account, not a guarantee that everybody likes the result.

### Fix the model before calculating

There is one production period, at most six indivisible panels, and six buyers who each want at most one. Serve the buyers with the highest valuations first; making a quantity means making the first units in the cost list. All numbers are known. Chosen trades are matched and completed within the period, without cancellations or transaction costs. The producer takes the common price as given. There are no fixed costs, taxes, administration costs, income effects or other market distortions. Private resource costs equal their opportunity costs. No cleaner production method is available in this catalogue.

| Additional unit | Buyer valuation | MPC | External harm | MSC |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 100 | 20 | 40 | 60 |
| 2 | 90 | 30 | 40 | 70 |
| 3 | 80 | 40 | 40 | 80 |
| 4 | 70 | 50 | 40 | 90 |
| 5 | 60 | 60 | 40 | 100 |
| 6 | 50 | 70 | 40 | 110 |

Every amount in the table is in cr for that additional panel. Work through unit 4: the buyer values it at 70, making it uses 50, and the neighbours lose 40. Its private combined contribution is 70−50=20. Its complete contribution is 70−50−40=−20. A transaction can pass the private test while failing the complete one.

## The market has two best quantities

Set the common price at **60 cr per panel**. Buyers valued at 100, 90, 80 and 70 strictly prefer buying. The buyer valued at 60 is indifferent; the buyer valued at 50 does not buy. The producer earns a positive extra margin on the first four units, zero on the fifth and a negative margin on the sixth.

A market clears when the quantity buyers choose equals the quantity the producer chooses to sell. Four and five are both best quantities for the producer, and either can clear this market when the indifferent buyer and producer make matching choices. We will use an explicit rule: **participate when indifferent**, which selects five. That is a tie rule, not evidence that five is the unique optimum.

Let **B** be the buyers’ cumulative valuation and **C** the cumulative resources used. The **combined private surplus**, B−C, adds what buyers and producer gain from the trade. It differs from the producer’s profit.

| Quantity | B | C | Combined private surplus B−C |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 100 | 20 | 80 |
| 2 | 190 | 50 | 140 |
| 3 | 270 | 90 | 180 |
| 4 | 340 | 140 | 200 |
| 5 | 400 | 200 | 200 |
| 6 | 450 | 270 | 180 |

At five units, buyers pay 5×60=300. Their surplus is 400−300=100. The producer’s profit is 300−200=100. Add the two: 100+100=200, the combined private surplus. The 300 payment cancels inside this boundary because it is an expense to buyers and income to the producer. The 200 resource cost remains: resources have an opportunity cost even though the sale also creates a receipt.

### When to use this distinction

Use it whenever “benefit” might mean revenue, buyer value or net surplus. A producer that takes price as given compares price with its own marginal cost. A producer choosing a price would need a different model of revenue and demand.

## Compare every feasible quantity

Include the neighbours. Write **H** for cumulative harm and **W = B−C−H** for the complete net surplus. Compare quantities from zero through six, including the option of making nothing.

| Quantity | B−C | H | W |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 80 | 40 | 40 |
| 2 | 140 | 80 | 60 |
| 3 | 180 | 120 | 60 |
| 4 | 200 | 160 | 40 |
| 5 | 200 | 200 | 0 |
| 6 | 180 | 240 | −60 |

The complete maximum is **60 cr at either two or three panels**. The third unit’s valuation 80 exactly covers its resources 40 and harm 40, so adding it changes W by zero. Keep both maxima. For a single displayed decision, our second declared rule chooses **the larger quantity among the maximizing quantities**: three.

At the selected private quantity five, the buyers’ 100 and producer’s 100 are offset by neighbours’ 200 loss, leaving W=0. At three, B=270, C=90 and H=120, so W=60. Those are different totals even though both quantities support worthwhile private trades.

## Measure the avoidable loss

The **deadweight loss** here is the highest feasible W minus W at the quantity being assessed. It measures avoidable net welfare loss relative to this benchmark. It can also record a gain forgone by producing too little; the same definition compares every row.

For the selected market quantity five: 60−0=**60 cr**. You can check it unit by unit when moving from the selected complete optimum three to five:

| Extra unit | Resources plus harm | Buyer valuation | Net loss |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 | 50+40=90 | 70 | 20 |
| 5 | 60+40=100 | 60 | 40 |
| Total |  |  | 60 |

Equivalently, those two units add 20 to combined private surplus but 80 to harm: 80−20=60. They add harm and also bring benefits. Counting only the extra harm would miss those benefits.

The **total external harm at five units is 200**, which is a different quantity. At a complete optimum of three, some harm remains, but no other feasible quantity gives a higher W. Reducing harm to zero is not our objective irrespective of what must be forgone.

### Where the familiar triangle fits

In a continuous benchmark with straight demand and social-cost curves, the lost surplus between the relevant intersections can form a triangle. With integer units, add their actual net contributions or compare cumulative totals. Joining the rows with a line does not make half a panel feasible. In either representation, the definition is a welfare difference, and the data and feasible choices determine its size.

## A smaller harm can leave a private optimum efficient

Change **only the harm to 20 cr per panel**. Keep valuations, resources, period, allocation and price 60 fixed. Private incentives and their two maxima remain unchanged. Recalculate the complete account:

| Quantity | Combined private surplus | Harm | W |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 80 | 20 | 60 |
| 2 | 140 | 40 | 100 |
| 3 | 180 | 60 | 120 |
| 4 | 200 | 80 | 120 |
| 5 | 200 | 100 | 100 |
| 6 | 180 | 120 | 60 |

Now three and four tie at 120. The larger-quantity rule selects four; the participation rule still selects five privately. That selected market choice loses 120−100=20. But a market clearing at its other private optimum, **four**, has W=120 and **zero deadweight loss**, despite positive external harm.

The finite catalogue explains why a positive cost wedge need not strictly change an optimum. Its private marginal gains are 80, 60, 40, 20, 0 and −20. Subtract the harm from each. As harm rises between 20 and 40, for example, the first three full contributions stay positive and all later ones negative: three remains the unique complete optimum throughout that open interval. At the endpoints, include the zero-contribution ties. Larger harm cannot raise the larger-quantity optimum here, but it need not move it at every change.

In the usual smooth competitive diagram with downward demand, upward private cost and interior intersections, adding a positive marginal external cost moves the full-cost intersection to a smaller quantity. That is a useful benchmark. Its strict movement does not remove the ties or flat ranges in this discrete catalogue.

## Check the inference before choosing a remedy

A high sales volume does not, by itself, measure external harm. A low price does not tell you whether the full marginal cost exceeds the next buyer’s valuation. In the main catalogue, we could calculate because the valuations, resources, harm and feasible production choices were supplied.

Before using the calculation elsewhere, identify the affected people, the period over which harm accrues, its incremental size, the resources forgone and the available alternatives. A cleaner technique could change C and H together. A capacity limit could prevent extra output. A price-setting producer could choose a different private quantity. Each change requires rebuilding the relevant account.

### When this model helps

Use the ledger to expose a missing effect and test whether it changes a specified decision. If the missing measurement could change which row maximizes W, it matters to this comparison. The catalogue alone does not establish which tax, standard or bargain would implement that row, what enforcement costs, or how gains and losses should be distributed. Those are additional decisions; the later lessons examine corrective instruments.
