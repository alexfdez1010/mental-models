A sale may reward its buyer and seller while also helping people outside the deal. Include that outside benefit, then check which feasible choice leaves the most net surplus. A positive spillover can change the best quantity; it need not create a strict gap in every catalogue.

We will use invented **decorative panels** installed in a courtyard visible from a public walkway. All amounts are **fictional credits**, abbreviated **cr**, for one common period. They put the stipulated valuations and resources on a comparable scale; they do not measure real aesthetic preferences.

A **positive externality** is a benefit an activity gives others that the relevant decision-makers do not fully take into account. Here, visitors enjoy the installed panels without paying the buyer or producer. The visitors are outside the sale but inside our evaluation.

**Marginal** means the change from one additional unit. **Marginal private benefit**, or **MPB**, is the buyer’s extra valuation. **Marginal external benefit** is the additional value to the visitors. **Marginal social benefit**, or **MSB**, adds them: MSB = MPB + marginal external benefit. The word “social” sets an accounting boundary. It does not assert that every person benefits equally.

### Fix the model and its boundary

There are five buyers, each wanting at most one panel, and capacity for at most five installed panels in this period. Serve the highest-valuing buyers first; producing a quantity uses the first units in the cost list. All values are known. Each cost includes production and installation at their opportunity cost: what those resources could have done elsewhere. External benefits are additional, additive and not already in the buyers’ values. Include buyers, producer and visitors with equal credit weights; no other benefit or harm is omitted within this case.

The producer takes a common price as given. Chosen market trades are matched and completed within the period. There are no fixed, transaction, cancellation or administration costs, income effects, taxes or other distortions. Another production method is outside this catalogue. These assumptions make the arithmetic inspectable; a real application would need evidence for them.

| Additional panel | MPB | Marginal resources | External benefit | MSB |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 90 | 40 | 30 | 120 |
| 2 | 80 | 50 | 30 | 110 |
| 3 | 70 | 60 | 30 | 100 |
| 4 | 50 | 70 | 30 | 80 |
| 5 | 40 | 80 | 30 | 70 |

Each entry is in cr for that extra panel. For panel 4, the buyer values it at 50 and visitors gain 30, giving MSB=80. Its resources cost 70. The complete contribution is 80−70=10; its private combined contribution is 50−70=−20.

## Check what the private market chooses

Set the price at **65 cr per panel**. The three buyers with values 90, 80 and 70 prefer buying to not buying; those with values 50 and 40 do not. The producer’s extra margins on successive units are 25, 15, 5, −5 and −15. Profit rises through three and falls after that, so the producer uniquely chooses three too. Desired purchases and sales match: the selected trades clear this market. No participation tie is hidden here.

Write **B** for cumulative buyer valuation and **C** for cumulative resources. At three panels, B=240 and C=150. Buyers pay 3×65=195, leaving buyer surplus 240−195=45. Producer profit is 195−150=45. Together they retain **B−C=90**. This is the **combined private surplus**, not the producer’s profit alone.

The payment 195 is an expense for buyers and income for the included producer. It cancels when we add their accounts. The resource cost 150 remains because the resources have an opportunity cost. Visitors receive external benefit 3×30=90, so the complete total is 45+45+90=180. Adding the payment again would count an internal transfer as another benefit.

### When to use these accounts

Specify whose account a number belongs to whenever “benefit” could mean valuation, sales revenue or profit. A producer taking price as given compares price with marginal cost; a producer choosing a price would require another revenue model.

## Compare the whole catalogue

Let **E** be cumulative external benefit and **W = B−C+E** the **complete net surplus**. We will call a quantity efficient if it maximizes this stipulated complete account among the feasible integers zero through five. “Efficient” does not mean fair, unanimously preferred or privately self-enforcing.

| Quantity | B | C | B−C | W if benefit is 20 per panel | W if 30 | W if 40 | W if 50 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 90 | 40 | 50 | 70 | 80 | 90 | 100 |
| 2 | 170 | 90 | 80 | 120 | 140 | 160 | 180 |
| 3 | 240 | 150 | 90 | 150 | 180 | 210 | 240 |
| 4 | 290 | 220 | 70 | 150 | 190 | 230 | 270 |
| 5 | 330 | 300 | 30 | 130 | 180 | 230 | 280 |

With benefit 30 per panel, W rises to **190 at four**, then falls to 180 at five. Four is the unique complete maximum. Three and five tie at 180, but neither maximizes W. An equality between two rows is not sufficient to call them optima.

The benchmark compares physically feasible assignments, including four installed panels. It does **not** say four would trade voluntarily at the unchanged price 65. At that price, the fourth buyer declines and the producer’s fourth margin is negative. Implementing another quantity would require a specified agreement or intervention with its own incentives and costs.

## Measure what is forgone and retain every tie

**Deadweight loss** here means the best feasible W minus W at the assessed quantity. It is a nonnegative gap, including when a better outcome was never produced. With benefit 30, the market’s three panels leave W=180 against the feasible maximum 190: **10 cr of forgone net surplus**. The visitors already receive 90. Producing the fourth would add 30 of external benefit, but also reduce the private combined account by 20. Only the net 10 is forgone welfare.

Keep the private data and price fixed; change only the external benefit. At 20, the fourth unit adds 50+20−70=0. **Three and four both maximize W at 150.** A declared larger-quantity tie rule selects four, but three is also efficient, so the market’s loss is zero. A gap between selected quantities can coexist with no welfare gap.

At 40, the fifth adds 40+40−80=0; **four and five tie for the maximum 230**. At 50, the missing fourth and fifth add 30 and 10, so the unique feasible maximum is five at 280, versus 240 at the market’s three: loss 40. Five is the capacity limit. We have not described a sixth unit and cannot optimize over it.

For all nonnegative per-panel benefits e, the private marginal contributions are 50, 30, 10, −20 and −40. Add e to each. Before e=20, three is the complete maximum; at 20, three/four tie; between 20 and 40, four; at 40, four/five tie; above 40, five within this capacity. Retain the maximizing set first, then apply the larger-quantity rule if a single selection is needed.

### Where the familiar triangle fits

With continuous quantities and suitable straight curves, a lost-surplus area can be triangular. This catalogue has indivisible units: sum their actual net contributions or compare cumulative totals. A line through its points does not make half a panel feasible. Losses from either a negative or positive externality use the same nonnegative welfare-gap definition; the sign of the spillover does not give the loss an opposite sign.

## Decide what evidence would change the conclusion

For the main catalogue, the fourth panel costs 20 more resources than its buyer values it. An outside benefit above 20 makes it increase W; exactly 20 leaves a tie; below 20 makes it reduce W. If a measurement only narrows that benefit from 18–22 to 19–21, the best quantity is still unresolved. Learning whether the fourth unit’s benefit lies above or below 20 could change the decision. A precise sales-revenue figure would not answer that question.

Check the period, resource alternatives and affected people before adding values. If visitors’ benefit has already been included in a buyer’s valuation, adding it again double-counts. If a new technique changes cost, recalculate the entire feasible catalogue. If an agreement lets private parties capture some outside value, their incentives may change too; private price 65 is no longer necessarily the right response model.

A low quantity or low producer profit alone does not establish a positive externality. You need evidence of an additional benefit to outsiders and a comparison of feasible choices. A benefit can be observable even when it is unpriced. Once a welfare gap is demonstrated, choosing a policy still requires its responses, resource costs and distributional effects.
