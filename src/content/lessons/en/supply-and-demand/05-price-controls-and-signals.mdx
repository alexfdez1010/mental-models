The equilibrium diagram compares quantities buyers plan to buy and sellers plan to offer. Matching quantities make those plans compatible in the model; a completed transaction still requires a buyer, seller and execution.

A **price control** restricts allowed prices. A maximum forbids prices above a limit; a minimum forbids prices below one. Neither definition says every permitted trade must occur at exactly that limit. In the examples below, we state the actual price being analysed separately and keep the schedules fixed.

## Price ceilings restrict the allowed price

A **price ceiling** is a legal maximum. If tickets may not cost more than a specified amount, lower prices remain allowed. In the usual competitive diagram with a single crossing, a maximum below the reference clearing price prevents that price. A maximum above it need not constrain it.

For the comparison, suppose the posted price is set at the binding maximum. The diagram then gives a quantity demanded above quantity supplied: **excess demand**, also called a **shortage at that price**. This compares willingness, not necessarily physical stock or a completed sales count.

**Worked example — rent control.** Use this constructed apartment schedule, holding other conditions fixed:

| Monthly rent | Apartments offered (supply) | Apartments wanted (demand) |
|---|---|---|
| \$1,800 | 1,000 | 400 |
| \$1,400 | 700 | 700 |
| \$1,000 | 500 | 1,000 |
| \$800 | 400 | 1,200 |

At **\$1,400**, the two planned quantities are 700. Now impose a maximum of **\$800** and separately assume the actual posted rent is **\$800**. The table gives **400 offered** and **1,200 wanted**, so excess demand is **1,200 − 400 = 800 apartments**.

If all 400 offered apartments are matched and rented, 800 willing households remain unserved. The schedule does not tell us who obtains those apartments. A queue, lottery or priority rule could allocate them differently. Maintenance changes, side-payments and waiting costs require additional behavioural assumptions and evidence; they do not follow from the subtraction alone.

The 1970s gasoline price caps in the United States were accompanied by [long queues](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/bernanke20080604a.htm). That historical illustration does not make a particular queue or access rule automatic in every constrained market.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Distinguish the listed price from access">
A served buyer may pay less under a binding maximum, while another willing buyer may receive nothing. Check who trades and include any waiting or other access costs before judging the result. A lower tag alone does not establish the full cost or benefit for every participant.
</Callout>

### When to use it

Identify the maximum, the actual posted price and the schedules at that price. Then ask how the available opportunities are allocated and how many trades complete. Treat a proposed queue or quality effect as a further claim to investigate.

## Price floors restrict the allowed price

A **price floor** is a legal minimum. Higher prices remain allowed. In the usual single-crossing competitive model, a floor above the clearing price excludes that reference price. If the actual price is at that binding floor, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded: **excess supply**, or a **surplus at that price**. Offers are not automatically observed unsold inventory.

**Worked example — agricultural price supports.** Suppose the reference wheat price is \$5 a bushel, a minimum is \$8 and the actual posted price is \$8. With the usual upward supply and downward demand relationships held fixed, the model gives more offered than buyers want at that price. If a government then buys the excess, those purchases add another buyer to the account. The allocation, storage and costs must be recorded as well as the price rule.

**The famous, contested example — minimum wage.** Here's the same shape applied to the price of
*labour*. A **minimum wage** is a price floor on an hour of work: employers may not pay below it.
The textbook supply-and-demand model says the same thing it said about wheat — set the wage above
the market-clearing level and you'd expect a **surplus of labour**: more people wanting jobs at
that wage than employers want to hire, which is just another word for **unemployment**, felt most
by low-skill or entry-level workers.

<Callout variant="info" title="Honest caveat — the labour market is not a wheat market">
That clean prediction is the *model*, and it's a genuinely useful first lens — but real labour
markets are messier than a strawberry stand, and the empirical evidence is genuinely **mixed and
debated**. A few reasons the simple model can break: some employers have **monopsony** power (few
big employers in a town, so they were paying *below* the competitive wage to begin with, and a
floor can actually raise employment); **efficiency wages** (paying more can make workers more
productive and stick around); and decades of real-world studies that find effects ranging from
"noticeable job loss" to "barely any." Treat the model as a thinking tool that tells you *what
forces are in play* — not as a verdict. This lesson takes no political side; it hands you the lens
and leaves the policy fight to you.
</Callout>

### When to use it

Separate the minimum from the actual wage or goods price being analysed. A gap between offered and demanded quantities is a model comparison; interpreting employment, inventories or government purchases needs the corresponding assumptions and records.

## A price limit leaves an allocation question

A limit changes the allowed price set. It does not rewrite the willingness schedules by definition, identify the posted price or tell us which buyers and sellers complete trades. Those are separate parts of an audit.

**Ticket resale** illustrates the distinction. Suppose the organiser chooses an official ticket price of \$50 and some resales occur at \$300. The chosen official price is not automatically a legal price ceiling. Under an assumed fixed ticket supply, if more people want tickets at \$50 than are offered, there is excess demand at that price. Resale is one possible way opportunities change hands; its actual extent and effect require a record. A few observed resales alone do not identify the entire demand schedule or a unique market-clearing price.

## A price can carry useful information

A price affects the trade-offs buyers and sellers face. A higher receipt can make offering a good more attractive; a higher payment can make some buyers switch to alternatives. Under the competitive model, these incentives can support decentralised coordination.

The pencil supply chain is a useful illustration: timber, graphite, lacquer, equipment and transport can be supplied by different firms responding to prices and orders. It does not follow that one observed price contains every relevant fact or that no organisation is needed anywhere in the chain.

A price can reflect costs, preferences, scarcity and the rules of exchange. It does not identify their separate contributions from the number alone. Nor does willingness to pay reveal need independently of budget. To judge a change, specify a comparison and inspect the affected participants, resource costs and any effects outside the market boundary.

<Callout variant="success" title="Keep the signal and its limits together">
Prices can communicate incentives and support coordination. A price limit can alter which trades are feasible under a fixed schedule, but price, completed quantity, participant selection and fairness are different questions. The model helps organise them; the transaction record supplies evidence about what happened.
</Callout>

Opportunity cost remains the link to the prerequisite. A payment is part of a buyer's sacrifice and a seller's receipt, while giving up resources has its own opportunity cost. In the next workshop you will put both sides inside one account and see why the payment is not an extra net gain to the whole group.
