This is a **constructed demand model**, not an estimate from a real stall. For one business day, quantity requested is `Q(P)=200−10P`, only for prices from **8 to 12 credits per litre**. `P` is the numerical price in credits per litre; `Q` is litres requested during that day. Other demand conditions are held fixed by assumption.

At least 120 litres are available, every requested litre sells at the common posted price, and there are no refunds, taxes or rationing. That fulfillment assumption lets us use requested quantity as sold quantity here. A demand schedule by itself does not establish completed sales.

| Policy | Price in credits/litre | Litres sold in the day | Revenue in credits |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 10 | 100 | 1000 |
| B | 12 | 80 | 960 |

Both rows are alternatives for the same modeled day. They are not two observed days from which we have identified a causal price effect. Weather, competitors or customers' budgets could change an actual demand schedule.

## Name the percentage bases

**Price elasticity of demand** measures quantity's responsiveness to price in relative terms. For finite changes, you must name the percentage convention. Here we work with a downward-sloping demand schedule and report magnitudes as nonnegative numbers.

Using the **original policy as the base**, moving A→B gives a price change of `2/10=20%` and quantity change of `−20/100=−20%`. The magnitude ratio is **1**. Reverse B→A and the bases change: price changes by `−2/12`, quantity by `20/80`, and the magnitude ratio is **3/2**. Both are correctly calculated original-base ratios, answering direction-dependent comparisons.

The **midpoint method**, also called midpoint arc elasticity, uses the average of the two endpoint prices and quantities. Those bases remain the same when you reverse direction:

$$E_{\mathrm{arc}}=\left|\frac{(Q_2-Q_1)/((Q_1+Q_2)/2)}{(P_2-P_1)/((P_1+P_2)/2)}\right|.$$

Average price is `(10+12)/2=11`; average quantity is `(100+80)/2=90`. Thus:

$$E_{\mathrm{arc}}=\frac{20/90}{2/11}=\frac{11}{9}.$$

It is the same 11/9 (about 1.222) in reverse. For this convention, an arc is **elastic** above one, **inelastic** below one and **unit elastic** exactly at one. Original-base ratios, arc coefficients and local coefficients are different summaries; attach the name to the number.

A coefficient is dimensionless: converting credits to hundredths of a credit changes the raw slope, but cancels in percentage changes. Keep positive endpoint prices/quantities for the comparisons here. If both prices are equal, the percentage price change is zero and this ratio cannot identify elasticity; do not manufacture a zero coefficient.

## Calculate revenue for the whole change

**Total revenue** is price times the quantity actually sold. Here `10×100=1000` and `12×80=960` credits. Revenue falls **40 credits**, or **4%**. This does not say that profit falls.

Rebuild the second policy's revenue in two non-overlapping pieces: the eighty retained litres at the old price contribute **800**; the extra two credits on each of those eighty litres contribute **160**. Total **960**. Against the old revenue, the price gain of 160 is smaller than the lost twenty litres' old-price contribution of `20×10=200`.

More generally, if price rises by fraction `g` and sold quantity falls by fraction `h`, both relative to the original values, the revenue factor is **`(1+g)(1−h)`**. It equals one at `h=g/(1+g)`, not at `h=g`. With price 12, holding the old 1000-credit revenue requires **250/3 litres**, about **83.333**. Fractions of litres are meaningful; eighty litres falls short.

Even an original-base ratio **below one** is insufficient for the usual exact revenue shortcut. In a **different endpoint table**, let price still change 10→12 but quantity change 100→82. The original-base ratio is `18%/20%=0.9`, yet revenue becomes `12×82=984`, a **1.6% decrease**. Its midpoint coefficient is `99/91`, above one. This changed endpoint is not generated by the earlier linear schedule at price twelve.

For two positive endpoints on the downward schedule, with the second price higher, the **midpoint** revenue rule is exact: arc elasticity below one accompanies higher endpoint revenue; above one, lower revenue; at one, equal revenue. Check the products when the endpoints are available.

<details>
<summary>Check the exact midpoint identity</summary>

Substitute the midpoint formula and simplify:

$$R_2-R_1=\frac{(P_2-P_1)(Q_1+Q_2)}{2}(1-E_{\mathrm{arc}}).$$

When `P2>P1` and `Q2≤Q1`, the factor outside the parentheses is positive. The revenue difference therefore has the sign of `1−E_arc`. This identity describes the stated endpoints; it does not validate their estimates or predict other conditions.

</details>

## Subtract costs separately

Declare **all costs within this toy model** for the same day as `C(Q)=100+4Q` credits: one hundred fixed plus four for each litre sold. The resulting **model profit** is revenue minus those modeled costs.

| Policy | Revenue | Fixed cost | Variable cost | Total cost | Model profit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1000 | 100 | 400 | 500 | 500 |
| B | 960 | 100 | 320 | 420 | 540 |

Revenue falls forty; total costs fall eighty; model profit therefore rises forty. We compare two stipulated policies, not every possible business choice. A real account must also consider costs omitted from this model, including relevant opportunity costs.

## Keep local and interval claims distinct

A **local**, or point, elasticity describes responsiveness near one price. It need not be constant along a demand curve. In our stipulated straight-line schedule, near price ten, an extra price unit reduces quantity by ten litres. Scaling that local slope by price/quantity gives magnitude `10×10/100=1` at that point. It does not make revenue constant across the finite move to twelve: we already calculated 960 rather than 1000.

<details>
<summary>Optional local-rate calculation</summary>

A derivative is the local rate of change. Where quantity is positive and the curve is differentiable, the downward-demand magnitude is `E_point=−Q′(P)P/Q(P)`. Here `Q′(P)=−10`. Also `R(P)=P(200−10P)=1000−10(P−10)^2`. Revenue has a zero first-order change at ten but falls by forty at twelve. One unit-elastic point is not an entire interval of constant revenue.

</details>

The next figure is a **separate constructed family**, not a fit to the stall's table: **`Q(P)=5(P/5)^(−e)` only for 5≤P≤10**. Quantity is litres during one fixed model day; price is credits/litre; revenue is credits for that day. This family has constant local elasticity `e` throughout the admitted interval. Its revenue is `25(P/5)^(1−e)`.

At **e=1**, moving price 5→10 changes quantity 5→2.5 and keeps revenue at **25**. Price doubles while quantity halves: equal revenue does not require equal original-base percentage magnitudes. At **e=0.9** revenue increases with price; at **e=1.1** it decreases. Neither parameter is exactly unit elastic. These results rely on the constant-e family, not on every real market sharing its shape.

## Interpret the coefficient within its setting

Substitutes, budget constraints, adjustment time and available capacity can matter for responsiveness. These are considerations for forming and testing a model, not permanent labels that determine a measured elasticity for every medicine, luxury or city. Specify which good, population, price interval and horizon the coefficient describes. A high price or steep-looking graph alone does not identify it.

**Supply elasticity** applies the same midpoint normalization to offered quantities. In a separate stipulated supply table, price 10→12 accompanies quantity offered 100→140 litres for a day. The midpoint supply coefficient is `(40/120)/(2/11)=11/6`, above one. This is quantity **offered**, not a completed-sales record. A supply constraint can prevent demand from being fulfilled, which would break the earlier revenue calculation's fulfillment assumption.

Likewise, two real sales records from different days can differ because weather, incomes or competitors changed. You can calculate a descriptive percentage ratio from the records; attributing it to price alone needs evidence that separates those other changes. This lesson does not provide that empirical identification from two observations.

<summary>Check the independent transfer</summary>
