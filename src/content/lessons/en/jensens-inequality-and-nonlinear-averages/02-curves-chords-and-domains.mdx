One pair of inputs can give a positive gap even when a response is not convex over the whole relevant interval. The theorem needs an interval-wide shape condition, rather than a favorable calculation at a few observed points.

We will define that condition geometrically, then separate it from increasing output and desirable outcomes.

## Read the chord at the same input mean

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}

  explanation={"Convexity is an interval-wide chord condition."}

A function’s **domain** is the set of inputs where it is defined. A one-dimensional **convex domain** is an interval: it contains every weighted point between any two of its members.

A response $f$ is **convex** on an interval if, for every pair $x,y$ in that interval and every mixing weight $0\le\theta\le 1$,

$$
f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y).
$$

The straight segment joining $(x,f(x))$ and $(y,f(y))$ is a **chord**. At the horizontal weighted mean $\theta x+(1-\theta)y$, the chord’s height is the weighted average of the endpoint responses. Convexity places the curve at or below that height.

For the square response, inputs two/six and weights three/one give the mean input three. The curve height is nine and the chord height twelve. More generally, expanding the square shows:

$$
\theta x^2+(1-\theta)y^2-[\theta x+(1-\theta)y]^2
=\theta(1-\theta)(x-y)^2\ge 0.
$$

This identity verifies every pair and mixing weight, not just the displayed example.

### When a picture is evidence

A plotted chord makes a definition easier to inspect. A finite set of sampled curve points does not prove the inequality for every point in the interval. Here the algebra supplies that missing general argument.

## Reverse the sign and locate equality

A function is **concave** if its negative is convex. Its curve lies at or above its chords, reversing the inequality. An **affine** function has the form $a+bx$ and is both convex and concave: its graph is a straight line, so the two operations agree.

A function is **strictly convex** if the chord inequality is strict whenever $x\ne y$ and $0<\theta<1$. Equal arguments or a weight of zero/one do not produce strictness. Affine functions therefore satisfy convexity but not strict convexity on a nontrivial interval.

### When “greater” needs a condition

Use the non-strict sign for a convex response. To claim strict inequality, check both the response’s strictness and actual variation among positive-weight inputs. The next lesson extends that distinction to more than two rows.

## Separate curvature from direction and bounds

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  question={"A function is bounded below and unbounded above. What follows about its curvature?"}
  options={[{"text":"Those bounds alone establish convexity."},{"text":"Those bounds alone do not establish convexity.","correct":true},{"text":"Those bounds alone establish strict convexity."}]}
  explanation={"The square-root counterexample has those bounds but is concave."}
  pretest={true}
/>


Convexity does not say whether more response is good. Our square-work example is a cost. Nor does increasing output imply convexity: a square-root response is increasing on nonnegative inputs but concave.

A lower bound and an unbounded upper range do not establish convexity either. The function $r(x)=\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,\infty)$ has both properties. Yet with inputs zero/four and weights three/one, the mean input is one. Its response is $r(1)=1$, while the mean endpoint response is $(3/4)0+(1/4)2=1/2$. The convex inequality fails.

This one failed pair is enough to disprove convexity on that domain. In contrast, a single successful pair would not establish convexity everywhere. No payoff floor or upside slogan supplies the missing chord test.

### When to choose a response

Specify the response first, including its units and domain. Then establish its shape over the full interval joining the positive-weight inputs and their mean. The objective determines how to interpret the larger side; it does not change the mathematical sign.
