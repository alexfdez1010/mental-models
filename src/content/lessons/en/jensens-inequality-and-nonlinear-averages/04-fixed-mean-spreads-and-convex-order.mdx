“More variable” can hide several different claims. A square response notices variance exactly. A different convex response may rank the same pair in the opposite direction.

To make a comparison that works for every convex response, we need a stronger construction than a larger variance number.

## Learn what the square gap measures

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}

  explanation={"Variance is E[X²]−(E[X])² for the finite law."}
  pretest={true}
/>


For a supplied finite law with mean $\mu$, its **variance** is the weighted mean squared distance from that mean:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_i p_i(x_i-\mu)^2=E[X^2]-\mu^2.
$$

Expanding the square proves the second equality because $E[X]=\mu$ and the weights sum to one. Thus the square-response Jensen gap equals variance when the response coefficient is one. With input measured in jobs, variance has units of jobs squared. A work coefficient converts that into staff-minutes; the two units are not interchangeable.

A constant input three has square response nine and variance zero. Equal-probability inputs two/four keep the mean three but give mean square response ten, so variance is one.

### When variance answers the response question

For an exact quadratic response, the identity tells you the gap. It does not make variance sufficient for every nonlinear response. The meaning of the response and any conversion coefficient still matter.

## Preserve each parent mean before averaging

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  question={"A replacement preserves each parent mean. Which check identifies that condition?"}
  options={[{"text":"The overall child and parent means match, without examining branches."},{"text":"The child variance equals the parent variance, without examining means."},{"text":"Each child branch’s normalized mean equals its own parent value.","correct":true}]}
  explanation={"The sufficient construction is conditional, not merely an overall equality."}
  pretest={true}
/>


A **conditional mean** $E[Y\mid X=x]$ averages the possible child values of $Y$ under the probabilities specified for a particular parent $X=x$.

Let $X$ be two/four with equal probability. If $X=2$, replace it by $Y=1$ or $3$, equally likely. If $X=4$, replace it by $Y=3$ or $5$, equally likely. Each child pair has its own parent’s mean:

$$
E[Y\mid X=2]=2,\qquad E[Y\mid X=4]=4.
$$

The four complete paths each have probability one quarter. The two paths reaching three are distinct paths to the same value, whose probabilities add. The final law of $Y$ has weights one/two/one on values one/three/five.

Both unconditional means are three. The square-response means are ten for $X$ and eleven for $Y$. More importantly, apply finite Jensen separately within each parent branch:

$$
f(x)=f(E[Y\mid X=x])\le E[f(Y)\mid X=x].
$$

Multiply each branch inequality by its parent probability and add. This gives $E[f(X)]\le E[f(Y)]$ for every finite convex response on the common interval. The construction preserves the conditional parent means, rather than just the overall mean.

### When zero-mean noise is not enough

Writing $Y=X+Z$ is sufficient here if $E[Z\mid X]=0$. Independence is not required for the branch proof. An unconditional $E[Z]=0$ alone is weaker and can remove variation instead.

For example, keep $X=2$ or $4$ equally, but set $Y=3$ always. Then $Z=3-X$ is plus one or minus one with overall mean zero. Its conditional mean is plus one at the first parent and minus one at the second. Mean square response falls from ten to nine. The condition used in the proof has failed.

## Compare all convex responses rather than one summary

For finite laws on a common interval, say $X$ is below $Y$ in **convex order** when $E[f(X)]\le E[f(Y)]$ for every finite convex function on that interval. We compare laws with the same input mean. The conditional construction above is one sufficient way to establish that order; we are not claiming an unproved converse representation theorem.

Convex order is partial: some pairs cannot be ranked. Here is an exact pair on $[0,4]$:

A has values one/three equally. B has values zero/two/four with weights one/six/one out of eight. Both have mean two and variance one. Consider two convex hinges, each a maximum of zero and an affine function:

$$
h_2(x)=\max(0,x-2),\qquad h_3(x)=\max(0,x-3).
$$

For $h_2$, A’s response mean is one half and B’s one quarter. For $h_3$, A’s is zero and B’s one eighth. The ranking reverses. Thus neither law is above the other for every convex response.

### When a comparison deserves the stronger name

Use convex order only for an all-convex-function statement justified by a proof or a sufficient construction. Checking one response or matching means and variances does not establish it. The term is mathematical; it does not call every response “risk” or decide which direction an objective prefers.

[Rothschild and Stiglitz’s primary paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0531(70)90038-4) discusses conditional mean-preserving dispersion, partial ordering and the limits of variance. Its economic interpretation is not needed for these finite constructions. The branch proof and hinge numbers above are worked locally.
