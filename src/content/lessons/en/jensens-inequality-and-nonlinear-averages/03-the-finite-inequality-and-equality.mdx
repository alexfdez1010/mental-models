A two-point chord compares two inputs. A table can contain many more. The finite Jensen inequality extends the comparison by grouping rows, provided each group keeps its own denominator.

We will work one three-input proof completely before stating how the grouping repeats.

## Group a finite weighted law

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  question={"To average a subgroup, what happens to its original outer probabilities?"}
  options={[{"text":"They remain unchanged and already sum to one."},{"text":"They are discarded and replaced by equal shares."},{"text":"They are divided by the subgroup’s positive total mass.","correct":true}]}
  explanation={"Normalize within the subgroup, then retain its mass in the outer comparison."}
  pretest={true}
/>


Take nonnegative weights with positive total, normalize them to $p_i$, and retain the positive-weight support values $x_i$. Suppose the finite-valued response $f$ is convex on an interval containing those values and everything between them. Then:

$$
f\left(\sum_i p_i x_i\right)\le\sum_i p_i f(x_i).
$$

The left side transforms the weighted input mean. The right side averages transformed inputs. For a probability law this is $f(E[X])\le E[f(X)]$; for a complete fixed catalogue it is the same weighted arithmetic without an inferred future sampling law.

Return to inputs zero/two/six with weights two/one/one. Their overall mean is two. Group the first two rows. Their mass is $3/4$, but their **within-group normalized weights** are $2/3$ and $1/3$. The group mean is:

$$
x_G=\frac23\times 0+\frac13\times 2=\frac23.
$$

Now combine that group mean with the remaining value six:

$$
\frac34\times\frac23+\frac14\times 6=2.
$$

For the square response, first use the chord inequality on the two groups:

$$
f(2)\le\frac34 f(2/3)+\frac14 f(6)
=\frac34\times\frac49+9=\frac{28}{3}.
$$

Then use convexity inside the first group:

$$
\frac{28}{3}\le
\frac34\left(\frac23 f(0)+\frac13 f(2)\right)+\frac14 f(6)
=1+9=10.
$$

Together, $4\le 28/3\le 10$. The intermediate bound is neither the final response mean nor the response at the overall mean; it is the result of replacing only the first group by its mean.

### When a subgroup is reweighted

Divide its original weights by its own mass before computing its conditional mean. Carry that group’s mass back into the outer average. Keeping the original outer probabilities inside every subgroup loses normalization.

## Repeat the argument without adding hidden assumptions

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  question={"Does the finite Jensen inequality require successive input draws to be independent?"}
  options={[{"text":"Yes, otherwise a weighted sum cannot be defined."},{"text":"No; this inequality concerns one supplied finite weighted law.","correct":true},{"text":"Yes, unless every response is affine."}]}
  explanation={"A finite distribution already defines the sum; no sequence-of-draws independence is used."}
  pretest={true}
/>


After zero-weight rows are removed, a single supported value gives equality. With more values, group the first rows into mass $q$ and leave one row outside. If both groups are nonempty, $0<q<1$.

The interval domain contains the first group’s weighted mean. Apply the two-point inequality between that mean and the final value. Apply the same grouping argument within the first group. Expanding the normalized group weights restores the original probabilities. Repeating this step reaches the finite sum above.

This is an induction: the one-row case starts the proof, and a valid smaller group supports the next size. All sums and function values here are finite, so no infinite-limit interchange or integrability assumption is hidden. Independence between different draws is irrelevant; this statement concerns one supplied distribution of an input.

For concave $f$, apply the convex argument to $-f$ and reverse the sign. An affine response makes every grouping step an equality.

### When the theorem applies

Check the full interval, not only the listed points. A response defined only at isolated inputs does not yet provide a value at every group mean. A model with an infinite law requires a separate check that its expectations and theorem conditions are meaningful; this finite proof does not establish that extension.

## Distinguish variable inputs from strict inequality

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  question={"A convex response and variable positive-weight inputs always give a strictly positive gap…"}
  options={[{"text":"False; an affine segment can give equality.","correct":true},{"text":"True; every convex function is strictly convex."},{"text":"True; variation alone supplies strictness."}]}
  explanation={"Strictness belongs to the response and the support together."}
  pretest={true}
/>


If $f$ is strictly convex and there are at least two distinct positive-weight inputs, the finite inequality is strict. To see why, separate the smallest support value from all larger values. Their conditional mean is strictly larger; both outer weights are positive, so that grouping already makes the first chord inequality strict.

Two sources of equality remain easy to recognize. A single supported value has nothing to average across. An affine response on the relevant interval commutes with the average even when input values differ.

Convex responses can also contain affine pieces. Define the hinge $h(x)=\max(0,x-4)$. It is convex because it is the pointwise maximum of two affine functions, zero and $x-4$. For equal-probability inputs one and three, both responses and the response at their mean two are zero. The inputs vary while the active response interval is flat.

### When an equality is informative

It may reflect a degenerate input, an affine response or an affine part of a convex response. It does not by itself show that every input is identical. State the strictness conditions before interpreting a zero gap.
