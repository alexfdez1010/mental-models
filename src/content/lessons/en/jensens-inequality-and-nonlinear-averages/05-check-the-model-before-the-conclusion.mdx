A correct theorem can be applied to the wrong response. A model that squares inputs up to a cap is not the uncapped square function on the full interval, even if their outputs coincide on some observed rows.

We will audit the response domain, the supplied law and the accuracy claim separately.

## Check the response over the full interval

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  question={"A capped square agrees with a square at some inputs. Can the uncapped square’s convexity be assumed across the cap?"}
  options={[{"text":"No; the actual response needs its own interval-wide check.","correct":true},{"text":"Yes; agreeing below a cap fixes the whole shape."},{"text":"Yes; any nonnegative response is convex."}]}
  explanation={"The capped response has a different shape across the relevant interval."}
  pretest={true}
/>


Define $g(x)=\min(x^2,9)$ on $[0,6]$. It agrees with the square up to three and then stays flat. At the original inputs two/six, its outputs are four and nine.

With probabilities three quarters/one quarter:

$$
E[g(X)]=\frac34\times 4+\frac14\times 9=\frac{21}{4}.
$$

The mean input is still three, and $g(3)=9$. Therefore $E[g(X)]-g(E[X])=21/4-9=-15/4$. This negative gap is not a counterexample to Jensen: the capped response fails convexity over the support interval $[2,6]$.

The failed chord is a direct witness. Do not classify the whole response from the fact that it agrees with a convex formula below its cap.

A domain can fail before an inequality is even available. The reciprocal $1/x$ is undefined at zero. A law equally supported at minus one/one has mean zero, so its response at the mean is undefined. Moreover, the connecting interval crosses the domain’s hole. Do not report that response as zero, infinity or a finite mean by convention.

### When an input is outside the model

Report the missing definition or condition. A response defined at every observed row can still be undefined at the mean or between rows. Restricting to an interval where the model is defined is a substantive assumption, not cosmetic notation.

## Change probabilities without calling it a fixed-mean spread

Keep square response and input values two/six. Let the probability of six be $p$, so that of two is $1-p$. Then:

$$
E[X]=2+4p,\qquad E[X^2]=4+32p.
$$

Subtracting the square of the new mean gives:

$$
E[X^2]-(E[X])^2=16p(1-p).
$$

### When to show sensitivity

Use it to expose what follows from plausible specified inputs. Name what stays fixed and what changes. Do not label an arbitrary probability edit a mean-preserving intervention.

## Bound response error without inventing evidence

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  question={"A justified uniform response-error bound applies on the entire relevant interval. How many parts of the Jensen gap can change?"}
  options={[{"text":"Both the mean response and the response at the mean.","correct":true},{"text":"Only the mean response over supported inputs."},{"text":"Only the probability weights of the input law."}]}
  explanation={"The response error can shift both quantities that are subtracted."}
  pretest={true}
/>


Suppose a model response is $f$ and the true response is $g$, on the same relevant interval. You have separately justified the uniform bound $|g(x)-f(x)|\le\varepsilon$ throughout that interval. A bound just at observed input rows would omit the response at the mean.

For a fixed finite law, the mean response can shift by at most $\varepsilon$, because its nonnegative weights sum to one. The response at the mean can also shift by at most $\varepsilon$. Subtracting the two quantities therefore gives:

$$
\left|[E[g(X)]-g(E[X])]-[E[f(X)]-f(E[X])]\right|\le 2\varepsilon.
$$

For the model gap three and $\varepsilon=1/2$ response point, the true gap lies between two and four. Those bounds concern the same supplied law; they do not establish that either response is convex or that the assumed error bound has empirical support.

### When a bound is useful

Use it after identifying where the error guarantee comes from and the interval it covers. The calculation propagates a justified assumption. It cannot manufacture one from a neat table or replace evidence with a tolerance chosen after seeing the result.
