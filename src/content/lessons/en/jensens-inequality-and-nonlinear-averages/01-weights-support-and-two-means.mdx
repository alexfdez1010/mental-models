A table can contain three rows without describing three possible outcomes. One row may have zero weight. And a list of weights is not normalized merely because it looks roughly balanced.

Before applying a response rule, specify what the rows mean and which denominator turns their weights into shares.

## Turn weights into a complete finite law

Let input values be $x_i$ and weights $w_i$. This course requires each weight to be nonnegative and the total $W=\sum_iw_i$ to be positive. Define normalized weights:

$$
p_i=\frac{w_i}{W},\qquad \sum_i p_i=1.
$$

For values two and six with weights three and one, $W=4$, so the shares are $3/4$ and $1/4$. Multiplying all weights by the same positive factor preserves their normalized shares and both averaging calculations. It does not create more independent evidence.

The **support** is the set of distinct values with positive weight. A zero-weight row contributes nothing and does not become a possible outcome of a stipulated probability law. Repeated rows at the same value can have their weights added without changing the law.

### When the rows need interpretation

If weights describe probabilities, justify that complete law separately. If they are counts in a fixed catalogue, the normalized shares describe that catalogue. The same arithmetic can calculate a descriptive average without making a claim about future sampling.

The input mean is:

$$
\mu=\frac{2\times 0+1\times 2+1\times 6}{4}=2.
$$

The response at that mean is $f(2)=4$. To average responses, transform before weighting:

$$
E[f(X)]=\frac{2\times 0^2+1\times 2^2+1\times 6^2}{4}=10.
$$

The six-response-point gap is $10-4=6$. The weighting denominator is four in both calculations, not the number of distinct input values. Taking the ordinary unweighted mean of zero, two and six would answer another question.

### When the mean is not an option

A weighted mean can lie between supported inputs. In the first law, three is the mean even though only two and six occur. Evaluating a function at the mean requires that intermediate input to be in its domain. It does not by itself make the mean a listed outcome or a feasible action.

### When to compare distributions

Record the new weights, not just the resulting response mean. A probability edit generally changes the input mean as well. Later we will construct a change that preserves each parent’s conditional mean; that is a different claim.
