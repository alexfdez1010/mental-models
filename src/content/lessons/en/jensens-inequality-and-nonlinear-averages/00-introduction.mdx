An “average batch” sounds like a tidy way to estimate work. It can also be a batch that never occurs, passed through a rule in the wrong order.

Suppose a constructed process receives two jobs with probability three quarters and six jobs with probability one quarter. Its declared processing-work model squares the batch size. Averaging the inputs and then squaring will not give the same answer as squaring each possible input and averaging the work.

**Jensen’s inequality** explains when the shape of a response function orders those two calculations. Its home is mathematics, particularly convex analysis and probability. Our goal is to use it without quietly changing a distribution, a domain or the objective.

Write $E[X]$ for its weighted input mean. It is:

$$
E[X]=\frac34\times 2+\frac14\times 6=3\ \text{jobs}.
$$

A **response function** assigns an output to each allowed input. Here the stipulated work rule is $c(x)=\alpha x^2$, where $\alpha=1$ staff-minute per job squared. Thus a two-job batch requires four staff-minutes and a six-job batch thirty-six. This is a teaching model, not an empirical law of workers or machines.

**Transform each input, then average**:

$$
E[c(X)]=\frac34\times 4+\frac14\times 36=12\ \text{staff-min}.
$$

**Average the inputs, then transform**:

$$
c(E[X])=1\times 3^2=9\ \text{staff-min}.
$$

The **Jensen gap** in this order is $E[c(X)]-c(E[X])=3$ staff-minutes. The common mean input is three jobs; the outputs differ because the square response is nonlinear. A nonlinear function need not preserve weighted averages.

### When the shortcut needs checking

Reach for this comparison when someone replaces varying loads, concentrations or other inputs by one average before applying a response model. First identify which operation the question actually asks for. Neither calculation validates the response model or its probabilities.

## Decide what a larger response means

Here the response is processing work. Twelve staff-minutes is more work than nine, not an automatic advantage. A convex cost can make variation expensive. A convex benefit would require its own model, units and objective.

The expected work of twelve is also not a third possible batch result. A single batch in this law has four or thirty-six staff-minutes of modeled work. An expectation combines possibilities; it need not be realized by one observation.

Nor does the gap itself identify a feasible intervention that saves three staff-minutes. Keeping every batch at three jobs might require coordination, violate an arrival restriction or be unavailable. The final workshop supplies feasible plans and counts their additional work before choosing between them.

### When to attach a preference

Name the output and objective: lower work, higher benefit, a deadline, or another criterion. The inequality compares mathematical quantities. A preference needs that extra interpretation and a comparison of complete feasible alternatives.

We will start with finite lists of values and nonnegative weights. That avoids hidden assumptions about convergence of an infinite expectation. The course defines its additional vocabulary before using it: support, function domain, convexity, strictness, conditional means and convex order.

1. [Weights, Support and Two Means](/jensens-inequality-and-nonlinear-averages/01-weights-support-and-two-means) normalizes the data and distinguishes two operations.
2. [Curves, Chords and Domains](/jensens-inequality-and-nonlinear-averages/02-curves-chords-and-domains) defines the shape over an interval.
3. [The Finite Inequality and Equality](/jensens-inequality-and-nonlinear-averages/03-the-finite-inequality-and-equality) proves the weighted result and checks when equality survives variation.
4. [Which Changes in Variability Can Be Ranked](/jensens-inequality-and-nonlinear-averages/04-fixed-mean-spreads-and-convex-order) uses a conditional construction and two opposing hinge responses.
5. [Check the Model Before the Conclusion](/jensens-inequality-and-nonlinear-averages/05-check-the-model-before-the-conclusion) changes curvature, probabilities and response accuracy.
6. [Audit a Batch Plan](/jensens-inequality-and-nonlinear-averages/06-audit-a-batch-plan) counts complete work and tests an independent transfer.
7. The [28-question final exam](/jensens-inequality-and-nonlinear-averages/final-exam) locks each submitted answer and shows the score at the end.

The prerequisite is [Thinking in Probabilities](/thinking-in-probabilities), which supplies finite weighted expectation. We do not assume calculus or a background in finance. A derivative test will not be required for the calculations or exam.
