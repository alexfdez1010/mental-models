A report claims that a service improvement “cut waiting in half.” Before accepting it, identify the quantity, population and comparison. The same sentence might refer to an observed cohort mean, a time-average queue size or a theoretical stationary mean.

This workshop combines the course's tools. Each case supplies its inputs; no real service outcome is being claimed.

## Identify what the reported number is

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"A model produces T=30 minutes. What must be specified before calling it an observed mean wait?"}
  options={[{"text":"Whether it is a model result or data estimate, and whether it includes service.","correct":true},{"text":"Only whether both rates use the same time unit."},{"text":"Nothing; T always means observed waiting."}]}
  explanation={"A model total stay and an observed wait are distinct measures."}
/>


A model card says admitted arrivals are Poisson at eight jobs per hour, independent services are exponential at ten per hour, and the other M/M/1 assumptions hold in a stationary regime. The model mean total stay is $60/(10-8)=30$ minutes. Mean waiting before service is 24 minutes.

A bare report of the two rates would not establish those distributional assumptions. Even with the full card, thirty is a mean total stay, rather than a maximum or a wait excluding service.

An audit therefore keeps four questions visible: what the job boundary includes, which time window or regime is used, what inputs are known and which outcome the decision targets.

### When to request more evidence

If an observed log supplies no service starts, it may not separate waiting from processing. If rejected requests are missing, its population may not represent all demand. If the rates shift by hour or class, a constant-rate stationary calculation needs further justification.

P is the only completed job by minute 4. Its full stay is two minutes. Multiplying the one-completion rate $1/3$ by that stay gives $2/3$, which misses Q's partial presence and includes P's unobserved first minute. Those errors do not cancel here.

R arrives at the right endpoint. Its presence before that endpoint has duration zero, while an after-event cursor at time 4 includes it. The observation window and instantaneous state are different quantities.

### When a record is censored

Keep the observed part of an open job's presence. Do not assign it a zero full stay, drop it because it has not departed or silently add an unknown future. The area calculation does not supply a statistical estimate of the missing remainder.

## Follow the requests outside the boundary

Now compare two explicitly stipulated stationary model cards. In the first, admitted arrivals are Poisson at eight per hour and service rate is ten. In the second, admitted arrivals are Poisson at six per hour with the same service model. The corresponding model mean total stays are thirty and fifteen minutes.

The second card excludes some requests before admission. Suppose the declared external demand remains eight per hour. The two-per-hour difference lies outside the admitted-system boundary; this case supplies no waiting or completion outcomes for those excluded requests.

The admitted-system model mean halves. That does not establish that all requests are served sooner, that the machine became faster or that the change causally improved a real operation. It also does not prove that a real admission rule would produce the stipulated Poisson stream.

### When the boundary is part of the policy

A reduction in admitted demand can be a legitimate design choice, but its consequences belong in the decision. Include outcomes for the excluded requests when the objective concerns all requests, rather than moving inconvenient work out of the denominator.

## Choose within a declared menu

The final case is a planning exercise. Assume the stated M/M/1 model remains valid. Every incoming job must be admitted. Arrival rate may be anywhere from six to eight per hour; no probability distribution is assigned to that interval.

The requirement is mean total stay at most fifteen minutes for every rate in the interval. Implementation budget is three effort credits. Among feasible listed options, prefer the lower implementation cost. These credits are a fictional resource unit, separate from minutes and service rates.

At the largest arrival rate, eight, the three mean total stays are thirty, fifteen and ten minutes. Option A misses the time requirement. B meets it and uses two of the three credits. C meets the time requirement but exceeds the budget. B is therefore the feasible choice in this menu.

That conclusion is bounded by the stated alternatives, requirement, budget and model. It supplies no universal optimal server size. In an actual service, the rate/service assumptions and implementation consequences need evidence.
