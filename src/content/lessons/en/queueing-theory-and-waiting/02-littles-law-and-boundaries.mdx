A queue length is a stock at an instant. A stay is a duration for a job. The bridge between them is an area: every minute that a job is present contributes one job·minute to the system's occupancy.

We will add the individual rectangles first. That makes it easier to see which boundary a later formula is actually describing.

For example, during minutes 2–3, A is in service and B/C are waiting. Three jobs for one minute contribute three job·minutes. After minute 8 the system is empty; the final two minutes contribute zero.

This equality is accounting. It does not need a random-arrival assumption or a claim that the server is FCFS. FCFS produced these particular intervals; counting the same intervals by row or by time gives the same area.

A **time-average occupancy** divides the area by the observation duration:

$$
L_{[0,10)}=\frac{13}{10}=1.3\ \text{jobs}.
$$

A fractional mean does not mean that the system contains 1.3 jobs at one instant. Its instantaneous counts are whole numbers; the average combines the time spent at each level.

### When the area is useful

Use it when a dashboard's time-weighted mean and a list of individual durations seem inconsistent. Compare the same intervals before deciding which measurement is wrong.

## Close the cohort before using full stays

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"When can all full stays be used directly for this finite window’s area?"}
  options={[{"text":"Whenever at least one job has completed."},{"text":"Whenever arrivals are Poisson."},{"text":"When the included complete presence intervals exactly cover the observed cohort’s presence, with no omitted earlier/open pieces.","correct":true}]}
  explanation={"A closed cohort supplies the finite cancellation; Poisson assumptions are not required."}
/>


Here the system had no earlier jobs, and all four supplied jobs leave inside the observation window. The four full stays therefore contribute exactly the entire observed area.

The **cohort** is the set of jobs included in the calculation. For these four jobs:

$$
\overline{T}=\frac{3+4+4+2}{4}=\frac{13}{4}=3.25\ \text{min}.
$$

The finite admission rate is $\lambda_{[0,10)}=4/10$ jobs per minute. Multiplication cancels the cohort size:

$$
\lambda_{[0,10)}\overline{T}
=\frac4{10}\times\frac{13}{4}
=\frac{13}{10}
=L_{[0,10)}.
$$

This is the complete-cohort finite identity on this trace. The observation duration belongs in the rate denominator; the number of jobs belongs in the stay mean denominator.

**Little's law** gives a corresponding long-run relationship when the relevant finite limits exist. Sigman's [sample-path notes](https://www.columbia.edu/~ks20/4106-18-Fall/Notes-LL.pdf) state those conditions and account for partial stays at an open boundary. The long-run result does not require Poisson arrivals or FCFS. It also does not make arbitrary short-window averages interchangeable.

### When to multiply

Name the population, boundary and clocks first. For a finite complete cohort, cancellation proves the product directly. For a long-run claim, check that the quantities are the compatible limits whose existence and finiteness the result requires. A neat numerical match alone does not establish a process model.

## Separate waiting from service

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"To turn mean system occupancy into mean waiting occupancy, what must be removed?"}
  options={[{"text":"The mean number in service.","correct":true},{"text":"Exactly one job under every condition."},{"text":"The arrival rate."}]}
  explanation={"For one server the mean number in service is its busy fraction, which need not equal one."}
/>


The four waits are 0,2,3,0 minutes. Waiting area is five job·minutes, so mean waiting occupancy is $5/10=1/2$ job. Service area is eight job·minutes, so the machine is occupied for $8/10=4/5$ of the window.

The averages preserve the same decomposition as the snapshots:

$$
L=L_q+\overline{B}=\frac12+\frac45=\frac{13}{10}.
$$

The complete-cohort mean wait includes both zero waits:

$$
\overline{w}=\frac{0+2+3+0}{4}=\frac54\ \text{min}.
$$

Using the all-admission rate gives $(4/10)(5/4)=1/2$. You can instead condition on the two jobs that actually wait: their rate is $2/10$, their mean wait is $5/2$, and the product is again $1/2$. Mixing the rate of all four with the positive-only mean gives 1, which is the wrong population pairing.

  explanation={"(2/10)(5/2)=1/2. Alternatively (4/10)(5/4)=1/2; mix neither population."}

For each job, stay equals wait plus service. Across this complete cohort, the means are $13/4=5/4+2$. The mean number in service is $4/5$, not automatically one: the machine is idle after minute 8.

### When the boundary changes

“Queue” sometimes means only waiting jobs and sometimes includes service. Write the definition rather than trusting the word. Keep zero-duration waits in an all-admission cohort, or change both the rate and the conditional mean together.

## Stop the clock while jobs remain open

Now end observation at minute 4. A contributes its complete three-minute stay. B contributes only minutes 1–4, three minutes; C contributes minutes 2–4, two minutes. D has not arrived. The observed system area is therefore 8 job·minutes, giving $L_{[0,4)}=8/4=2$ jobs.

Only A has departed before that endpoint. Its full stay times the departure count, divided by four minutes, gives $3/4$. That misses B/C's observed pieces. Adding their entire future stays would make the opposite mistake: it would treat unobserved later time as if it had occurred inside the window.

For any individual presence interval $[a_i,d_i)$ and observation window $[l,r)$, the overlap duration is:

$$
c_i=\max\bigl(0,\min(r,d_i)-\max(l,a_i)\bigr).
$$

The outer maximum turns a non-overlap into zero. Summing these clipped durations gives the window's area. The same operation applies to waiting and service intervals.

Try the third window, 2–5 minutes. A contributes 1 minute, B contributes 3 and C contributes 3, giving 7 job·minutes in a three-minute observation. Its mean occupancy is $7/3$, while the complete stays of all four jobs remain unchanged.

The cursor is a snapshot after events at its timestamp. An arrival exactly at a window's right endpoint has zero exposure before that endpoint, although it can appear in the cursor's after-event count. A departure exactly at that endpoint can have a fully observed stay. A single instant has no duration, so these conventions do not change an area.

The full trace is supplied to make this audit reproducible. In a live observation, an open job's eventual departure may be unknown. Clipping its observed presence does not require pretending that the unknown remainder is zero or forecasting it from this diagram.

### When a short window is all you have

Report the window occupancy and the duration actually observed. Keep open jobs visible. To estimate complete-stay behavior beyond the window, you need a separately justified method and its assumptions; the area identity supplies no missing future.
