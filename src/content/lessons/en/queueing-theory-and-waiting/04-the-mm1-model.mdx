A complete list of future jobs let us calculate a finite trace. Often those future inputs are unknown. A stochastic queueing model supplies distributions for them, then asks what follows if the assumptions hold.

**M/M/1** names one such model. The two M symbols refer to memoryless interarrival and service distributions; the 1 means one server. The code is not an empirical diagnosis. We must state the full model before using its results.

## Specify the uncertain inputs

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"Which addition turns two average rates into the full M/M/1 assumptions?"}
  options={[{"text":"Nothing else is needed."},{"text":"A short line observed once."},{"text":"The stipulated Poisson arrivals, independent exponential services and server/boundary/regime conditions.","correct":true}]}
  explanation={"Rates are only parameters inside the specified model."}
/>


A **homogeneous Poisson arrival process** has a constant rate $\lambda$, independent arrival counts in disjoint time intervals and the following count law. Over a duration $t$, the number of arrivals $K$ satisfies:

$$
P(K=k)=e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots
$$

Here $k!$ means the product from 1 through $k$, with $0!=1$; $e$ is the constant approximately 2.71828. The expected count is $\lambda t$. Rate and duration must use matching time units.

With $\lambda=6$ jobs per hour and $t=10$ minutes $=1/6$ hour, the expected count is 1. The chance of no arrivals is $e^{-1}\approx0.3679$; the chance of at least one is about 0.6321. An expected count of one does not mean exactly one arrival is certain.

An **exponential service duration** with rate $\mu$ has tail probability:

$$
P(S>s)=e^{-\mu s},\qquad s\ge0.
$$

Its mean service duration is $1/\mu$. At $\mu=10$ per hour, that mean is 0.1 hour, or six minutes. Individual services need not take six minutes.

Services are independent of one another, have the same exponential law and are independent of the arrival process. The model also assumes one work-conserving FCFS server, unlimited waiting room, constant rates, no rejection, no abandonment and no rework. Equal-time arrival probability is zero in the Poisson model; our finite trace still needed a tie convention.

### When the model is a candidate

Inspect how requests are admitted, when work is available, whether services overlap and whether the conditions change by time or job class. A pair of average rates alone does not establish independence, an exponential service law or any of the other assumptions.

## Understand what memoryless means

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"Under exponential service, does surviving the first six minutes force service to end in the next six?"}
  options={[{"text":"No; the conditional remaining-time law has the same tail.","correct":true},{"text":"Yes; twelve minutes is the maximum."},{"text":"Yes; mean service is a deadline."}]}
  explanation={"Exponential durations have no finite upper cap."}
/>


For the six-minute mean service example, the probability that service exceeds six minutes is $e^{-1}\approx0.3679$. The probability that it exceeds twelve minutes is $e^{-2}\approx0.1353$.

Given that it has already exceeded six minutes, the chance of needing more than another six is:

$$
P(S>12\mid S>6)=\frac{e^{-2}}{e^{-1}}=e^{-1}\approx0.3679.
$$

That is the memoryless property. It concerns the stipulated duration distribution. It does not say that a real machine forgets repairs or that all long jobs have the same remaining work.

## Derive a stationary occupancy law

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"Does a stationary occupancy distribution mean the job count never changes?"}
  options={[{"text":"Yes, every count is frozen."},{"text":"Yes, all arrivals must stop."},{"text":"No; its distribution is unchanged with observation time.","correct":true}]}
  explanation={"A stationary random count can still move up and down along a realization."}
/>


A **stationary distribution** is a probability distribution for the number present that does not change with the observation time. The count itself still changes as jobs arrive and depart.

Starting an empty system at time 0 gives $N(0)=0$. That deterministic startup is not the stationary distribution derived below. We are calculating a regime under the model, not choosing a universal warm-up period or simulating its first few minutes.

Let $\pi_n$ be the stationary probability of $n$ jobs in the system, including service. For this M/M/1, balance between neighbouring states gives:

$$
\lambda\pi_n=\mu\pi_{n+1},\qquad n\ge0.
$$

Arrivals move the count up; a completion when the system is nonempty moves it down. Writing $\rho=\lambda/\mu$, the balance recursion gives $\pi_n=\pi_0\rho^n$.

For $0<\rho<1$, the geometric sum is $1+\rho+\rho^2+\cdots=1/(1-\rho)$. Normalizing total probability to one gives:

$$
\pi_0=1-\rho,\qquad \pi_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n.
$$

You can check the infinite sum through finite pieces: probabilities from 0 through $m$ add to $1-\rho^{m+1}$, and the remaining tail is $\rho^{m+1}$. As $m$ grows, that tail tends to zero.

If $\rho\ge1$, the balance sequence cannot be normalized: a positive $\pi_0$ makes the sum diverge, while $\pi_0=0$ leaves total probability zero. This M/M/1 then has no stationary probability distribution. That statement does not make an individual finite trace infinitely long at a finite time.

### When to use the law

Use it for the stated model in its stationary regime and stable domain $0<\lambda<\mu$. Do not treat the probability bars as observations from one finite cohort. With no arrivals, there is no typical admitted job whose stay can be averaged; our client-time formulas use $\lambda>0$.

## Obtain the means without subtracting the wrong customer

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"For a stable one-server model, which quantity should be subtracted from L to obtain Lq?"}
  options={[{"text":"The stationary busy fraction ρ.","correct":true},{"text":"Always one."},{"text":"The arrival rate λ in jobs/h."}]}
  explanation={"Mean number in service is ρ, not one and not a rate."}
/>


For a nonnegative integer count, you can count its value in layers: one unit if $N\ge1$, another if $N\ge2$, and so on. For a finite cap $m$:

$$
E[\min(N,m)]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}P(N\ge j).
$$

Under the geometric law, $P(N\ge j)=\rho^j$. Let the cap grow; the geometric sum converges, so:

$$
L=E[N]=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}=\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}.
$$

The server is busy exactly when $N>0$. Its stationary busy fraction is $1-\pi_0=\rho$. Thus the mean number waiting is:

$$
L_q=L-\rho=\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho}.
$$

Subtracting one would incorrectly pretend that the server is always busy. A stable queue has positive probability of being empty.

Little's law now connects these stationary measures to client times. With rates per hour:

$$
T=\frac{L}{\lambda}=\frac1{\mu-\lambda}\ \text{hours},
$$

$$
T_q=T-\frac1\mu=\frac{\lambda}{\mu(\mu-\lambda)}\ \text{hours}.
$$

At $\lambda=6,\mu=10$, load is $3/5$. We get $L=3/2$ jobs, $L_q=9/10$ job, total stay $1/4$ hour=15 minutes and waiting $3/20$ hour=9 minutes. Mean service is six minutes, and $15=9+6$.

### When a numerical answer is still conditional

A correct evaluation of the formula does not validate its inputs. [MIT's M/M/1 handout](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-854-introduction-to-manufacturing-systems-fall-2016/927056a1af54772a587fd84ad4951e71_MIT2_854F16_Mm1Queue.pdf) distinguishes system, queue and service measures. The [queueing lectures](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/1-203j-logistical-and-transportation-planning-methods-fall-2006/a9235f5e4e0aee12a55778e5beaf0ddb_lec5.pdf) state the model framework. None makes an arbitrary observed service an M/M/1 merely because its rates fit on the sliders.
