Four jobs share one machine. Someone adds their service times, divides by the number of jobs and calls the result “the average wait.” That calculation gives an average service requirement. It has not yet accounted for who arrives while another job is occupying the machine.

We will reconstruct the missing clock. The inputs are fixed teaching data, and every job is admitted. Nothing is being estimated from a real workshop.

All times are minutes from the same origin. The server is available and the system is empty just before time 0. There are no breakdowns, pauses, rework, cancellations or unlisted jobs.

**First-come, first-served**, abbreviated **FCFS**, gives earlier arrivals priority. Tied arrivals retain their order in the input table. The identifier is a label, so changing a name does not move that job forward. Once a job starts, its service is **nonpreemptive**: a later arrival cannot interrupt it.

A **work-conserving** server starts the next available job whenever it is free. It may be idle when no admitted job is available; it does not insert a voluntary break while work waits.

### When these inputs are enough

They determine this constructed trace because the service durations and future arrivals are supplied. In practice, knowing past arrivals does not reveal future arrivals or unknown remaining service. A computed trace inherits the information in its inputs.

## Work one row before completing the table

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"B arrives at minute 1; the preceding job departs at 3. What sets B’s earliest start?"}
  options={[{"text":"1+3"},{"text":"The larger of 1 and 3","correct":true},{"text":"The smaller of 1 and 3"}]}
  explanation={"B needs both arrival and server availability."}
/>


For job B, arrival is minute 1. Job A occupies the machine from 0 to 3. B cannot start before either its own arrival or the previous departure, so:

$$
b_B=\max(1,3)=3.
$$

Here $b$ denotes service start. Add B's two minutes of service:

$$
d_B=3+2=5.
$$

Waiting time is start minus arrival: $3-1=2$ minutes. Total stay is departure minus arrival: $5-1=4$ minutes. B's stay contains both its wait and its service.

For jobs in FCFS order, write $a_i$ for arrival, $s_i$ for required service, $b_i$ for start and $d_i$ for departure. With the previous departure initially 0:

$$
b_i=\max(a_i,d_{i-1}),\qquad d_i=b_i+s_i.
$$

$$
w_i=b_i-a_i,\qquad T_i=d_i-a_i=w_i+s_i.
$$

The maximum is important. If the next job arrives after the previous one has left, the machine must wait for that arrival. A future job cannot be served early merely to keep the server busy.

### When to check a surprising delay

Locate its arrival, previous departure and start. A long stay can include waiting, long service or both. Their remedies need not be the same, and this trace alone supplies no costs for choosing one.

## Read a state after its events

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"At the instant a job departs and the next starts, can both be counted as occupying this one server after events?"}
  options={[{"text":"Yes, because their intervals share an endpoint."},{"text":"No; the departed job is excluded and the new service is included.","correct":true},{"text":"Only if their IDs are in alphabetical order."}]}
  explanation={"Half-open intervals give one after-event state."}
/>


Define $N(t)$ as jobs present in the **system** at time $t$, including service. Define $N_q(t)$ as jobs waiting to start. Let $B(t)$ be one when the server is busy and zero otherwise.

A job is present from arrival up to, but excluding, departure: $[a_i,d_i)$. It waits on $[a_i,b_i)$ and uses the server on $[b_i,d_i)$. The notation $[x,y)$ includes $x$ and excludes $y$.

We report the state after events at the timestamp. At time 3, A has departed and B has started; C is waiting. Thus $N(3)=2$, $N_q(3)=1$ and $B(3)=1$. At time 6, C departs and D starts, so the system contains only D.

For this single-server trace:

$$
N(t)=N_q(t)+B(t).
$$

The identity separates waiting from service. Work conservation adds another condition: if any job waits, the server must be busy. A made-up schedule could satisfy the identity while leaving a waiting job beside an idle machine; it would violate the service rule.

### When simultaneous events occur

Release the completed job, admit the new arrivals and then start the earliest waiting job. A fresh arrival does not jump ahead of someone who was already waiting. If several arrive together, use their declared input order.

## Check the supplied trace on one clock

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"One job present for three minutes contributes what presence area?"}
  options={[{"text":"Three jobs per minute."},{"text":"One third of a job."},{"text":"Three job·minutes.","correct":true}]}
  explanation={"Area multiplies the number present by duration."}

The diagram lets you inspect the same inputs at different times. Its shaded interval is the observation window. The full bars still show the complete supplied trace; they are not a forecast of a job whose future service is unknown.

It also reports **presence area**. One job present for three minutes contributes three job·minutes. Summing these durations gives the area beneath the system's occupancy steps. Waiting area uses only waiting intervals; service area uses only service intervals. Dividing an area by the window's duration gives an average number present, rather than a typical job's duration.
