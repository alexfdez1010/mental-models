Two one-machine traces each contain four jobs, two minutes of service per job and a ten-minute observation window. They finish the same number of jobs and consume the same eight minutes of service. Their waiting times still differ.

This comparison is constructed to isolate timing. It is not a sample proving that any increase in an unspecified “variability” measure worsens every possible queue.

In the batch, A starts at 0, B at 2, C at 4 and D at 6. The waits are 0,2,4,6, adding to 12 minutes. Dividing by four jobs gives a mean wait of three minutes. Mean stay includes the two service minutes, so it is five minutes.

The spaced trace has zero wait and a two-minute mean stay. Both finish all four jobs by minute 8. Moving arrivals together changes who has to wait behind an occupied server; it does not change any required service duration.

### When a rate hides timing

Use this comparison when someone infers zero waiting from spare average capacity. Across 0–10 minutes, both traces use 80% of the available server time. One still has a substantial waiting line early in the window.

## Compare like-for-like averages

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"The batch has mean stay 5 min and admission rate 4/10 jobs/min. Its mean system occupancy is…"}
  options={[{"text":"2 jobs","correct":true},{"text":"5 jobs"},{"text":"4/5 job"}]}
  explanation={"(4/10)×5=2, using the complete cohort."}

The time-average number present is $8/10=4/5$ job in the spaced trace and $20/10=2$ jobs in the batch trace. The admission rate is $4/10=2/5$ jobs per minute in both.

For the spaced trace, $(2/5)\times2=4/5$. For the batch trace, $(2/5)\times5=2$. Little's complete-cohort accounting still works. The two outcomes differ because mean stays differ, rather than because an accounting identity failed.

Finite **busy fraction** is actual time serving divided by available observation time. It is not the same as the number currently waiting. A server can be continuously occupied during an interval while nobody waits, if each new job arrives exactly when the previous service ends.

Select the 0–8-minute window in the diagram. In the spaced trace the busy fraction is one and waiting remains zero. Extending that perfectly synchronized deterministic pattern keeps that result. It does not describe M/M/1 arrivals and services.

The later stochastic model has its own stationary boundary at $\lambda<\mu$. Do not apply its near-boundary delay formula to every timeline with a busy fraction near one.

### When equal averages are insufficient

Compare the ordering, clustering and dependence that the averages omit. Here every input is known, so we can identify the effect of changing the supplied arrival times in this deterministic calculation. Real causal claims need evidence that the other relevant conditions stayed comparable.

## Declare what happens when arrivals tie

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"Z and A arrive together. If IDs are labels, what determines their order here?"}
  options={[{"text":"Alphabetical sorting of their IDs."},{"text":"Whichever has the largest service requirement."},{"text":"Their declared input order.","correct":true}]}
  explanation={"The finite FCFS convention preserves input order for equal arrival times."}
/>


Our FCFS convention keeps input order when arrival times tie. Consider Z and A both arriving at 0: Z needs three minutes, A needs one. B arrives at 3 and needs two minutes.

With input order Z/A/B, Z runs 0–3, A runs 3–4 and B runs 4–6. The total wait is $0+3+1=4$ minutes. When B arrives at 3, A has already waited since 0; the newcomer does not jump ahead.

Now supply the tied arrivals in the order A/Z/B. A runs 0–1, Z runs 1–4 and B runs 4–6. The total wait is $0+1+1=2$ minutes. The names and service requirements did not change; the declared priority within the tie did.

### When the detail affects an audit

Logs often record several arrivals at the same coarse timestamp. Before treating their row order as genuine priority, ask what the service actually used. An invented ordering can create an invented wait estimate even when all durations were copied correctly.

## Transfer the comparison without inventing a distribution

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  pretest={true}
  question={"Equal monthly mean arrival and service rates reveal which complete sequences occurred?"}
  options={[{"text":"Exactly one pair of sequences."},{"text":"They do not identify complete sequences or dependence.","correct":true},{"text":"Only the sequence with the shortest waits."}]}
  explanation={"Many timing patterns can share those summaries."}
/>


A report says two months had the same average arrival count and the same average service duration. Those two summaries do not specify their complete arrival/service sequences, a probability law or the dependence between jobs.

The examples here establish results for their stated sequences. They do not identify a universally sufficient “variability” score, prove every batch harmful under every objective or tell you how likely either trace is in another service.

For that next step, a model must describe the uncertain inputs. [MIT's queueing-model framework](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/1-203j-logistical-and-transportation-planning-methods-fall-2006/a9235f5e4e0aee12a55778e5beaf0ddb_lec5.pdf) distinguishes the arrival process, service process, number of servers and discipline. The following lesson will state all of them before calculating stationary measures.
