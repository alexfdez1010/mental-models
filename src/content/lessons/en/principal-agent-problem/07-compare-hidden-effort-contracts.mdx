An archive offers a success bonus to a cataloguer. The batch passes its quality check. Did the bonus buy extra checking, or did routine work get lucky? The invoice alone cannot tell you. Before judging the outcome, work out what the proposed contract made worthwhile.

This workshop compares **fully specified payment schedules**. That means payments for each outcome in our small model; it does not mean a real contract covering every future contingency. You will solve the agent's choice before calculating the principal's return.

## Write the whole game

Consider **one hypothetical archive batch**. Success means passing an independently applied, pre-agreed quality check by Friday. The check always resolves the result at the deadline; failing to submit a verifiable batch counts as failure. The archive is the principal; Noor, the cataloguer, is the agent.

| Quantity | Routine work L | Extra checking H |
|---|---|---|
| Success probability | 0.40 | 0.80 |
| Failure probability | 0.60 | 0.20 |
| Effort cost | 0 credits | 18 credits |

Success is worth **120 credits** to the archive; failure is worth **zero**. Noor can instead take an outside opportunity worth **10 credits**, with no archive work. Credits are invented accounting units for this one batch. Noor's effort cost is expressed in equivalent utility units; it is not an additional invoice paid by the archive.

The assumptions do real work:

- Both parties are **risk-neutral**: compare expected net credits, without an extra premium for uncertainty. They share the stated probabilities and know the effort costs.
- Noor can accept or decline, then privately choose either effort. No other effort, hidden type, future promotion or intrinsic reward enters this model.
- The outcome is verifiable and cannot be manipulated. Payment is enforceable, nonnegative and contingent only on that outcome. There are no deposits, fines, monitoring charges or renegotiation.
- We leave choices at exact equality **unspecified**. A tie does not secretly mean “choose what the principal wants.”

These are stipulated model inputs, not measured forecasts of real cataloguers. The hidden-action framework comes from contract theory; its sequence separates contract offer, participation, effort and chance. [Holmström's original model](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/1979-holmstrom.pdf), [MacKie-Mason's formal timing and constraints](https://open.umich.edu/sites/default/files/downloads/MacKie-Mason-SI680-w08-class3-LectureNotes-asymmetric-contracting-model_0.pdf)

## Find the agent's best response

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="To predict effort after a contract is accepted, which comparison does the agent need?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Expected payment minus effort cost under each available action.', correct: true },
    { text: 'The success payment alone under the action named by the principal.' },
    { text: 'The principal’s expected output before deducting any transfer.' }
  ]}
  explanation="An action is a best response when it maximises the agent’s expected net utility under the offered payments. The principal’s preferred action need not be that choice."
/>

Write the failure payment as $w_F$ and the success payment as $w_S$. Under effort $e$, the success probability is $p_e$ and effort cost is $c_e$. Noor's **expected net utility** is:

$$
U_e=p_e w_S+(1-p_e)w_F-c_e
$$

For the **Lean** offer, failure pays **0** and success pays **50**:

| Action | Expected payment | Effort cost | Expected net utility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Routine L | 0.40 × 50 + 0.60 × 0 = 20 | 0 | 20 − 0 = 20 |
| Checking H | 0.80 × 50 + 0.20 × 0 = 40 | 18 | 40 − 18 = 22 |

Checking is the **unique best response conditional on working**: **22 > 20**. The bonus does not guarantee success; it makes the action with the higher success probability privately worthwhile under these assumptions.

Now change the offer to **Flat**, paying **15** in either outcome. Routine utility is **15**; checking utility is **15 − 18 = −3**. Noor chooses routine work. A higher chance of success creates no extra payment under that schedule.

The **incentive-compatibility constraint (IC)** says that the intended action must be a best response. For checking, $U_H\ge U_L$ is the weak condition; $U_H>U_L$ makes it uniquely best among these two actions. Checking whether Noor accepts is a separate step.

## Check participation separately

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A contract makes checking better than routine work, but both give less than the outside opportunity. What does the model predict?"
  options={[
    { text: 'The agent declines, despite the conditional incentive to check.', correct: true },
    { text: 'The agent accepts because checking beats routine work.', correct: false },
    { text: 'The principal can ignore the outside opportunity after offering pay.', correct: false }
  ]}
  explanation="Effort comparison is conditional on taking the work. Acceptance compares the best contract utility with what the agent can obtain by declining."
/>

An **outside option** is the agent's alternative to entering this arrangement. The **participation constraint (PC)** requires the best available contract utility to be at least as large as that option, written $\bar U$:

$$
\max(U_L,U_H)\ge\bar U
$$

Lean passes strictly: **22 > 10**. Flat also passes: **15 > 10**, although it induces routine work. The **Small** offer, paying **0** on failure and **20** on success, gives routine utility **8** and checking utility **16 − 18 = −2**. The best contract utility is **8 < 10**. Noor declines; the archive's realised decision is **no batch**, with value **zero**, rather than a profitable project using an imaginary willing worker.

Participation and incentives can move separately. Keep Lean's payments but raise the outside opportunity to **26**. Checking still beats routine, **22 > 20**, yet the contract is refused. Raising both payments by **5**, to failure **5** and success **55**, gives:

- Routine utility: **0.40 × 55 + 0.60 × 5 = 25**
- Checking utility: **0.80 × 55 + 0.20 × 5 − 18 = 27**

The difference remains **2** and acceptance becomes strict, **27 > 26**. In this risk-neutral model, adding the same fixed amount raises both utilities equally. Under risk aversion or a different set of actions, that shortcut needs re-examination.

At **exact equality** with the outside option, accepting and declining are both optimal in this model. The weak participation inequality establishes willingness in the usual weak sense; it does not select acceptance without a tie convention. Our chosen offers avoid that participation tie.

### When to use the separation

Use both checks when comparing delegated work arrangements. A proposal can have the desired marginal reward and still be unattractive overall. A generous fixed payment can attract someone while leaving the desired extra effort unrewarded.

## Rank the principal's returns and handle the threshold

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Two accepted contracts induce the same checking action and have the same success-payment spread. One adds a fixed payment in every outcome. What changes here?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Agent utility rises, while the principal pays more for the same response.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Checking necessarily becomes more likely because every payment is larger.' },
    { text: 'The higher fixed payment raises the principal’s output value by the same amount.' }
  ]}
  explanation="An added fixed payment cancels from the effort-utility difference in this model. If participation already held and effort is unchanged, it raises expected transfers without raising expected output."
/>

The archive's expected net return under the **chosen and accepted** effort is:

$$
\Pi_e=p_e\,120-\bigl[p_e w_S+(1-p_e)w_F\bigr]
$$

Lean induces checking, so expected gross value is **0.80 × 120 = 96**; expected transfer is **40**; archive return is **96 − 40 = 56 credits**. Noor's effort cost has already influenced Noor's choice. Subtracting it again from the archive's return would mix two accounting perspectives. To calculate joint surplus instead, subtract effort cost from gross value and let the transfer cancel between the parties.

| Offer | Failure pay | Success pay | Routine utility | Checking utility | Predicted response | Archive expected return |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flat | 15 | 15 | 15 | −3 | Accept; routine | 48 − 15 = 33 |
| Small | 0 | 20 | 8 | −2 | Decline | 0 |
| Edge | 0 | 45 | 18 | 18 | Accept; effort tied | 30 with routine or 60 with checking |
| Lean | 0 | 50 | 20 | 22 | Accept; checking | 96 − 40 = 56 |
| Padded | 20 | 70 | 40 | 42 | Accept; checking | 96 − 60 = 36 |

Among the listed offers with a **determinate response**, Lean yields the highest archive return. Edge cannot be given a single predicted return without deciding how the effort tie is resolved. If a model assumes ties go to the principal's preferred action, say so; that would change this comparison. We have not solved for an optimal contract over every possible payment schedule.

Why does Edge sit on a boundary? Write the success-payment spread as $b=w_S-w_F$. The checking advantage is:

$$
U_H-U_L=(0.80-0.40)b-18=0.40b-18
$$

Thus weak IC requires **b ≥ 45**. At **b = 45**, the advantage is **zero**. A spread **strictly greater than 45** gives a unique checking best response. The fixed payment cancels; paying more in every state is different from increasing this spread.

If the improvement were smaller or the cost higher, the same spread might no longer induce checking. A near-threshold calculation is only as reliable as those inputs.

## Read outcomes without pretending to observe effort

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Lean is offered and the batch fails. Does the failure show that the incentive calculation was wrong?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; failure remains possible even when checking is chosen.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; a uniquely optimal checking choice guarantees success.' },
    { text: 'Yes; the outcome reveals that routine work was chosen.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Checking improves a probability rather than fixing an outcome. The specified checking action still has a positive failure probability."
/>

Under Lean and the stated model, checking is uniquely best and participation is strict. Even then, **one in five** is the model's failure probability. On success, Noor receives **50**, incurs effort cost **18**, and has net **32**; the archive receives net **120 − 50 = 70**. On failure, Noor receives **zero**, still incurs effort cost **18**, and has net **−18**; the archive receives **zero**. Weight those outcomes and you recover agent utility **22** and archive return **56**. Neither expected value is the realised payment or return in either outcome.

Outside a model with fully trusted behavioural assumptions, a success alone cannot reveal effort because routine work could also succeed. Inferring hidden effort from results requires a relevant probabilistic model and additional evidence; it is not a mechanical identification of action. Repeated outcomes may help assess the model, but changed case mix, common disruptions, selection and changing contract terms can confound a simple before/after comparison.

Risk neutrality is an explicit simplification here. Risk-averse agents may evaluate the two payment distributions differently. Intrinsic motivation, additional tasks, manipulable quality checks, uncertain effort costs, limited enforceability and costly monitoring can also change the best response or participation. The qualitative trade-offs earlier in the course need their own assumptions; an illustrative interior peak is not a theorem about every hidden-action model.

### When to use the model

Use it when a delegated action is costly, hidden and affects a verifiable outcome probabilistically. Write down the alternatives, payment states and outside option before celebrating a bonus or blaming an agent. If the inputs are uncertain, investigate whether reasonable changes cross the incentive or participation boundary.

## Transfer the method to a projection kit

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Two offers have the same success-payment spread but different fixed payments. Can one be accepted and the other refused?"
  options={[

    { text: 'No; identical spreads force identical net utilities and acceptance decisions.' },
    { text: 'No; the principal’s output value alone determines whether the agent accepts.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Recall the two comparisons: payment differences affect effort incentives, while the utility level relative to the outside option determines participation."
/>

A **different constructed case**: a technician sets up a projection kit for one public talk. Success means passing the agreed projection check before the talk begins; otherwise the result is failure. Success is worth **100 credits** to the organiser; failure is worth **zero**. Keep the earlier risk-neutrality, observability, enforcement and tie assumptions, but use these new inputs:

| Action | Success probability | Failure probability | Effort cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Routine setup L | 0.50 | 0.50 | 0 |
| Extra test H | 0.75 | 0.25 | 10 |

The technician's outside option is **24 credits**. Offer **A** pays **0 on failure and 44 on success**. Offer **B** pays **2 on failure and 46 on success**. Predict acceptance, effort and organiser return before opening the solution.

  question="Which prediction follows for the projection-kit offers?"

    { text: 'Only A is accepted; its lower expected transfer makes it more attractive to the technician.' },

  explanation="Evaluate both effort utilities and compare the maximum with the outside option. The worked solution separates those steps and calculates the organiser’s return."

<details>
<summary>Check the utilities, threshold and organiser return</summary>

| Offer | Routine utility | Extra-test utility | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0.50 × 44 = 22 | 0.75 × 44 − 10 = 23 | Decline: 23 < 24 |
| B | 0.50 × 46 + 0.50 × 2 = 24 | 0.75 × 46 + 0.25 × 2 − 10 = 25 | Accept and test: 25 > 24 |

Under B, testing strictly beats routine by **1 credit**. Its expected payment is **0.75 × 46 + 0.25 × 2 = 35**. The organiser's expected return is **0.75 × 100 − 35 = 40 credits**. A is not an available working arrangement merely because its conditional action would also be testing.

Both spreads are **44**. Here the probability improvement is **0.25**, so the weak effort threshold is **10 ÷ 0.25 = 40**. The spread clears that threshold in both offers; only B clears participation. The main archive threshold must not be copied into a different task.

</details>
