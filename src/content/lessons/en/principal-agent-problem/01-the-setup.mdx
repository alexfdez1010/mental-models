An invoice can tell you what someone was paid. It cannot, by itself, tell you what they knew, which choices they had or why they acted. Before diagnosing a principal–agent problem, draw the relationship and read the payment rule. A job title is not a motive.

## Identify the delegation

A **principal** engages another party to act on their behalf and delegates some decision authority. The **agent** performs that delegated work. The same person can be an agent in one relationship and a principal in another. This contractual definition comes from [Jensen and Meckling's agency framework, section 1.4](https://josephmahoney.web.illinois.edu/BA549_Fall%202010/Session%205/Jensen_Meckling%20%281976%29.pdf).

Suppose a museum commissions Noor to catalogue one collection by Friday. Noor chooses how to check the records. The museum is the principal and Noor the agent. A visitor benefits from accurate records, but that benefit alone does not tell us that the visitor delegated authority to Noor.

Before predicting a response, fill in the actual arrangement:

| Question | What the constructed catalogue case specifies |
|---|---|
| Who delegates to whom? | The museum commissions Noor |
| Which choices are delegated? | Noor chooses the checking procedure |
| What counts as an outcome? | A submitted catalogue passes or fails an agreed quality check |
| What information is available? | Specify separately who sees the procedure and the result |
| What determines payment? | The contract must state its amount, conditions and payment date |
| What matters to each party? | The museum values usable records; Noor's preferences and costs still need specification |

A **principal–agent problem** concerns the difficulties of arranging delegated decisions when interests and information need not coincide. In the hidden-action model developed later, costly effort and limits on contracting create an incentive constraint. Identifying the roles starts the analysis; it does not establish dishonesty or a harmful choice.

A used-car seller can know more about a car than the buyer without being hired to choose on the buyer's behalf. That can be an asymmetric-information trade. To call it delegated agency, identify the additional mandate rather than letting the word “seller” do the work.

### When to use the map

Use it when several people benefit from, pay for or influence a decision. Separate those facts before assigning principal and agent roles.

## Define the payment before inferring the motive

A taxi meter and a fixed fare can reward a trip differently. Neither payment label is a complete theory of the driver's behaviour. We need the base, timing, available actions and everything else the driver values.

Here is a **constructed one-year fund-management contract**, used only to inspect accounting. At the start, an account contains **€500,000**. The contract fixes the fee at **1.5% of those opening assets**, paid **from the account at year-end**. There are no deposits, withdrawals, other charges or taxes. Returns below are gross, before that fee. This is a stipulated convention, not a claim about how every real asset-management fee is calculated.

**Fee = 0.015 × €500,000 = €7,500**

**Client's closing balance = opening assets × (1 + gross return) − fee**

| Gross return for this one year | Assets before year-end fee | Manager's fee | Client's balance after fee |
|---|---|---|---|
| +20% | €600,000 | €7,500 | €592,500 |
| 0% | €500,000 | €7,500 | €492,500 |
| −20% | €400,000 | €7,500 | €392,500 |

For the loss row, €500,000 × 0.80 = €400,000 before the charge; subtracting €7,500 leaves **€392,500**. Calling €400,000 the client's after-fee money would omit the payment. For the zero-return row, reconcile the whole amount below.

The fee is equal across these three rows. That establishes a feature of this payment rule, not that the manager ignores returns. Future assets, retention, reputation, professional duties, intrinsic preferences and effort costs are absent from this small ledger. To predict an action, specify the available actions and how the manager ranks their consequences. If the agent prefers a different action from the principal under the same circumstances, there is **incentive misalignment**. The contract's label alone does not prove it.

### When to compare another rule

Change the contract explicitly. If the fee were instead **1.5% of closing assets before the charge**, a +20% gross return would give a €9,000 fee: 0.015 × €600,000. The client's balance would be **€591,000**. The opening-assets calculation cannot be reused after changing the base. Nor does a return-sensitive fee establish complete alignment on risk, effort or every future consequence.

## Ask what is hidden, and from whom

A closed box can hide a fact someone knows, a choice someone makes, or a result nobody yet knows. Those are different information problems.

**Information asymmetry** means relevant knowledge is distributed unevenly. It does not mean the informed party is infallible or that the other side can never learn. **Hidden action** concerns an agent's choice that the principal cannot observe or verify for the relevant arrangement. **Hidden information** concerns a privately known fact, such as a skill, cost or task condition.

| Constructed event | What is hidden? | Timing in this example |
|---|---|---|
| An applicant knows their qualification does not meet the advertised requirement | A privately known characteristic | Before signing |
| A technician opens a casing and alone learns which internal fault is present | Newly acquired task information | After signing, before deciding what to do |
| A cataloguer skips a required check without recording it | An action | After signing |
| Neither party knows whether tomorrow's shared shipment will be delayed | No informational advantage is stipulated | Future uncertainty shared by both |

The familiar **adverse-selection** case involves private characteristics affecting which parties accept a deal. The familiar **moral-hazard** case involves hidden choices after agreement. That before/after comparison helps organise those cases; it does not define all information asymmetry. [Holmström's section 6](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/1979-holmstrom.pdf) explicitly allows the agent to learn new task information after the payment rule is fixed.

The technician's new knowledge is a fact, not an action just because it arrives late. A later choice about reporting it can introduce another issue. Likewise, being uncertain about the weather is not evidence that the other party secretly knows it.

### When to use the distinction

Write down the fact or action, who knows it and when they learn it. Then ask what evidence could change the information available. Cheap information can help; whether it resolves the decision depends on its reliability, coverage and use.

## Keep the diagnosis within its assumptions

Two questions are useful in the simple hidden-action model: **could the parties prefer different actions, and which relevant actions cannot be contracted on?** They are a diagnostic for that incentive problem, not an exhaustive classification of everything that can go wrong in cooperative work.

Distinguish three steps. An action may be **observable** to a person. It is **verifiable** for a contract if suitable evidence can establish it to the relevant enforcer under the model. Contractual **enforcement** then makes the promised consequence effective. A dashboard does not automatically provide all three. [MacKie-Mason's contracting notes, sections 2–5](https://open.umich.edu/sites/default/files/downloads/MacKie-Mason-SI680-w08-class3-LectureNotes-asymmetric-contracting-model_0.pdf) separate these constraints and the agent's willingness to participate.

Suppose a taxi fare rewards extra distance. Now stipulate an agreed route, independently verifiable detours and an enforceable deduction greater than the extra fare each detour earns. Under those terms, the detour is no longer the more profitable choice on that payment comparison. Merely displaying a GPS route would not establish the deduction or its enforceability.

| Stated assumptions about this task | What follows about this incentive problem? |
|---|---|
| Relevant actions fully verifiable and enforceable | Terms can condition on those actions; that hidden-action constraint is absent |
| Only success or failure is verifiable, and either effort can produce either | The outcome alone does not identify effort |
| Parties rank the relevant actions identically | That conflict of desired action is absent; error and limited ability can remain |
| Interests may diverge and relevant action is hidden | Compare incentives, information and feasible contract terms before predicting conduct |

Perfectly aligned goals do not give an agent perfect knowledge, competence or control over luck. A relative, trusted colleague or mission-driven employee is not aligned by definition. Conversely, a hidden action need not produce a bad outcome: a specified payment can make careful work worthwhile, as the [later contract workshop](/principal-agent-problem/07-compare-hidden-effort-contracts) calculates.

An owner doing their own task has no separate agent for that task. They can still face uncertainty or delegate other tasks. Removing one relationship does not remove every problem in the organisation.

### When an outcome is informative

A passed check can be useful evidence without being proof of every underlying action. If both careful and routine work could pass, specify the probabilistic relationship before inferring effort. Measurement, monitoring and participation can also have costs even when they improve a contract.

## Count delegation links, not job titles

A relay has runners and handovers. Counting the runners does not count the handovers. An **agency chain** is a sequence in which an agent delegates a further task and becomes principal for the next relationship.

Take this **constructed organisation chart**: shareholders → board → chief executive → division manager → worker. There are **five parties and four directed delegation links**. At each link we would inspect the actual authority, information and incentives; the diagram alone establishes no hidden gap or loss.

| Link | Principal in that relationship | Agent | Stated delegated responsibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shareholders | Board | Oversee the organisation under its stipulated mandate |
| 2 | Board | Chief executive | Choose and execute an operating plan |
| 3 | Chief executive | Division manager | Organise the division's work |
| 4 | Division manager | Worker | Choose the assigned task procedure |

A chief executive is an agent of the board and a principal for the division manager in this chart. Those roles do not require a guess about whether the executive wants an empire, whether the board wants easy meetings or whether workers dislike effort.

Now suppose the shareholders separately hire an auditor to inspect specified records. That adds **one party and one delegation link**: shareholders → auditor. The expanded map has **six parties and five delegation links**. The auditor's report may inform oversight without revealing every hidden action.

The auditor is another agent whose mandate, incentives and evidence deserve inspection. It does not follow that a further auditor must always be hired, or that oversight cannot work. That is a decision about the benefit and cost of a particular information arrangement, not an infinite chain forced by the word “monitor”.

### When to trace a chain

Use the link map to locate a missing authority, conflicting incentive or unavailable piece of evidence. Identify the affected link rather than assuming every intermediary subtracts the same amount of value.

## Distinguish several principals from several stakeholders

**Common agency** concerns several principals influencing the same agent's decisions. In **delegated common agency**, several parties give that agent decision authority. Their objectives can conflict, but conflict, manipulation or serving the loudest are not guaranteed by the headcount.

[The original common-agency model](https://bernheim.people.stanford.edu/publications/common-agency) also discusses **intrinsic common agency**: a decision maker already controls an activity and several parties can shape its incentives, including through specified regulatory powers. Thus voluntary hiring is not the only formal setting. For either use, specify the rights or incentive mechanisms that make the parties principals. A list of people affected by an outcome is insufficient.

Try a different constructed map. **North Museum** and **South Museum** each commission the same editor, Lina, to choose how to prepare their respective records for a shared catalogue. Each museum authorises work on its records and controls acceptance and payment under its stated commission. Lina delegates proofreading decisions to **Sam**. **A visitor** reads the published catalogue and has no commissioning, delegated-authority or incentive-setting role in this example.

The museums might prefer different priorities for a limited editing day. To predict Lina's allocation, we would still need the feasible actions, payment terms, costs and preferences. The map alone does not select a winner in a tug-of-war.

### When to use this distinction

Use it when one worker, office or representative faces several mandates or incentive setters. Ask what each can actually request, observe and reward. A common agent may coordinate tasks well, face conflicting demands or decline an arrangement; explaining any response requires more than counting principals.
