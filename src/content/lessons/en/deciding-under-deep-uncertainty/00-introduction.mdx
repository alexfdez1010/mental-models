An event team can prepare one check-in plan. One plan has the smallest worst outage; another stays closest to what the team could have achieved in each condition. Both calculations can be correct. They answer different questions.

This course is about choosing when the model, probabilities or priorities are uncertain or disputed. You still need assumptions. The job is to expose them and test which ones change the decision.

## What do you actually know?

**Risk**, in the distinction used here, concerns uncertainty described by a sufficiently specified probability model: a fair die has six equally likely faces. **Deep uncertainty** arises when the parties cannot confidently agree on the model, the probabilities, or how to value the outcomes. It need not mean that no numerical assumption can ever be useful.

An expected-value calculation is conditional on its probabilities and outcome values. If those inputs are uncertain, show alternatives and sensitivity rather than presenting the output as a measured forecast. More spreadsheet tabs do not validate the inputs.

## Declare the decision before ranking plans

A **scenario** is a stipulated condition used to test a plan. A **feasible plan** meets the constraints you have declared. An **objective** tells you what to improve: for example, minimize the largest service outage in the catalogue. A separate requirement might reject every plan with more than thirty-five unavailable minutes in any included condition.

**Robustness** means satisfactory performance across a specified range of conditions, according to stated criteria. You must say what counts as satisfactory and which conditions were examined. A plan can be robust in that analysis and fail in an omitted condition.

Optimization is not restricted to one guessed future. Minimizing the worst listed cost and minimizing the worst hindsight gap are both optimizations. They can favor different feasible plans. You will calculate that difference rather than choosing a winner from its reassuring label.

## Six lessons, then the exam

1. **Risk vs. Uncertainty** introduces the distinction between specified odds and disputed unknowns.
2. **When Expected Value Lies** examines the limits of averages, tail outcomes and repeated paths.
3. **Robustness, Objectives and Feasible Plans** distinguishes acceptability requirements, worst-cost and worst-regret objectives.
4. **Buying Back Uncertainty** introduces reversibility, slack, experiments and scenario tools, whose usefulness depends on their costs and conditions.
5. **The Deep-Uncertainty Playbook** brings those questions into a decision routine.
6. **Audit a Scenario Catalogue** works through a service-desk table, a preparation limit, an omitted joint condition and an independent transfer.

The final exam follows the workshop. Answers lock when submitted during the exam; the score appears at the end.
