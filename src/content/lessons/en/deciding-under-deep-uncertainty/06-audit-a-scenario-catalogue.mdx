This is a **constructed case**, with fixed outcomes by assumption. One plan is selected before a four-hour service window. Its **cost** is total minutes when the check-in service is unavailable during those 240 minutes. Smaller is better. None of the cells estimates the probability of a condition or explains its cause.

A single worker prepares the plan before opening. Only **120 worker-minutes** are available initially. A/B/C require 80/100/120. Each is a complete alternative; mixing plans and changing plans after the condition becomes known are excluded. Preparation is a resource constraint; it is not another service outage to add to the cost column.

The initial catalogue contains three conditions. It deliberately leaves out high load combined with a failed link.

| Plan | Preparation in worker-minutes | Normal load, link working | High load, link working | Normal load, link failed |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 80 | 10 | 30 | 40 |
| B | 100 | 15 | 20 | 30 |
| C | 120 | 0 | 35 | 35 |

The last three columns are unavailable-service minutes. Choose a criterion before ranking: minimize the largest original cost, or minimize the largest hindsight opportunity gap. They describe different priorities.

## Calculate both objectives completely

For **minimax cost**, first take the largest cost in each row: A has 40, B 30 and C 35. Then minimize those maxima. **B wins with a worst cost of 30 minutes** in this catalogue. This rule does not predict that B's thirty-minute condition will occur.

For **minimax regret**, find the best cost a feasible plan could achieve separately in each condition. The first column's minimum is C's 0; the second is B's 20; the third is B's 30. Subtract those benchmarks from every plan's costs:

| Plan | Normal gap | High-load gap | Failed-link gap | Largest gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 10 − 0 = 10 | 30 − 20 = 10 | 40 − 30 = 10 | 10 |
| B | 15 − 0 = 15 | 20 − 20 = 0 | 30 − 30 = 0 | 15 |
| C | 0 − 0 = 0 | 35 − 20 = 15 | 35 − 30 = 5 | 15 |

**A wins minimax regret with a largest gap of 10 minutes.** Its original worst cost is still 40. The gap is not an extra charge added to those forty minutes, and “regret” here is not a measurement of emotion.

If `D` is the feasible plan menu, `S` the included conditions and `L(a,s)` a plan's cost, the scenario benchmark is `m(s;D) = min L(a,s)` over plans in `D`. Regret is `R(a,s;D) = L(a,s) − m(s;D)`. For each plan in `D`, minimax cost takes `max L(a,s)` over the conditions in `S`, then selects a plan with the smallest such maximum. Minimax regret makes the same comparison using `max R(a,s;D)`. These definitions require nonempty `D` and `S`.

The benchmark may use a different plan in each column. It is a hindsight comparison: the team must still choose **one plan before** it knows the condition. No feasible plan can deliver the composite 0/20/30 row by magically switching after the fact.

## Change the budget and rebuild the benchmark

Now only **110 worker-minutes** are available. A and B remain feasible; C needs 120 and must leave the comparison. Its zero in the normal condition cannot remain the feasible benchmark.

The new column minima are **10/20/30**. A's gaps become **0/10/10**, with maximum 10. B's become **5/0/0**, with maximum 5. **B now minimizes worst regret as well as worst cost.** C was not the previous minimax-regret winner, yet removing it changed the result because it had defined a column benchmark.

Keeping the old 0/20/30 comparator after excluding C would answer a different question: how plans compare with an alternative the team cannot prepare. Use budget 110 in the inspector and check the benchmark row.

At budget **80**, only A is feasible. Against itself it has zero regret in every included condition, even though its original cost can be substantial. At budget **79**, no plan is feasible. There is no comparator or minimizer to report. “No feasible plan” is a useful result; a reassuring zero would be an arithmetic fiction.

### When to use this check

Recompute the benchmark whenever staffing, permissions, time or another declared constraint changes the menu. Do not confuse an infeasible alternative with a forgone feasible opportunity.

## Add the joint condition

The original catalogue tested high load and link failure separately. It did not test them together. Add the stipulated fourth condition:

| Plan | High load and failed link | Worst cost across all four | Worst regret across all four |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 60 | 60 | 10 |
| B | 90 | 90 | 30 |
| C | 70 | 70 | 15 |

Here the fourth benchmark is 60. The new gaps are A0/B30/C10. With budget 120, **A now minimizes both criteria**: its worst cost is 60 and worst regret 10. The worst-cost recommendation changed from B to A. This is sensitivity to a newly included condition, not evidence that the joint condition is likely or that A will prevent all outages.

The fourth column's costs are assumptions, just like the first three. A real team would need evidence for them and a process for questioning omitted conditions. Four columns do not exhaust all futures.

## A catalogue count is not a chance

Use **35 unavailable minutes** as a descriptive test, not yet a filter. In the initial catalogue A passes in 2/3 entries; B and C pass in 3/3. In the expanded catalogue A passes in 2/4; B and C pass in 3/4. The inspector still applies only the preparation budget: it does not silently enforce this separate test.

Now duplicate the normal condition in the initial catalogue. A passes that duplicate too, so its descriptive fraction rises from 2/3 to 3/4. No new physical condition or probability evidence has appeared.

A catalogue's unweighted mean also gives each entry a vote. Original means for A/B/C are **80/3, 65/3, 70/3** minutes; B has the lowest. Duplicating the normal entry gives **90/4, 80/4, 70/4**, so C has the lowest. These are valid averages of the specified lists. Calling them expected future costs requires a justified probability-weight model; repeating an entry does not supply one. Neither maximum-cost nor maximum-regret rankings change merely because an identical condition is repeated.

If the 35-minute ceiling is instead a **hard requirement in every included condition**, apply it to feasibility before ranking. B/C meet it in the initial set; A does not. In all four conditions, **none** meets it. A descriptive count, a mandatory requirement and an objective are three different roles for a number.

### Sources and limits

[Anderson and Zachary’s 2022 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.01420v1) defines minimax costs and feasible-menu regret, and examines sensitivity to alternatives and scenarios. [RAND’s guide](https://www.rand.org/pubs/tools/TL320/tool/robust-decision-making.html) explains framing, vulnerability analysis and why counts of successful futures are not probabilities. The [Green Book 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-green-book--2/the-green-book-2026) supports explicit objectives, feasibility and sensitivity analysis. These sources do not provide the constructed service costs. This finite exercise does not certify a real intervention or cover every future.
