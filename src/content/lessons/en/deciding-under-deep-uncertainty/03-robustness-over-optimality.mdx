A plan can have the smallest worst outage without having the smallest worst hindsight gap. Before calling one “robust”, say which result you want and what you are willing to rule out.

## Acceptability and optimization

An **optimal** plan minimizes or maximizes a specified objective over feasible alternatives. That objective can involve one scenario, a probability-weighted expectation, or the worst result across several scenarios. Optimization does not, by itself, imply a forecast or a fragile choice.

A **robust** plan performs acceptably across a specified range of conditions. “Acceptably” needs a test: an outage ceiling, a minimum output, or another stated requirement. Passing the test within a catalogue does not certify every future condition. A plan can also be optimal for a declared robustness criterion.

For example, if service must be unavailable for at most thirty-five minutes in **every listed condition**, a plan with costs ten/thirty/forty fails because forty exceeds the ceiling. A smaller average does not repair the failure. The average here is simply (10 + 30 + 40)/3 = 80/3, about 26.7 minutes; the requirement concerns each condition, not that mean.

## Two objectives on one table

Consider a constructed four-hour event service. Plans A/B/C need 80/100/120 worker-minutes of preparation before opening; all are available under a 120-minute preparation budget. Their service costs are unavailable-service minutes during the 240-minute window. Preparation and service cost describe different resources and are not added.

In the initial three conditions, A costs **10/30/40**, B **15/20/30**, and C **0/35/35**. Smaller cost is better. **Minimax cost** chooses the plan with the smallest largest cost: A's maximum is 40, B's 30 and C's 35, so B wins this objective.

**Regret**, in this calculation, is a numerical gap: a plan's cost minus the smallest cost achievable by a feasible plan in that same condition. It is an opportunity-cost comparison, not measured distress. The feasible column minima are **0/20/30**. A's gaps are **10/10/10**; B's are **15/0/0**; C's are **0/15/5**. **Minimax regret** minimizes the largest gap, selecting A with 10 rather than B/C with 15.

## Requirements are choices too

A decision maker may declare a consequence unacceptable and exclude plans that produce it under the evaluated conditions. That is a **hard constraint**. It should specify the consequence, the conditions and the evidence used to assess it. If no plan meets the constraint, report infeasibility and reconsider the plan menu or requirement; a ranking rule cannot manufacture a passing option.

Irreversible consequences can justify strict requirements in a particular decision. They do not make a finite test a proof against all unmodeled harm, or establish everyone's preferences automatically. A low regret score can coexist with a bad outcome: when only one plan is available, it has zero regret against itself regardless of its original cost.

**Satisficing** means taking an option that meets a declared aspiration or acceptability level rather than continuing to seek the highest score. A first passing option is satisfactory **for that test**. Its broader robustness depends on what the test and catalogue cover, while the cost of further search is another consideration.

## Where the analysis can break

Changing the scenario set can change either recommendation. Removing a feasible alternative can change a regret benchmark even if that alternative was not selected. Neither rule needs scenario probabilities; both still depend on assumptions about costs, constraints and coverage.

Minimizing the worst cost is not a prediction that the worst condition will occur. It is a declared objective. Minimizing regret is not a guarantee of a balanced portfolio or an acceptable original cost. Comparing their trade-offs requires the actual table and the decision maker's priorities.

<details>
<summary>Why can removing an unchosen alternative change regret?</summary>

The removed plan might have set the lowest cost in a column without minimizing the worst gap overall. Removing it raises that column's feasible benchmark, which can change other plans' gaps unequally. The final workshop computes the change rather than treating the original benchmark as permanent.
