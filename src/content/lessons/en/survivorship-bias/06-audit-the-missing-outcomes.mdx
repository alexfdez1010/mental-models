A spreadsheet course advertises that **80% of its completers passed an independent practical check**. The director wants to replace that headline with “at least 70% of everyone who started passed”. You have the enrolment register, some assessment records and a limited audit budget.

Finding the denominator is only the beginning. Some outcomes are still missing. This workshop shows how to make a defensible decision while those gaps remain, without quietly turning “unknown” into “failed” or “probably passed”. All people, counts and decisions below are **invented teaching data**.

## Fix the question before counting

Treat the enrolment register like a guest list: it identifies who belongs in the count even when some seats in the results table are still empty. A **cohort** is this fixed starting group, followed through a stated period.

Our register contains **200 distinct starters on day zero**. Nobody is added later or counted twice. The outcome is **passing the same independent spreadsheet check by day 60**, using a fixed pass rule. Course completion is a separate status. For this constructed case, every starter took that check; its pass or non-pass outcome exists even when the administrator cannot yet match the assessment record to the register. Assume that every successfully matched label is correct.

The administrator initially has these records:

| Course status | Passes verified | Non-passes verified | Outcome not yet matched | People |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Completed the course | 96 | 24 | 0 | 120 |
| Left the course, record matched | 20 | 20 | 0 | 40 |
| Left the course, record unresolved | 0 | 0 | 40 | 40 |
| **Starting cohort** | **116** | **44** | **40** | **200** |

The advertised completer rate is **96 ÷ 120 = 80%**. Among all people with matched outcomes, the rate is **116 ÷ 160 = 72.5%**. Neither calculation is the full-cohort rate: the first excludes all leavers; the second still excludes the unresolved records.

The chart's **7.5 percentage-point gap** compares two selected denominators. It is not an estimate of how much finishing the course helps. People might leave because they struggle, have already learned enough, move away or lack time. Those possibilities need evidence; the word “leaver” does not supply their assessment result.

### When to use this setup

Write down the population, outcome, deadline and observation rule whenever a dashboard silently switches from “everyone enrolled” to “graduates”, “reachable users” or “records successfully linked”. A complete register secures the denominator. It does not fill the missing cells.

## Bound the answer without inventing the missing labels

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="What is a defensible first move when the roster is complete but some binary outcomes are missing?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Assign every missing outcome the observed completer rate without stating an assumption.' },
    { text: 'Calculate the lowest and highest rates compatible with the known counts.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Delete the unresolved people from the target population.' }
  ]}
  explanation="A binary outcome has only two possibilities. Assigning all unknowns to each extreme in turn gives logical limits without pretending either extreme is the actual result."
/>

There are already **116 verified passes**. At one extreme none of the 40 unresolved people passed; at the other, all 40 passed. Therefore:

- **Lower limit:** 116 ÷ 200 = **58%**
- **Upper limit:** (116 + 40) ÷ 200 = **78%**

These are **logical bounds**: limits on the rates still possible for these fixed 200 people. They assume the roster and known labels are correct and the outcome has the same meaning for everyone. Every whole-number pass total from 116 to 156 is compatible with the records. The endpoints do not say which total is likely.

Calling all missing outcomes non-passes gives a valid **lower bound** here. Calling it the measured full-cohort rate would claim more than the records show. Equally, the upper bound is not an optimistic forecast. This approach follows the idea behind **partial identification**: the evidence can restrict an answer to a set rather than identify one number. [Manski's account of missing data and partial identification](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijar.2004.10.006)

The range is **20 percentage points wide**, exactly the missing share: **40 ÷ 200 = 20%**. That width describes how much these unresolved labels could change this binary rate. It does not measure bias in the completer headline, and it does not give uncertainty about a future cohort.

### What the director can conclude now

The proposed 70% threshold requires **0.70 × 200 = 140 passes**. There are 116 confirmed, so **24 of the 40 unresolved people** would have to be passes to meet it. Both meeting and missing that threshold remain possible. The data support “between 58% and 78% of this cohort passed”, not the proposed headline.

## Recover records and update the limits

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="The auditor recovers some missing records. What should happen to the logical range if earlier counts remain correct?"
  options={[
    { text: 'It must narrow; which endpoint moves depends on the recovered labels.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Its lower and upper limits must both rise.' },
    { text: 'The recovered records prove that all remaining outcomes match them.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Every recovered binary label removes an unresolved possibility. A verified pass raises the minimum; a verified non-pass lowers the maximum. Neither outcome tells us the labels of the remaining people."
/>

The auditor selects **20 of the 40 unresolved IDs by simple random sampling without replacement**: every subset of that size has the same chance of selection. All 20 selected records are successfully matched. The audit recovers **12 passes and 8 non-passes**, recorded under the original day-60 rule.

No one retakes the check in the audit. A new success on day 90 would answer another question. The auditor retrieves the original outcomes, uses the same matching standard for everyone selected and does not replace difficult IDs with convenient volunteers.

| Audit stage | Verified passes | Verified non-passes | Unresolved | Fixed total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before recovery | 116 | 44 | 40 | 200 |
| After recovery | 128 | 52 | 20 | 200 |

The new minimum is **(116 + 12) ÷ 200 = 128 ÷ 200 = 64%**. The new maximum is **(128 + 20) ÷ 200 = 148 ÷ 200 = 74%**. Recovering the passes raised the lower bound by six percentage points; recovering the non-passes lowered the upper bound by four. The range has narrowed to **10 percentage points**.

### Where random selection matters

The updated **bounds** use the verified counts and remain valid even if records were recovered in a convenient order. Random selection becomes important when we want the recovered people to **represent those not recovered**. That stronger step needs its own reasoning.

## Separate an extrapolated estimate from verified totals

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="The recovered sample has a known pass fraction. Does random sampling make that fraction exactly equal to the fraction among all originally unresolved records?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Yes, a random sample must be a miniature copy.' },
    { text: 'No, it supports estimation but leaves sampling uncertainty.', correct: true },
    { text: 'No, random samples cannot inform any missing-data question.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Random selection makes samples representative in expectation over repeated selections, not identical to the whole missing group in this particular selection."
/>

A **point estimate** summarizes an unknown quantity with one estimated number. Among the recovered 20, **12 ÷ 20 = 60%** passed. Under the stated equal-chance sampling and complete recovery of selected outcomes, use that fraction to estimate the pass count among the **original 40 unresolved people**:

**Estimated passes in the original unresolved group = 40 × (12 ÷ 20) = 24**

Add the 116 passes known **before** follow-up: **116 + 24 = 140 estimated passes**, or **140 ÷ 200 = 70%** of the cohort.

  ]}
  rebuiltLabel="Selected contributions to estimated passes"
  valuePrefix=""
  valueSuffix=" people"

  verdictTemplate="The estimated total is {total}. This is not the verified pass count."
  caption="An estimated contribution requires the stated sampling design. The separate logical bounds still describe the compatible actual totals."

Each recovered person has a sampling weight of **40 ÷ 20 = 2** for that original group. This is the same design principle used in follow-up subsampling: selected cases represent more than themselves. [NORC's report on subsampling, pp 1–2, 6 and 8](https://gss.norc.org/Documents/reports/methodological-reports/MR106.pdf)

Each original unresolved ID has an inclusion probability of **20 ÷ 40 = 1/2**. Across repeated random audits, an actual passer therefore contributes an average of **(1/2) × 2 = 1 estimated pass**; an actual non-passer contributes zero. This explains the weighting, while a particular sample can still have a different pass fraction from its source group.

Avoid two tempting shortcuts. **128 ÷ 180 ≈ 71.1%** simply pools everyone now observed, without giving the follow-up its proper representation. **128 + 24 = 152** counts the recovered passes twice: once in 128 and again inside the estimate for all 40. An equivalent correct calculation is **128 + 20 × 60% = 140**, estimating only the still-unresolved group in the second term.

### Conditions for using the estimate

The sample was random among all unresolved IDs, every selected outcome was recovered accurately, and the outcome definition stayed fixed. If only the easiest or most enthusiastic selected people provide records, the observed subset has another selection filter. Random invitations alone do not fix missing results after invitation. Sampling variability remains even when the plan is followed; the sample fraction does not reveal the actual last 20 labels.

Do not silently choose whichever figure suits the brochure. Report **128 verified passes, 20 unresolved outcomes, a compatible range of 64–74%, and a design-based estimate of 70% with sampling uncertainty**. AAPOR likewise warns that a response rate alone does not determine the amount or even the presence of nonresponse error. [AAPOR Standard Definitions](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/standard-definitions/)

## Turn the target into a record-recovery decision

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="The estimate lands exactly on the director's target, but the logical range crosses it. What can establish the fixed-cohort claim without extrapolating the remaining labels?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Recover enough original records to put the whole compatible range on one side of the target.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Round the estimate upward.' },
    { text: 'Publish the completer rate as the full-cohort rate.' }
  ]}
  explanation="A threshold can sometimes be settled before every label is known. The lower bound can reach it, or the upper bound can fall below it."
/>

The threshold is **at least 140 passes among 200 starters**. After follow-up, 128 passes and 52 non-passes are verified. There are two exact stopping conditions:

| What further recovery finds | Why it settles the threshold claim |
|---|---|
| **12 additional passes** | Verified passes reach 128 + 12 = **140**. Even if every remaining unknown is a non-pass, the cohort meets 70%. |
| **9 additional non-passes** | Verified non-passes reach 52 + 9 = **61**. At most 200 − 61 = **139** can have passed, so the cohort is below 70%. |

Finding eight more non-passes would not rule out the claim: **200 − 60 = 140** could still pass. The boundary is inclusive, so equality meets “at least”. Fix that wording before running the audit.

### Why this stopping rule is limited

It is exact accounting for a fixed cohort; it does not establish a rate for next year's learners. Choosing easy records first can still tighten these logical bounds if their labels are accurate, though it would not justify a representative point estimate. If the budget ends before either condition is met, publish the unresolved range. Do not turn an audit deadline into a missing-data assumption.

Nor does a complete cohort prove the course **caused** the passes. That causal question needs a suitable alternative to taking the course and a design that supports the comparison. The audit answers how many of these starters passed under the stated rule.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The counts and stopping conditions">
The lower bound is **32 ÷ 50 = 64%** and the upper bound is **(32 + 8) ÷ 50 = 80%**. A 75% target requires **at least 38 successes**, because 75% of 50 is 37.5 and a person cannot contribute half an outcome.

Six more verified successes would bring the lower count to **38** and confirm the target. Three more verified non-successes would bring that count to **13**, leaving at most **50 − 13 = 37 successes**, and rule it out. Both conclusions use the original day-30 event, not later merges.

### When the workshop is useful

Use it when a fixed roster and comparable binary outcomes exist but some outcomes are unobserved. For averages, other outcome ranges or an incomplete starting register, the calculations need to change. A percentage among survivors remains a valid description of those survivors if labelled accurately; the error is promoting it to a different population or a causal claim.
