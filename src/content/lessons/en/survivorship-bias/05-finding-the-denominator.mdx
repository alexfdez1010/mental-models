A repair course puts a confident sentence on its poster: “Three quarters of the people who passed used our optional practice sessions.” You want to know whether taking those sessions improves a new participant's chance of passing. The poster answers a question. It may not answer yours.

The practical defence against **survivorship bias** starts by specifying which people and outcomes belong in the claim. Recovering overlooked records can change the answer, but missing people need not have done worse. Even a complete table does not automatically explain why groups differ.

All course, survey and prototype counts below are **constructed teaching data**, not results from actual programmes.

## Name the population and the question

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest

    { text: 'The passing rate of everyone who attended optional practice.' },
    { text: 'The increase in passing caused by offering optional practice.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The denominator is the people who passed. The percentage can describe that group accurately while leaving the passing rate among all practice attendees unanswered."

First pin down four parts of the question:

| Part | Definition | In this case |
|---|---|---|
| Target population | The people or cases the claim is meant to describe | All 300 participants enrolled when this course began |
| Outcome | The result counted, with a consistent measurement rule | Meeting all five checks on the published repair-skills rubric in a 20-minute independent assessment |
| Observation window | When that result is measured | The assessment on day 30 after the course began |
| Denominator | All eligible cases for the particular rate being calculated | For a passing rate among practice attendees, all practice attendees in that starting population |

A **cohort** is a group defined at a starting point and followed forward. Here, attending practice means taking at least one optional 30-minute session during days 1–20, before the assessment. Assume the day-30 assessment result is available for **every starter**, including participants who left the course. People chose whether to practise; they were not assigned by lottery.

Here is the complete **two-by-two table**: practice or no practice, crossed with met or did not meet the standard.

| Recorded participation | Met the standard | Did not meet it | Total participants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attended optional practice | 120 | 80 | 200 |
| Did not attend optional practice | 40 | 60 | 100 |
| Total | 160 | 140 | 300 |

The poster calculates **120 ÷ 160 = 75%**: practice attendees among the people who met the standard. That is a real percentage with a real denominator. Calling it “only a numerator” would be wrong. Its limitation is the **selected denominator**, not the absence of one.

Your question needs different divisions:

- Met the standard **among practice attendees**: **120 ÷ 200 = 60%**
- Met the standard **among non-attendees**: **40 ÷ 100 = 40%**
- Difference between those recorded rates: **60% − 40% = 20 percentage points**

A percentage point is the difference between percentages on the same scale. Twenty percentage points here is not a claim of a 20% relative increase, nor yet a causal effect. An **association** is an observed relationship: here, outcome frequency differs between groups. A **causal effect** concerns the change produced by the practice offer or participation, rather than merely a difference between people who chose differently.

### When to use this definition step

Use it whenever a claim moves from “the successful people did this” to “doing this produces success.” Write the event and denominator in words before calculating. Keep the same population, measurement rule and window when comparing groups.

## Keep departures separate from outcomes

A current class register is a seating plan, not a history of everyone who enrolled. If names disappear when people leave, it can silently change the population behind a rate.

Keep a starting register with stable IDs. Record later participation status separately from the measured outcome. A participant might leave because the course was too difficult, because their work schedule changed or because an attractive opportunity took them elsewhere. Those stories suggest different missing outcomes. None is established simply by a missing name.

For the 300-person case, the table is complete because we **assumed the independent assessment was obtained for everyone**. Having 300 enrolment IDs alone would not make that true. A real ledger needs the assessment result, its date and source, or an explicit unresolved status for each eligible person.

| Record | What it establishes | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Left the course; independent day-30 assessment met the standard | A verified positive outcome for a starter | Why the person left |
| Still enrolled; no assessment result can be found | Membership in the starting population | Whether the standard was met |
| Left the course; no assessment result can be found | A departure from participation | Whether the standard was met |

If the outcome had instead been **“attended and passed the course's day-30 session,”** documented absence could count as not meeting that outcome. That is a different question from skill demonstrated in an independent assessment. Declare the rule before looking at results; do not turn missing skill measurements into negative outcomes after the fact.

### When the direction is unknown

Reach for this distinction when records are deleted, people stop replying or a source contains only current members. Investigate the reasons for selection. If outcomes remain missing, say what is missing and narrow the conclusion. Do not assume the missing cases are worse, better or identical to those observed.

## Investigate why data are missing

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="A feedback survey obtains replies from 95% of its starting list. What can that rate alone establish?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Most listed people replied; the missing answers may still matter.', correct: true },
    { text: 'The respondent answers fairly represent every relevant subgroup.' },
    { text: 'The remaining people must hold less favourable views of the course.' }
  ]}
  explanation="A response rate describes completeness. It does not identify who is missing, what they would have said or whether the difference matters for the question."
/>

Consider a **separate feedback survey of 100 people**, not the 300-person skills cohort. It remains open for seven days after a session; we count replies received within that window. Its list includes 90 returning participants and ten newcomers.

| Survey group | Replied | Did not reply | Starting list | Response rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Returning participants | 90 | 0 | 90 | 90 ÷ 90 = 100% |
| Newcomers | 5 | 5 | 10 | 5 ÷ 10 = 50% |
| Everyone | 95 | 5 | 100 | 95 ÷ 100 = 95% |

The five missing people are only **5% of the whole list**, but **half of the newcomers**. The survey's 95% overall response does not guarantee a good account of newcomers' experiences. It also does not prove their answers would differ. Their answers are unknown.

  client:visible
  title="Overall completeness can hide a subgroup gap"
  scenarios={[
    { label: 'Response to the separate feedback survey', groups: [
      { label: 'Returning participants who replied', events: 90, total: 90 },

AAPOR's survey standards distinguish the response rate from nonresponse error: the rate alone cannot establish whether that error exists or how large it is. Relevant differences between respondents and nonrespondents matter. [AAPOR Standard Definitions](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/standard-definitions/)

A **missingness mechanism** is the process that determines which measurements become unavailable. Compare these hypothetical mechanisms; the response count does not tell you which occurred.

| Possible mechanism | Example | Consequence for inference |
|---|---|---|
| Independent of people's profiles and answers | A fair lottery selects files lost in a storage fault | The remaining average can be representative across repeated lotteries; one realised subset can still differ by chance |
| Depends on a recorded profile | Evening attendees reply less often; within each time slot, response does not further depend on the answer | Adjustments using that profile may be justified if this assumption holds and each needed group has observed data; the unadjusted average is not automatically representative |
| Depends on the unseen answer even within recorded profiles | People with especially disappointing experiences are less likely to answer | Observed profiles alone do not remove the missing-outcome dependence |

These distinctions concern assumptions about the process, rather than a percentage cutoff. Rubin's original missing-data analysis formalizes why the conditions for ignoring a missingness process matter. [Rubin, Inference and missing data](https://people.csail.mit.edu/jrennie/trg/papers/rubin-missing-76.pdf)

### What to investigate before adjusting

Inspect contact procedures, reasons recorded for nonresponse and profiles known for the whole starting list. Follow-up can reveal outcomes or challenge a proposed mechanism. Matching respondent profiles to the list is useful evidence, but cannot prove that missing answers are identical within those profiles. State the assumption behind any extrapolation. The next workshop develops calculations when outcomes remain unresolved.

## Separate a complete comparison from a causal explanation

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="Return to the complete skills cohort: 60% of practice attendees met the standard, versus 40% of non-attendees. What does complete follow-up settle?"
  options={[
    { text: 'The recorded rates for these groups, with no missing outcomes.', correct: true },
    { text: 'That the optional sessions caused the full 20-point difference.' },
    { text: 'That prior experience cannot explain any part of the difference.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Completeness addresses missing outcomes in this cohort. Participants still chose whether to practise, and pre-existing differences can affect both that choice and the assessment."
/>

A **comparison group** supplies a reference group for a question. It is defined here by not attending optional practice, not by doing badly. Forty non-attendees met the standard; eighty attendees did not. Calling the comparison group “the failures” would confuse the group definition with the outcome.

A **confounder** is a pre-existing factor that influences both the choice being compared and the outcome, which can produce a misleading causal explanation. Prior repair experience is a plausible example: it may influence both who takes optional practice and who passes the assessment.

Here is an expanded version of the **same constructed cohort**, with experience recorded before the course. All four groups have complete outcome counts; none are estimated from missing records.

| Prior experience | Practice | Met standard | Did not meet it | Total | Met-standard rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Experienced | Attended | 112 | 48 | 160 | 112 ÷ 160 = 70% |
| Experienced | Did not attend | 28 | 12 | 40 | 28 ÷ 40 = 70% |
| New to repair | Attended | 8 | 32 | 40 | 8 ÷ 40 = 20% |
| New to repair | Did not attend | 12 | 48 | 60 | 12 ÷ 60 = 20% |

Rebuild the original rows:

- Practice attendees: **112 + 8 = 120** met the standard, out of **160 + 40 = 200**, giving **60%**
- Non-attendees: **28 + 12 = 40**, out of **40 + 60 = 100**, giving **40%**

Experienced participants make up **160 ÷ 200 = 80%** of the practice group, but only **40 ÷ 100 = 40%** of the non-practice group. Within each experience band, the recorded rates are equal. Different group composition can therefore account for the aggregate association in this constructed table.

This exposes an alternative to “practice caused a 20-point gain.” It does **not** prove the sessions have zero effect: other differences may remain, and these are recorded associations. The example demonstrates why complete follow-up and identifying a cause are separate requirements.

### What a causal comparison would need

One stronger design would assign an **offer** of extra practice by lottery before the assessment, then measure the same outcome for all assigned participants, whether they accepted the offer, stayed enrolled or left. The resulting comparison addresses the effect of **being offered practice**. Comparing only the people who took it would reintroduce selection.

The design also needs reliable measurement, attention to people sharing the practice material across groups, and an assessment of uncertainty. A finite comparison can differ by chance; random assignment does not make every realised group identical. [J-PAL on random assignment and its limits](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/randomization)

Comparing two group rates at one follow-up is **not difference-in-differences**. That method compares **before-to-after changes in both groups** and needs a credible account of how they would have changed without the intervention, commonly expressed as a parallel-trends assumption. Our table has one outcome occasion and does not perform that analysis. [World Bank researchers on the method and its assumptions](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/impactevaluations/often-unspoken-assumptions-behind-difference-difference-estimator-practice)

## Audit the record as well as the source's name

A starting register helps expose deletions because you can reconcile later records against the original IDs. It does not guarantee that every eligible person was enrolled correctly, every assessment was obtained or every value was recorded accurately.

Ask whether the record covers **your target population**, measures **your outcome**, uses **your window** and retains **unknown results**. A register of people who completed a course is complete for completers, not for everyone who started. A record of every local starter can describe that cohort without automatically representing another town or year.

| Source | What it could contribute | What to audit |
|---|---|---|
| Original enrolment list | The identities eligible for follow-up | Coverage, duplicate IDs and entry dates |
| Independent assessment ledger | Results and measurement dates | Missing results, shared criteria and recording errors |
| Interviews with selected graduates | Possible mechanisms and individual experiences | How people were selected and whose experiences are absent |
| A published complete comparison | Descriptive rates for its stated population | Denominators, window, outcome definition and causal design |

Testimony can reveal a useful mechanism without estimating how common it is. Records can support a rate without explaining its cause. Choose a source for the question it can answer, rather than treating “registry” as a guarantee or “interview” as automatically worthless.

## Apply the denominator check to a different project

A makers' club enrols **80 people on day zero**. Before the work starts, 60 choose to use a planning checklist and 20 do not. The outcome is **delivering a prototype by the end of day 14**, verified from a complete delivery log for all 80 starters.

| Initial choice | Delivered by day 14 | Did not deliver by day 14 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Used the checklist | 18 | 42 | 60 |
| Did not use it | 12 | 8 | 20 |

The newsletter says: “60% of those who delivered used the checklist.” Calculate the delivery rate among checklist users before opening the explanation.

<FillBlank
  client:visible
  question="Complete the independent comparison"
  text="Among checklist users, the delivery rate is {{30%|60%|75%}}. The newsletter's 60% uses {{30 people who delivered|60 checklist users|80 starters}} as its denominator."
  explanation="Among the 60 checklist users, 18 delivered a prototype, giving 30%. There were 18 + 12 = 30 people who delivered; 18 of those 30 used the checklist, giving the newsletter's 60%."
/>

<details>
<summary>Compare the full calculation and its limit</summary>

The newsletter correctly calculates **18 ÷ (18 + 12) = 60%** among people who delivered. The group rates are **18 ÷ 60 = 30%** with the checklist and **12 ÷ 20 = 60%** without it. The observed difference is **30% − 60% = −30 percentage points**.

That result contradicts an inference that checklist users had a higher delivery rate in this cohort. It does not show that the checklist harmed them: people facing more difficult projects may have been more likely to choose it. We need evidence about selection and a suitable causal design before explaining the difference as an effect.

### A reusable check before acting

Write down the population, outcome and window. Match the denominator to the question. Reconcile starting IDs with known and unresolved outcomes. Investigate why records are missing, and distinguish the recorded association from an explanation of what caused it. If the data cannot answer the broader question, report the narrower answer they do support.
