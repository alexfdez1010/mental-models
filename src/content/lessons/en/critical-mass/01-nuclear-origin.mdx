“Each entity produces one descendant on average” sounds like a family that keeps replacing itself. It need not describe any individual family at all. In our main example the mean is one, yet after two generations **one is an impossible count**.

We will first locate the physical origin of the terminology, then work with an explicitly abstract, invented reproduction rule. The calculations concern that rule; they do not predict a reactor or assess physical safety.

## The physical origin stays qualitative

In nuclear fission, a nucleus splits, releasing energy and neutrons. Some neutrons can cause further fissions. **Criticality** describes a self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction. It is not another word for an explosion: controlled reactor operation can be critical. Nor is that word, or its absence, a complete safety assessment.

[Idaho National Laboratory's explanation of criticality](https://inl.gov/nuclear-energy/reactor-criticality/) and the [US Department of Energy's terminology guide](https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/what-criticality-and-7-other-misunderstood-nuclear-energy-terms) support this qualitative meaning. A real physical system requires a physical model and appropriate expertise. Nothing below provides material quantities, operating instructions or a safety test.

Use the origin to understand the term. When transferring it to another subject, name the new mechanism rather than importing a physical conclusion along with the vocabulary.

## Declare what reproduces and what leaves

Think of an abstract family tree: a parent either ends its branch or creates two new branches. This is a bookkeeping analogy, not a claim about human families. A **generation** is one reproduction step, not a measured duration.

Start with one entity at generation zero. A **probability** expresses a chance as a number between zero and one; the probabilities of all outcomes of a choice sum to one. Here $p$ is the probability of two descendants, and the remaining $1-p$ belongs to zero. Each entity leaves the process after producing its descendants:

| Descendants from one entity | Probability |
| ---: | ---: |
| 0 | $1-p$ |
| 2 | $p$ |

**Independence** means knowing one entity's outcome does not change another's probabilities, including across generations. The same law applies each time. Only new descendants enter the next generation: no surviving parents, immigration, merging, competition, resource limit or feedback. There is no finite pool from which descendants are taken.

Let $Z_t$ be the count in generation $t$, with $Z_0=1$. The **mean offspring count**, written $m$, is the probability-weighted average from one entity:

$$m=0(1-p)+2p=2p.$$

For $p=3/4$, the mean is $3/2$. One entity still produces either zero or two descendants; it never produces one and a half. For $p=1/2$, the mean is one; that does not insert a one-descendant outcome into the table.

Use a reproduction law when the question concerns newly generated entities. A model in which existing teams stay active, as in the introduction's contact network, counts something different.

## Enumerate the second generation

Set $p=1/2$. The first entity ends its branch with probability $1/2$, leaving no further entities. Otherwise it creates two descendants. Label those descendants left and right solely to enumerate their independent outcomes.

| First outcome | Descendants from left and right | Count in generation two | Probability of this complete case |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| No descendants | No parents remain | 0 | 1/2 |
| Two descendants | 0 and 0 | 0 | 1/8 |
| Two descendants | 0 and 2 | 2 | 1/8 |
| Two descendants | 2 and 0 | 2 | 1/8 |
| Two descendants | 2 and 2 | 4 | 1/8 |

Each of the last four rows has probability $(1/2)(1/2)(1/2)=1/8$: the first entity reproduces, then each of its two descendants takes the specified outcome. Add probabilities of rows with the same final count:

| Count in generation two | Probability | Count times probability |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 5/8 | 0 |
| 2 | 1/4 | 1/2 |
| 4 | 1/8 | 1/2 |

This table is a **probability distribution**: it assigns each possible count its probability. The probabilities sum to one and the weighted contributions sum to one. The most probable count is zero. **The mean is one, but the probability of a count of one is zero.** An average can sit in a gap between possible outcomes.

## Follow the mean without inventing a path

If a generation contains exactly $z$ entities, each contributes a mean of $m$ new entities. The next count therefore has mean $zm$. Averaging over the possible current counts gives a mean of $m$ times the current mean. Starting from one entity:

$$\mathbb{E}[Z_t]=m^t=(2p)^t.$$

The symbol $\mathbb{E}$ means **expectation**, another name for the probability-weighted mean. It does not mean “the count you should expect to observe every time.”

| Probability of two descendants | Mean per entity | Mean at generation zero | Mean at generation one | Mean at generation two | Mean at generation three |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1/4 | 1/2 | 1 | 1/2 | 1/4 | 1/8 |
| 1/2 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 3/4 | 3/2 | 1 | 3/2 | 9/4 | 27/8 |

For example, the last row's generation-three mean is $(3/2)^3=27/8$. It does not assert that each tree grows at that rate. A particular tree can stop at its first step even in that row.

In this branching terminology, a mean below one is **subcritical**, equal to one is **critical**, and above one is **supercritical**. These words classify the mean under this law. They do not turn a probability calculation into a guarantee for a single family.

## Zero is absorbing, even when the mean stays positive

An empty family has nobody left to reproduce. With no immigration, once $Z_t=0$, every later count is zero. This is an **absorbing state**: the rule cannot leave it.

Write $q_t$ for the probability that a family from one founder has reached zero **by generation $t$**. Because zero is absorbing, this is also the probability that its generation-$t$ count is zero. Initially $q_0=0$. To be empty by generation $t+1$, either:

1. The first entity leaves no descendants: probability $1-p$.
2. It leaves two, and **both** independent descendant families become empty within their remaining $t$ generations: probability $p q_t^2$.

These cases do not overlap, so

$$q_{t+1}=1-p+p q_t^2,\qquad q_0=0.$$

For $p=1/2$, first $q_1=1/2$. Next $q_2=1/2+(1/2)(1/2)^2=5/8$. Then $q_3=1/2+(1/2)(5/8)^2=89/128$.

| Generation | Probability of zero by then | Probability of a positive count | Unconditional mean |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| 1 | 1/2 | 1/2 | 1 |
| 2 | 5/8 | 3/8 | 1 |
| 3 | 89/128 | 39/128 | 1 |

There is no contradiction. At generation three, the positive counts carry all the weighted count. Restricting the average to those still present changes the denominator:

$$\mathbb{E}[Z_3\mid Z_3>0]=\frac{1}{39/128}=\frac{128}{39}.$$

The vertical bar means “among cases where the count is positive.” This conditional mean is about $3.28$, not one. The unconditional mean includes the extinct families. Selecting only surviving families would hide their probability of disappearance.

Use $q_t$ when the question names a deadline. Do not replace it with the mean or with a claim about an unlimited future.

## A finite deadline differs from eventual extinction

**Eventual extinction** means reaching zero at some finite generation, however late. Its probability is $q_\infty=\lim_{t\to\infty}q_t$. This uses an infinite horizon and the same independent reproduction law forever. A nonzero count at generation three does not prove survival forever.

For our three presets, the finite recurrence gives:

| Probability of two descendants | Zero by generation one | Zero by generation two | Zero by generation three | Eventual extinction |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1/4 | 3/4 | 57/64 | 15537/16384 | 1 |
| 1/2 | 1/2 | 5/8 | 89/128 | 1 |
| 3/4 | 1/4 | 19/64 | 5179/16384 | 1/3 |

The supercritical preset has a survival probability of $2/3$, **not certainty**. At the critical preset eventual extinction has probability one, although the mean at each finite generation remains one. At any fixed finite generation, the probability of having reached zero is still below one. Larger counts in a shrinking surviving subset can sustain that mean. Taking a limit of the probability of zero is not the same operation as averaging counts at a fixed generation.

<details>
<summary>Why the smallest fixed point gives the eventual probability</summary>

The events “zero by generation $t$” grow with $t$, so $q_t$ increases and stays at most one. Its limit exists. The recurrence is a polynomial, so substituting the limit gives

$$q=1-p+pq^2,\qquad (q-1)(pq-(1-p))=0.$$

A **fixed point** is a number left unchanged by this recurrence. The candidate roots are $1$ and $(1-p)/p$. A root is not automatically the answer: we need the one approached from $q_0=0$.

The function $f(x)=1-p+px^2$ is increasing on $[0,1]$. If $r$ is any fixed point in that interval, $q_0\le r$ and $q_t\le r$ implies $q_{t+1}=f(q_t)\le f(r)=r$. The limit therefore cannot exceed any such root; being a root itself, it is the smallest one.

At $p=1/4$, the other root is three, outside the interval, so $q_\infty=1$. At $p=1/2$, both roots equal one. At $p=3/4$, the smaller root is $1/3$.

This argument specializes the branching-process result in [Hao Wu's MIT lecture nineteen](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-445-introduction-to-stochastic-processes-spring-2015/27aaeb9eb9a9cecce93d9ad74413ddf5_MIT18_445S15_lecture19.pdf) and [Steve Lalley's notes, sections one point one to one point three](https://galton.uchicago.edu/~lalley/Courses/312/Branching.pdf). Their independence and nondegenerate-law conditions matter; the next comparison shows why the mean alone is insufficient.

</details>

Use the eventual probability only when an unlimited horizon and the unchanged law are relevant. A practical review after three rounds still needs the finite column.

## Add an independent founder without changing the law

Two founders create two independent family trees. The combined count is their sum. Its mean doubles, but the mean offspring per entity remains $m$. To have a total count of zero, both families must be empty:

$$\mathbb{E}[Z_t]=2(2p)^t,\qquad P(Z_t=0)=q_t^2.$$

At $p=1/2$ and generation two, each family has probabilities $5/8,1/4,1/8$ for counts zero, two and four. For a combined count of four, the possibilities are $(0,4),(2,2),(4,0)$, giving $(5/8)(1/8)+(1/4)^2+(1/8)(5/8)=14/64$.

| Combined count at generation two | Probability |
| ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 25/64 |
| 2 | 20/64 |
| 4 | 14/64 |
| 6 | 4/64 |
| 8 | 1/64 |

The mean is two. The zero probability falls from $5/8$ with one founder to $25/64$ with two. This is a change in the number of independent starting families, not a change from critical to supercritical.

At $p=3/4$, two independent founders instead have eventual extinction $(1/3)^2=1/9$. Their survival probability is $8/9$, still not certainty. If a shared shock makes the families dependent, this product needs a new justification; calculate their joint extinction probability under that dependence.

## Check which assumptions carry the conclusion

Consider a **different law**: every entity always produces exactly one descendant. Its mean is also one, but starting from one gives $Z_t=1$ forever. Zero is never reached. Calling it “critical” by its mean does not make it the zero-or-two law.

| Law from one founder | Mean offspring | Probability of zero by generation two | Eventual extinction |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Zero or two, each with probability one half | 1 | 5/8 | 1 |
| Always exactly one | 1 | 0 | 0 |

The usual critical-extinction result needs its assumptions; it excludes the degenerate always-one law. In our presets both zero and two have positive probability. **Degenerate** here means all probability is concentrated on a single outcome.

Other departures change other parts of the argument. Immigration lets an empty process restart. A shared reproduction outcome can make families dependent, so “both empty” need not have probability $q_t^2$. A law changing with time invalidates the fixed-law eventual calculation. A resource cap changes the distribution and the mean recurrence. None of these is repaired by renaming the mean.

Use the model to answer a clearly stated hypothetical or to expose what real evidence is missing. Estimating a reproduction law requires observations of relevant generation links and the selection process; a few rising counts alone do not establish the law. This lesson's exact arithmetic supplies no empirical estimate.
