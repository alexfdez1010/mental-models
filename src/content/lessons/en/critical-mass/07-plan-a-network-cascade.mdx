Five fictional teams are considering a shared filing convention. A team joins when enough of its named contacts already use it. You can commit a few teams at the start. Your job is to decide **where to start and whether the result arrives in time**.

This is a planning puzzle with stipulated behavior, not measured adoption data. The arrows and requirements below are fully known. Real teams can decline, leave or communicate differently; the puzzle deliberately holds those possibilities still.

| Team | Receives a contact from | Active contacts required |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| A | C | 1 |
| B | A | 1 |
| C | B | 1 |
| D | A and C | 2 |
| E | D | 1 |

An arrow points from contributor to receiver. A→B means A can contribute to B; it does not mean B contributes to A. A **seed** is a team committed active at round zero, independently of its own requirement. An active team stays active. Each round reads the active teams at its start, then activates all eligible teams together. A round is one update, with no assumed duration in days.

## Read one round without borrowing from the next

Think of a photograph taken at the beginning of each round. Everyone checks that photograph, and only then does the next photograph get taken. The analogy explains simultaneity; it does not claim real organizations work in synchronized meetings.

Let $S_t$ be the set of active teams after round $t$, $N_i$ the sources with arrows into team $i$, and $h_i$ its required contact count. The rule is

$$S_{t+1}=S_t\cup\{i:|N_i\cap S_t|\ge h_i\}.$$

The union retains the existing active teams. The count uses $S_t$, never a partly updated $S_{t+1}$. Equality is enough: D joins when both its incoming contacts are active.

Commit only A. At the first update B sees its required A. C still sees an inactive B; D sees A but not C; E sees an inactive D. Only B joins. Continue the same calculation:

| Round | Newly active | Active after the round | Why the next team can join |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | A, committed | A | B receives A |
| 1 | B | A, B | C receives B |
| 2 | C | A, B, C | D receives both A and C |
| 3 | D | A, B, C, D | E receives D |
| 4 | E | A, B, C, D, E | No inactive team remains |

The tempting shortcut is to scan A, then B, then C, updating each immediately. That lets an activation travel several arrows within one round. It solves a different timing model, even if the final set happens to agree.

Use snapshot updates when a problem specifies simultaneous rounds. If communication instead arrives at unequal times, model those arrival times explicitly before reusing this schedule.

## A seed budget does not specify a starting position

A bus ticket count does not tell you where the buses start. Likewise, the number of seeds omits their connections. Here the bus analogy has a limit: activation copies a state rather than moving a team out of its previous location.

Compare single-team starts. E has no outgoing contact: committing E leaves everyone else inactive. Committing D activates E, but no contact goes back into A, B or C. Committing C travels around the left-hand cycle and also helps D.

| Only seed | Newly active in successive rounds, including the initial round | Active by end round two | Active at closure |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| A | A → B → C → D → E | 3 | 5 |
| B | B → C → A → D → E | 3 | 5 |
| C | C → A → B and D → E | 4 | 5 |
| D | D → E | 2 | 2 |
| E | E | 1 | 1 |

**Closure** means an update would add no team under the current fixed rules. It can be partial: E alone is already closed. Closure does not mean everyone joined, and a flat total does not by itself mean a real project is permanently doomed.

The cycle A→B→C→A does not start spontaneously. With no seeds, every team sees zero active incoming contacts and every requirement is positive. The empty active set is also closed.

Use the incoming paths to compare placements; do not replace them with an average requirement. In this puzzle the average requirement is unchanged across all seed plans, yet their outcomes differ.

## Specify the deadline before comparing success

Suppose every committed seed costs **one planning unit**. All teams can be seeded. The objective is to activate all five teams **by the end of round two**, while minimizing seed cost. There are no other costs or benefits in this constructed objective.

Compare two plans that each cost two units:

| Round | Newly active with A and D | Newly active with A and E |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | A, D | A, E |
| 1 | B, E | B |
| 2 | C | C |
| 3 | None, already closed | D |

A+D meets the deadline. Seeding D directly does not require first activating its two contacts: that is exactly what exogenous commitment means. A+E eventually reaches the same five teams, but E cannot help D and D must wait for C. Equal seed cost and equal final coverage conceal a missed deadline.

A useful result report names the seed plan, the active set, and the round. “It reaches everyone” leaves out the very condition that decides this job. If the deadline changed, you would need to rerun the comparison.

## Prove the budget against every cheaper permitted plan

Finding one working plan proves sufficiency: this budget can work. Proving a **minimum** also requires necessity: no cheaper permitted plan works. Checking the cheapest cases is the missing half of many confident launch slides.

Here the empty set fails, and the singleton table shows that no single seed activates everyone by the deadline. A successful pair therefore establishes a minimum of two planning units. To identify all minimum-cost plans, check every pair rather than selecting the most impressive-looking node.

| Two seeds | Active by end round two | Meets all-team deadline |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| A, B | 4 | No |
| A, C | 5 | Yes |
| A, D | 5 | Yes |
| A, E | 4 | No |
| B, C | 4 | No |
| B, D | 5 | Yes |
| B, E | 4 | No |
| C, D | 5 | Yes |
| C, E | 5 | Yes |
| D, E | 2 | No |

For example, C+E gives A at the first update and B+D at the second, completing the set on time. A+C instead gives B+D at the first update and E at the second. Different paths can satisfy the same objective.

There are five successful pairs, not one uniquely best launch. All have the same cost and meet the specified deadline. Calling A+D *the* optimum would erase these ties. Calling two seeds a universal “critical mass” would erase the graph, requirements and deadline that made the result true.

Under these fixed progressive rules, adding seeds cannot remove an active team at a given round: it cannot reduce any incoming count. That is a local monotonicity claim. A larger seed set can still be worse on cost, and departures or changing thresholds would require another argument.

## Transfer to a changed requirement

Keep every arrow, commitment rule and update convention. Change only D's required incoming count to **one**. Commit only A. Before using the control, write the newly active set for each update and decide whether the deadline is met.

<summary>Check the complete transfer calculation</summary>

| Round | Active before the update | Newly active | Active after the update |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Initial commitment | A | A |
| 1 | A | B and D | A, B, D |
| 2 | A, B, D | C and E | A, B, C, D, E |

Only D's requirement changed. The eventual reach from A is still five; completion moves from round four to round two. This isolates a timing improvement from an increase in final coverage. In the visual, choose D's lower requirement and reset to A alone to inspect it.

</details>

A real change in a team's requirement would need evidence: what counts as an incoming contact, when it is observed, whether the team actually adopts at that count, and whether it stays active. The table stipulates all of these; it does not discover them.

<Callout variant="warning" title="A trace is a conditional result">
The exact answer depends on fixed directed contacts, threshold-triggered adoption, persistent activation and synchronized updates. Refusal, departures, shared outside announcements or extra communication delays can change both timing and coverage. One successful rollout does not identify the arrows or thresholds that caused it.
</Callout>

The source model in [Easley and Kleinberg, chapter nineteen](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book-ch19.pdf) links local adoption thresholds, initial adopters and network structure. It uses an undirected coordination game with fractional thresholds. Our directed contact counts are a deliberately constructed variant; its calculations above are derived from its own rules. [Granovetter's original paper](https://snap.stanford.edu/class/cs224w-readings/granovetter78threshold.pdf) also develops threshold models and discusses the importance of social structure and the limits of treating everyone as connected to everyone else.

## Reconstruct the decision

Without looking back, explain why E alone closes immediately, why A+D and A+E differ at the deadline, and what excludes a cheaper plan. Then use the recap to check which part of the argument you omitted.
