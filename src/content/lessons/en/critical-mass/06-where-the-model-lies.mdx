A threshold model can turn a vague story into a checkable prediction. It can also give a vague story an impressive-looking symbol. The difference is whether the rule, measurements and decision actually match.

This lesson audits seven places where the analogy can outrun its evidence. It preserves the distinction between a fixed multiplication rule and local contact thresholds. Neither model gives a universal diagnosis of every quiet project or sudden change.

## Extrapolation needs a rule and a range

A smooth trace is like a few footprints: it records part of a route without revealing the entire map. Two processes can agree on the observations and diverge later. That does not make forecasting impossible; it makes its conditions part of the forecast.

Consider constructed activity units, measured once per update:

| Update | Observed units | Constant doubling continues | Doubling with capacity capped at sixteen |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 4 | 4 | 4 |
| 1 | 8 | 8 | 8 |
| 2 | 16 | 16 | 16 |
| 3 | Not observed | 32 | 16 |

Both models fit every observed value. The last column uses $x_{t+1}=\min(2x_t,16)$; the other uses $x_{t+1}=2x_t$. The difference at the next update comes from the capacity assumption, not arithmetic. These three points do not establish a threshold, a capacity or a continuing multiplier.

Use extrapolation when a mechanism and an operating range justify it, and check how alternatives change the decision. “Straight lines always lie near thresholds” is too broad: a local linear approximation can be useful over a sufficiently limited range. The missing task is to test that range.

## Separate new activity from what has accumulated

A shrinking number of new arrivals does not erase everyone who arrived earlier. Think of a bath with a slowing tap: the flow can fall while the water level still rises. The analogy separates a flow from a stock; it does not tell you the drain rate unless you specify one.

Here teams adopt a filing convention and nobody leaves during the three measured periods. The opening active total is one hundred:

| Period | Newly active teams | Teams leaving | Active total at period end |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 20 | 0 | 120 |
| 2 | 15 | 0 | 135 |
| 3 | 10 | 0 | 145 |

New activity falls while the active total grows. Ratios of those new counts do not identify who caused whom to adopt. They could reflect different exposure windows, outside recruitment or a changing pool of eligible teams. Calling the project “dead” or “bound to tip soon” goes beyond this table.

A finite local model makes another distinction visible. Below, start with D committed. E can join; then no inactive team can meet its requirement. The run has no further additions but retains active teams, because persistence is a rule.

Use separate new, leaving and active-total measurements when assessing whether a rollout is spreading or being maintained. A decision to continue, revise or stop also needs the objective, cost and alternatives. Patience alone neither changes the declared rule nor identifies the real one.

## Count the comparison set before judging what is common

A display of successful launches is a display window, not a denominator. To estimate how common an outcome was, define the eligible cases, the outcome and the observation window before counting.

In a **constructed audit**, all ten eligible pilots are followed for the same four rounds. Success means all teams in a pilot are active by the end of that window. Two succeed. A presentation shows only those two pilots:

| Record | Successful pilots | Pilots included | Share meeting the defined outcome |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Selected presentation | 2 | 2 | 100% |
| Complete constructed register | 2 | 10 | 20% |

The presentation exaggerates the success share **for this register**. It does not prove that most trends in the world never tip. An invented register supplies no empirical base rate; a real register would still need relevant case selection before you transferred its rate to another setting.

Use this audit when examples have been selected by their outcomes. Keep missing cases, failures and observation windows visible. Do not “correct” a missing denominator by inventing a pessimistic probability.

## Conditions can change without changing just one multiplier

A map becomes stale if a bridge closes. Likewise, network links, requirements, available participants and communication delays can change. A single fitted multiplier may hide these different mechanisms.

In a small constructed contact rule, D receives from A and C. If D requires both, A alone is insufficient. Lower D's requirement to one and A becomes sufficient at the next update. Add an outside commitment to D instead and D starts active immediately. These interventions differ in both mechanism and timing; calling each “raising the same multiplier” loses the distinction.

The network model in [Easley and Kleinberg's chapter nineteen](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book-ch19.pdf) explicitly connects local requirements, starting adopters and structure. Our directed-count examples stipulate their own rules rather than claiming that all networks share one global threshold.

Use an updated model when a relevant condition changes. State what stays fixed, then rerun the trace. A finite population can bound the final total, but its adoption path need not be an S-curve, and the curve's shape alone cannot identify its cause.

## Precision is useful when it resolves the decision

A ruler marked in millimeters is useful when its measurements deserve those marks. Decimal places are not automatically evidence of accuracy or of fraud. Ask how uncertainty affects the decision.

Suppose a team's rule is to adopt when the incoming active count reaches its requirement. One contact is currently active. The requirement is unknown within the set $\{1,2\}$. Under the lower value it joins next round; under the higher value it does not. Reporting an average requirement of $1.5$ would conceal the two possible decisions.

For a separate fixed-multiplication model, an estimated multiplier interval from $0.98$ to $1.04$ spans unity. It does not establish growth or decline under that model. More observations may narrow the interval, but changing conditions or a wrong model need different evidence. Exact values in a stipulated puzzle are different from estimates about the world.

Use a range of defensible values and test whether the preferred action changes. If every plausible value leads to the same decision, coarse measurement may suffice. If the decision changes within the range, a confident point estimate is not a substitute for resolving the uncertainty or planning for it.

## An external jump is not evidence of self-propagation

A single outside announcement can move many teams at once. A relay carries activity from one team to another. Both can raise a total; they imply different mechanisms. The relay analogy is limited because a real audience can receive several messages and respond for several reasons.

Consider two constructed explanations for ten new teams joining on one day. Under one, all ten received a central invitation and none recruited anyone. Under the other, an initial team contacted peers who then contacted more peers. The same daily total fits both. You need contact timing, exposure and comparison evidence to distinguish them; merely drawing arrows after seeing the count does not establish them.

Use the distinction before diagnosing a sudden level change as a threshold crossing. Outside input can also coexist with propagation, so finding an announcement does not prove the reinforcing pathway absent.

## Persistence in a model is not a measured return path

Hysteresis means the state at the same present condition can depend on the path taken to get there. Offset switching thresholds can implement it. It does not logically require a huge gap, permanent irreversibility or a particular cost ranking.

Consider a **stipulated controller**, not a claim about any named device. It switches ON when its input reaches twenty-two units, switches OFF when it falls to twenty units, and keeps its previous state between those levels.

| Route to current input | Current input | Current state |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Start OFF, rise from 19 to 21 | 21 | OFF |
| Start ON at 23, fall to 21 | 21 | ON |

The same input produces different states because of history. That is a worked example of hysteresis. In contrast, a threshold-network puzzle that simply forbids departures has built persistence into its rule. It has not measured what happens when a real driver is lowered, nor proved that the return threshold differs from the outward threshold.

Use a return-path comparison when hysteresis matters. Specify the driver, history, state and time allowed to settle. Prevention and recovery may have very different costs, but hysteresis alone does not price either one. Compare feasible options, consequences and time horizons before recommending an action.
