A scaling claim often arrives as a finished-looking equation and a confident number. Your task is to reconstruct the steps that support it.

Use a constructed filling problem. A tank has a constant horizontal cross-sectional area of 0.30 square metre. It starts empty. An inflow of fifteen litres per minute runs for twenty minutes; initially there are no other flows. We seek the increase in liquid height. All these inputs are declared exact within the model.

## Reconstruct the boundary and the target

Think of the tank account as a ledger with an explicit boundary. The height comes from net accumulated volume and the given cross-section. Name the quantity before checking the arithmetic: $q$ is **net** volume per time, $t$ is duration and $A$ is area.

For this constant-area, constant-net-flow model,

$$
V=qt,\qquad h=\frac VA=\frac{qt}{A}.
$$

The first equation is the declared flow account from an empty start. The second follows from the specified prismatic geometry. If area varied with height, a single constant $A$ would not describe that geometry. If the tank began partly full, distinguish the final height from the increase.

### When to stop and ask for an input

Stop if the claim gives an inlet flow but silently needs net flow, or if it substitutes a surface area for cross-sectional area. Those quantities may share an area unit while answering different questions. Resolve the definition before interpreting a compatible equation.

## Convert the inputs, then calculate

Fifteen litres per minute is 0.015 cubic metres per minute. Over twenty minutes, net volume is 0.300 cubic metre. Dividing by 0.30 square metre gives one metre of height increase:

$$
h=\frac{(0.015\,\mathrm{m^3/min})(20\,\mathrm{min})}{0.30\,\mathrm{m^2}}=1\,\mathrm m.
$$

As an independent unit representation, the flow is 0.00025 cubic metres per second and the duration 1200 seconds. Their product is again 0.300 cubic metre. Keeping the flow number fifteen but writing litres per second would change the physical input.

### When a numerical match is only part of the audit

A recorded height of one metre would be consistent with this case. One agreement would not prove that every later case has the same area, flow or omitted losses. Preserve the declared conditions when carrying the calculation forward.

## Change one condition and recompute the account

Keep the inflow, duration, constant cross-section and empty start. Change only the outflow from zero to a constant three litres per minute. Predict the height increase before opening the account.

### When the transferred quantity keeps its name

Check its definition again. “Flow” can name an inlet or a net rate; “height” can name a change or a final level. A preserved variable letter does not guarantee a preserved quantity.

## Reject a single-power extrapolation of a mixed outcome

Return to the cost model with a fixed ten-credit term, three credits per square metre and two per cubic metre. For a three-metre closed cube, area is fifty-four square metres and volume twenty-seven cubic metres. The full model gives

$$
K=\bigl(10+3(54)+2(27)\bigr)\,\mathrm{cr}=226\,\mathrm{cr}.
$$

Scaling the one-metre total of thirty credits by the area factor nine would give 270 credits. Include the actual terms to see where the difference comes from. Credits denote a separate cost quantity; every term carries that same cost unit.

### When the audit is ready to use

You can now state the accepted result and its reach together: under the initial constant-flow and constant-area card, height increases one metre; with the declared outflow, it increases 0.8 metre; under the three-term cube-cost model, the three-metre total is 226 credits.

For another system, list the inputs and conditions again. The method transfers even when the numerical coefficient, variables or geometry do not.
