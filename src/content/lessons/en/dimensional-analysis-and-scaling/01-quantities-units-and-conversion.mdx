A unit conversion is a controlled rewrite of a quantity. Like changing a map’s scale, it changes the numbers used to describe an object while keeping the object in view. The conversion factor must transform every occurrence of the unit, including its powers.

## Convert products and quotients as complete quantities

Write a factor equal to one in the direction that cancels the old unit. For a distance of 150 centimetres,

$$
150\,\mathrm{cm}\times\frac{1\,\mathrm m}{100\,\mathrm{cm}}=1.5\,\mathrm m.
$$

A flow has a volume unit and a time unit. Starting from twelve litres per minute, first convert litres, then the reciprocal time unit:

$$
12\,\frac{\mathrm L}{\mathrm{min}}\times\frac{1\,\mathrm{m^3}}{1000\,\mathrm L}\times\frac{1\,\mathrm{min}}{60\,\mathrm s}=0.0002\,\frac{\mathrm{m^3}}{\mathrm s}.
$$

The minute cancels from the denominator because the second factor supplies a minute in the numerator. A common error is to multiply by sixty merely because “minutes to seconds” sounds like multiplication. Here you are converting **per minute**. Use the units to decide the direction.

### When a quotient needs special care

Check every factor when a quantity is quoted “per hour”, “per square metre” or “per item”. Converting the denominator changes its reciprocal. If a quantity is a sum, convert the terms into compatible units before adding them. One metre plus fifty centimetres is 1.5 metres; adding the bare numbers gives fifty-one without a coherent unit.

## Carry the power with the unit

A square of edge ten centimetres has area one hundred square centimetres. Converting the edge first gives 0.1 metre, whose square is 0.01 square metre. Converting the area directly gives the same result:

$$
100\,\mathrm{cm^2}\times\left(\frac{1\,\mathrm m}{100\,\mathrm{cm}}\right)^2=0.01\,\mathrm{m^2}.
$$

You can see why both factors are needed by stopping halfway. One linear conversion gives one metre-centimetre, not one square metre. A centimetre is still present and must be converted.

For a cube, the length conversion appears three times. A volume of eight cubic centimetres is eight millionths of a cubic metre:

$$
8\,\mathrm{cm^3}\times\left(\frac{1\,\mathrm m}{100\,\mathrm{cm}}\right)^3=0.000008\,\mathrm{m^3}.
$$

Litres are another volume unit: two litres equal two thousand cubic centimetres, or 0.002 cubic metres. “Cubic” in a unit name does not mean that every conversion uses the same numerical factor; identify the linear units or use the declared volume equivalence.

### When scale and conversion get confused

Converting a ten-centimetre cube into metre units preserves the cube. Building a twenty-centimetre cube changes it. First decide which task you are doing; the next lesson on geometry will use the second task explicitly.

## Check whether the scale has a shifted origin

Temperature gives a useful boundary case. The Celsius-to-kelvin relation for a temperature is

$$
T(\mathrm K)=t({}^\circ\mathrm C)+273.15.
$$

Here we use the numerical values in those two units. Twenty degrees Celsius corresponds to 293.15 kelvin, and forty degrees Celsius to 313.15 kelvin. Their thermodynamic-temperature ratio is $313.15/293.15=6263/5863$, approximately 1.0682. It is not two. A Celsius zero is an offset reference.

A **temperature difference** behaves differently: forty minus twenty degrees Celsius is a difference of twenty Celsius degrees, which equals a difference of twenty kelvin. The offset cancels when subtracting. It does not cancel when taking the ratio of the absolute temperature values.

### When to ask about the origin

Ask before interpreting “twice as much” on an offset scale. You need the scale transformation and a meaningful zero for the ratio you intend to use. This example concerns the declared Celsius–kelvin conversion; it is not a rule that every named scale has the same transformation.
