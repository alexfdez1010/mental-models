A small model and a larger object can share a shape while operating under different physical conditions. The useful question is which relationships your proposed transfer needs to preserve.

**Geometric similarity** fixes relative lengths and shape. **Dynamic similarity**, as used here, asks for matching dimensionless conditions relevant to a chosen physical model. Choosing that model and its variables is part of the work.

## Choose the conditions you intend to preserve

Return to the positive quantities $F,\rho,v,d,\mu$. Call the two groups

$$
C_D=\frac F{\rho v^2d^2},\qquad \mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho vd}{\mu}.
$$

The second is commonly called the Reynolds number in fluid models. For this lesson, its defined ratio and the listed dimensions are enough to do the account. Suppose the fluid properties $\rho$ and $\mu$ remain fixed. Preserving $\mathrm{Re}$ requires $vd$ to stay fixed, so $d'=kd$ pairs with $v'=v/k$.

Use a constructed comparison: density one thousand kilograms per cubic metre and dynamic viscosity one kilogram per metre-second. A length of half a metre with speed two metres per second gives $\mathrm{Re}=1000$. A length of one metre with speed one metre per second gives the same group. These stipulated properties are not measurements of a named fluid.

### When group matching helps

Use it after stating which properties stay fixed and which group matters to the proposed comparison. Geometric similarity is one condition. It does not establish that the selected group set captures every relevant physical effect.

## Carry a coefficient assumption into the force calculation

Rearranging the first group gives

$$
F=C_D\rho v^2d^2.
$$

Under the changes $d'=kd$ and $v'=v/k$, the product $v^2d^2$ is unchanged. If $\rho$ **and $C_D$** are also the same, this model predicts equal forces. The equality of the coefficient is an additional premise about the physical relationship and regime.

For the constructed pair above, stipulate $C_D=0.2$ in both cases. The original force is $0.2\times1000\times2^2\times0.5^2=200$ newtons. The larger case gives $0.2\times1000\times1^2\times1^2=200$ newtons. If the second coefficient were instead 0.3, its force would be three hundred newtons. Dimensional compatibility would allow either coefficient.

A different defined quantity can change even when this force prediction stays the same. The ratio $d/v$ has time units. Doubling $d$ and halving $v$ multiplies that ratio by four. It is a characteristic time ratio here; no measured transit-time distribution has been supplied.

### When a transfer needs measurements

Ask what supports a shared coefficient or a single-valued relation such as $C_D=f(\mathrm{Re})$. The listed variables and regime must be adequate for that claim. Matching dimensions or one numerical group does not establish the premise by itself.

## Add a missing effect and check another group

Suppose an acceleration $g>0$ also matters. The earlier five-variable list is then incomplete for this expanded model. A possible additional dimensionless combination is

$$
\mathrm{Fr}=\frac v{\sqrt{gd}}.
$$

The denominator has speed dimension: $[gd]=\mathsf L^2\mathsf T^{-2}$. The positive square root therefore has dimension $\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-1}$. With fixed $g$ and the length/speed changes that preserve $\mathrm{Re}$,

$$
\mathrm{Fr}'=\frac{v/k}{\sqrt{gkd}}=\frac{\mathrm{Fr}}{k^{3/2}}.
$$

At length factor two this is the original value divided by $2\sqrt2$; at factor four it is one eighth of the original. You preserved one group and changed another. The following graph shows only these declared comparisons, with no probability interpretation.

The dimensional table still has rank three: the earlier independent dimension vectors remain present. Six variables therefore allow three independent dimensionless groups. The displayed choices $C_D$, $\mathrm{Re}$ and $\mathrm{Fr}$ are independent: the force coordinate isolates the first, viscosity isolates the second, and the acceleration coordinate then isolates the third.

A fuller physical relation may require all three, for example an implicit relation $H(C_D,\mathrm{Re},\mathrm{Fr})=0$. Dimensional analysis has not established that acceleration is important in any actual experiment. It shows how the argument changes **if** that variable belongs in the model.

### When the first matched group is insufficient

List additional relevant variables before claiming dynamic similarity. Matching one visible group can be a useful controlled comparison while leaving other conditions unmatched. A change in surface condition, geometry or regime may also require more information; which effects matter is a modelling and evidence question.

## Keep the reach of the conclusion explicit

For the constructed pair, the arithmetic of group matching is fully specified. A prediction for force needs its coefficient premise. An extrapolation to another physical system needs evidence that the variable set and operating conditions remain appropriate.

The [MIT Buckingham note](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-25-advanced-fluid-mechanics-fall-2013/c0a4521f55e9191d557c167e99e97469_MIT2_25F13_The_Buckingham.pdf) treats dimensional reduction as conditional on the problem’s variable description. Keep that condition beside the calculation when reusing the model.
