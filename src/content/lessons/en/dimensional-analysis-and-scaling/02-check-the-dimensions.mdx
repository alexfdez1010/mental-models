A calculation can fail before you know any numerical values. If a proposed filling time ends in square metres, the arithmetic has answered a different question.

Here we use **dimension** for the underlying type of a physical quantity. A length can be expressed in metres or centimetres; both have the length dimension. The unit changes the representation, while the dimension tracks the kind of quantity through the algebra.

## Name the dimensions and build a product

We will track mass, length and time with the symbols $\mathsf M$, $\mathsf L$ and $\mathsf T$. These are dimension symbols, not particular units. The time symbol $\mathsf T$ does not stand for temperature. Temperature requires another base dimension and remains outside the mechanical three-column catalogue below.

A dimensional exponent says how often a base dimension appears in a product. Speed has dimension $\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-1}$. Acceleration, a change in velocity (speed with a direction) per unit time, has dimension $\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-2}$. A negative exponent means a reciprocal; exponent zero contributes the factor one.

We write the exponents in the fixed order $(\mathsf M;\mathsf L;\mathsf T)$. Thus speed is $(0;1;-1)$, time is $(0;0;1)$ and area is $(0;2;0)$. This tuple is a compact account, like three labelled columns in a ledger. It records exponents, not measured masses, lengths or times.

Multiplication adds the exponents of each dimension. Raising a quantity to a power multiplies its exponent vector by that power. For speed $v$ and time $t$,

$$
[vt]=(\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-1})\mathsf T=\mathsf L.
$$

For the alternative expression $vt^2$,

$$
[vt^2]=(\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-1})\mathsf T^2=\mathsf L\mathsf T.
$$

The second expression has an extra time factor and cannot be a length under these declarations. A numerical coincidence, such as inserting a time value of one, does not repair the general dimensional mismatch.

### When an exponent account helps

Use it before substituting numbers into a new formula, or while checking a derived expression. Write the target quantity first. “The output should be length” makes the required vector explicit and stops a correct-looking number from hiding an extra unit.

## Apply the necessary filter, then distinguish its survivors

Suppose a model concerns displacement at a constant speed along one direction for a time $t$, with the starting displacement set to zero. Both $vt$ and $2vt$ have length dimension. At a speed of three metres per second for four seconds, they give twelve and twenty-four metres.

Dimensional analysis retains both as compatible candidates. The specified constant-speed relation gives twelve metres, so it supplies information beyond dimensions. A different coefficient could represent a different defined quantity or model; cancelling units does not choose it.

The same dimension can also occur in quantities with different meanings. For example, work done by a ten-newton force through two metres along the force is twenty joules. A ten-newton force acting at a two-metre perpendicular lever arm has a torque magnitude of twenty newton-metres. Both dimensional products are $\mathsf M\mathsf L^2\mathsf T^{-2}$, but energy transfer and turning effect answer different questions. Matching those exponent vectors does not make the quantities interchangeable.

### When to ask for another kind of evidence

Once candidates pass the filter, inspect definitions, conservation relations, geometry, coefficients and observations. This is especially useful when an equation contains a dimensionless constant that could take several values. Dimensional compatibility cannot validate the written data or the assumption that the listed variables are complete.

## Form dimensionless ratios with a stated meaning

A **dimensionless quantity** has exponent vector $(0;0;0)$; its dimensions cancel. Six kilograms divided by two kilograms is three. That result is dimensionless and greater than one, so dimensionless does not mean probability.

By contrast, six square metres divided by two cubic metres is three per metre. One length dimension remains in the denominator. The area-to-volume ratio studied next is therefore not dimensionless.

The distinction also matters for a logarithm in a unit-independent physical expression. A logarithm $\log_b(x)$ asks for the exponent $y$ such that $b^y=x$, for a positive base other than one and a positive input. Here we only audit the input; we will not need numerical logarithm software. A length ratio such as $\ell/\ell_0$ is dimensionless when the positive reference length $\ell_0$ is defined.

For $\ell=100$ centimetres and $\ell_0=10$ centimetres, the ratio is ten and its base-ten logarithm is one. In metres, the same ratio is $1/0.1=10$, so the logarithm is unchanged. Taking the logarithm of the bare numerical length instead would give two from the centimetre value and zero from the metre value. It has lost the common reference.

### When a ratio needs its reference

Use an explicit reference when a nonlinear numerical transformation should describe the same physical situation in different units. Check its domain as well: a dimensionless logarithm input must still be positive in this real-valued model. A ratio can be dimensionless while carrying a meaningful dependence on scale, geometry or operating conditions.

The terminology follows the discussion of [dimensional exponents and quantities of dimension one in NIST chapter seven](https://www.nist.gov/pml/special-publication-811/nist-guide-si-chapter-7-rules-and-style-conventions-expressing-values). The finite examples here are declared calculations, not measurements of a particular device.
