A dashboard says the operation takes **20**. Before celebrating the improvement, you need the unit. Twenty seconds and twenty minutes make rather different promises to the person standing beside the machine.

**Dimensional analysis** tracks what a quantity measures through a calculation. **Scaling** asks how one quantity changes when a declared model changes size or scale. Together they give you a procedure: name the quantities, carry the units, test the equation and state the conditions of any extrapolation.

## Keep the physical quantity in view

Imagine two labels on the same tank: 240 litres and 0.240 cubic metres. The tank has not grown while someone changed the label. A **quantity** is the property being measured; a **unit** is the chosen reference for expressing it. The **numerical value** depends on that choice.

For volume, one cubic metre equals one thousand litres. Thus

$$
240\,\mathrm L\times\frac{1\,\mathrm{m^3}}{1000\,\mathrm L}=0.240\,\mathrm{m^3}.
$$

The factor represents one and preserves the physical volume. Its units cancel the old unit and leave the new one. The equality is also consistent with a second example: two litres are 0.002 cubic metres. Merely deleting “litres” would preserve the number and lose the quantity.

### When to check the representation

Do this when a number moves between reports, software or teams. Record the property, unit, time basis and conversion. A change from litres per minute to cubic metres per second can describe the same flow; retaining the number twelve and changing “per minute” to “per second” describes a different flow.

## Work one complete calculation

Use a constructed tank model: 240 litres to fill, a constant inflow of 12 litres per minute, and no other flows. The tank starts empty and the inflow remains unchanged throughout. Filling time is volume divided by flow:

$$
t=\frac{240\,\mathrm L}{12\,\mathrm{L/min}}=20\,\mathrm{min}=1200\,\mathrm s.
$$

Now represent that same inflow as 0.0002 cubic metres per second. Dividing 0.240 cubic metres by it gives 1200 seconds again. By contrast, a physically different inflow of 12 litres **per second** would fill this volume in 20 seconds. Conversion and a change to the system lead to different operations.

### When the calculation needs another model

The division above relies on a constant net flow and a known volume. A leak, varying input or a different starting volume changes the calculation. Correct units are useful even then, but they do not establish the missing inputs.

## Separate a successful check from a prediction

An equation is **dimensionally compatible** when its two sides measure the same dimensional type. In the tank example, flow multiplied by time has the dimension of volume. Twice that product also has the dimension of volume. Units alone cannot decide the coefficient.

The next lessons develop that distinction using exact tables, changing cube sizes and finite formula catalogues. We will also meet ratios whose numerical values survive a change of compatible units. Those ratios can still describe different regimes or omit an important variable.

Before carrying a result into another setting, ask what stayed fixed. A model for separate closed cubes has a different exposed surface from eight cubes joined into a block. A constant-flow example has a different boundary from one that leaks.
