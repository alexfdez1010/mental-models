Rejecting two of five options spends **40% of the options** on an opening sample. It does not make your chance of finding the best equal to 40%. One fraction counts options; the other counts successful arrival orders. The same percentage cannot do both jobs just because it has a memorable name.

The classical secretary problem asks when to commit while seeing only comparisons with options already encountered. We will execute a complete rule, count its successes and derive its finite probability. The familiar $1/e$ comes afterwards, as a limit under the stated model.

## Specify the game before the percentage

Imagine choosing one design for a poster. The designs have a fixed strict quality order, but the judge reveals only how each new design compares with those already shown. A rejected design cannot return. This is an invented decision game, not a measured model of hiring or design quality.

| Ingredient | Assumption in this lesson |
| --- | --- |
| Pool | Exactly $n$ options, with $n$ known before play |
| Arrival law | All $n!$ orders are equally likely |
| Comparisons | Perfect relative ordering among revealed options; no ties |
| Information | No global quality labels, numerical values or unseen comparisons |
| Action | Select the current option and finish, or reject it permanently |
| Objective | Score one for selecting the unique best overall, zero for every other choice |
| Other conditions | No inspection cost, refusal by the selected option or outside alternative |
| Final condition | Select the final option if no earlier selection occurred |

A **permutation** is one ordering of the fixed options. The notation $n!$, read “n factorial,” counts those orders: for three options it is $3\times2\times1=6$. **Uniform order** means each of those six orders has probability $1/6$.

A **record** is an option better than every option revealed before it. The first option is automatically a record. That makes it best among one observed option, not necessarily best in the whole pool.

### When this setup applies

Use the calculation only after checking the information and arrival law as well as the objective. Being unable to recall an option is one condition among several. Unknown pool size, informative absolute scores or the possibility of making several offers creates another problem.

## Execute one complete cutoff policy

Choose an integer $k$ before observing anything, with $0\le k\le n-1$. It is the number of opening options you will reject.

1. Reject the first $k$ options, retaining their relative comparisons.
2. Select the first later record and stop immediately.
3. If no eligible record appears, select the final option.

For $k=0$, select the first option. For $k=n-1$, only the last remains eligible, so it is selected regardless of whether it is a record. This final fallback is part of the rule, not a secret opportunity to recover the earlier best.

For three options, an analyst can label completed quality order $1<2<3$. These labels belong to the **completed record**, not the chooser's live information. Here is every possible arrival order and the selected global label:

| Completed arrival order | Reject none | Reject one | Reject two |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1, 2, 3 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| 1, 3, 2 | 1 | 3 | 2 |
| 2, 1, 3 | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| 2, 3, 1 | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| 3, 1, 2 | 3 | 2 | 2 |
| 3, 2, 1 | 3 | 1 | 1 |
| Selections of the best, label 3 | 2 of 6 | 3 of 6 | 2 of 6 |

Rejecting one wins on three orders and loses on three. In $(1,2,3)$ it correctly selects the first eligible record, label two, and loses. In $(3,1,2)$ it sacrifices the greatest in the sample and eventually selects the last. Both are losses produced by correct execution.

Use the trace to reveal one comparison at a time. The complete record opens only after selection. A replay helps inspect the policy; it is not another independent draw from the uniform model.

### When to inspect a trace

Use it to check whether each action uses permitted information and follows the rule. Use the complete set of orders to evaluate its probability of success. A single attractive run cannot replace that denominator.

## Derive the finite probability

For a chosen $k\ge1$, let $J$ be the arrival position of the unique greatest. To win at position $j>k$, two things must happen:

- The greatest arrives at $j$. Under uniform order this has probability $1/n$.
- Among the preceding $j-1$ options, their greatest lies in the rejected prefix. There are $k$ prefix positions among those $j-1$ equally likely positions, giving probability $k/(j-1)$ conditional on $J=j$.

Why the second condition? If the best of that preceding set is in the prefix, no intermediate option can beat the prefix leader, so the rule is still waiting when the global greatest arrives. If it is after the prefix, some earlier eligible record has already triggered selection.

These are conditional factors, not an assertion that arbitrary events are independent. Different positions of the greatest are mutually exclusive, so add their winning probabilities:

$$P_k=\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\frac{k}{j-1}=\frac{k}{n}\sum_{h=k}^{n-1}\frac{1}{h},\qquad 1\le k\le n-1.$$

The index $h=j-1$ counts preceding options. A **sum** adds one contribution for each admissible position. For $k=0$, use the separate result $P_0=1/n$; substituting zero into a sum starting at zero would introduce an undefined $1/0$.

**Worked calculation with five options and two opening rejections.** The greatest can arrive at positions three, four or five:

| Greatest's position | Probability of that position | Conditional chance the prefix contains the preceding leader | Winning contribution |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 3 | 1/5 | 2/2 | 1/5 |
| 4 | 1/5 | 2/3 | 2/15 |
| 5 | 1/5 | 2/4 | 1/10 |
| Total |  |  | 13/30 |

Thus $P_2=(2/5)(1/2+1/3+1/4)=13/30$. The greatest is outside the prefix with probability **$1-k/n=3/5$**, not $k/n=2/5$. The latter appears in the algebra after summing conditional terms; it is not the chance of arriving after the sample.

Ferguson's [classical secretary analysis, section two point one](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tom/Stopping/sr2.pdf) derives the threshold family and its finite success formula. His threshold $r$ is the first eligible position, so our rejected count is $k=r-1$. Keeping that indexing difference explicit avoids an off-by-one “disagreement.”

### When to use the formula

Use it for exact best-choice probability under uniform order and the complete stated policy. A ranked outcome table or a numerical-payoff objective needs its own calculation.

## Compare finite cutoffs before rounding

There are $5!=120$ orders for five options. Applying every possible cutoff gives:

| Rejected count k | Fraction rejected | Winning orders | Exact success probability |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 0 | 24 of 120 | 1/5 |
| 1 | 1/5 | 50 of 120 | 5/12 |
| 2 | 2/5 | 52 of 120 | 13/30 |
| 3 | 3/5 | 42 of 120 | 7/20 |
| 4 | 4/5 | 24 of 120 | 1/5 |

The best cutoff is two: rejection fraction $2/5=40\%$, success probability $13/30\approx43.33\%$. Those percentages answer different questions. The winning-order difference between rejecting two and one is only $2/120=1/60$, about $1.67$ percentage points; it is not two additional percentage points or a guarantee on the next run.

Ferguson's optimality argument also establishes that an optimal classical rule can be chosen from this threshold family. The finite comparison then picks its cutoff. Counting five cutoffs alone would not prove optimality over all imaginable rules if that structural argument were absent.

### When the distinction matters

For a small known pool, calculate the finite values. A headline percentage is a useful approximation for the appropriate large-pool model, not a substitute for comparing admissible integers.

## Meet the limit without turning it into a probability factor

Suppose $n$ becomes large while the rejected fraction $x=k/n$ stays between zero and one. The exact harmonic sum is approximated by a logarithm:

$$P_k\approx x\ln(1/x).$$

Here $\ln$ is the **natural logarithm**, the inverse of exponentiation with base $e\approx2.71828$. The expression is an approximation to the complete sum. Its two algebraic factors are not separately named probabilities: $\ln(1/x)$ can exceed one. At $x=1/10$, for example, it is $\ln(10)\approx2.30259$.

<details>
<summary>Locate the peak using calculus</summary>

Writing $x\ln(1/x)=-x\ln x$, its derivative is $-\ln x-1$. It is zero at $x=e^{-1}$. The second derivative is $-1/x<0$, so that point is the unique maximum on $0<x<1$. Substituting gives $e^{-1}\ln(e)=e^{-1}$.

The logarithm approximation comes from the sum of reciprocal terms, not from multiplying “probability after the sample” by a logarithmic probability. No calculus is needed to compare the finite table above.

</details>

As the classical pool grows, both the optimal rejected fraction and the resulting best-choice probability approach $1/e\approx36.79\%$. Their limits agree; their finite values need not. A limiting curve tending to zero at its left endpoint also does not make the finite policy $k=0$ have zero success: it still wins with probability $1/n$.

### When the limit helps

Use it to understand the shape and scale of the classical result, or to get a large-pool starting point. Keep $n$, the allowed integer cutoff and the model assumptions attached to any numerical decision.

## Transfer to another finite horizon

Now use **four options**, retaining uniform order and every other condition. Before opening the solution, compare the two interior cutoffs. Does rounding the same percentage settle which one wins?

<details>
<summary>Check all four cutoffs</summary>

| Rejected count | Exact success probability | Winning orders out of 24 |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 1/4 | 6 |
| 1 | 11/24 | 11 |
| 2 | 5/12 | 10 |
| 3 | 1/4 | 6 |

For one rejection, $(1/4)(1+1/2+1/3)=11/24$. For two, $(2/4)(1/2+1/3)=5/12=10/24$. Rejecting one wins by $1/24$. The rejected fraction is $1/4$, whereas the success probability is $11/24$.

An edge case also matters: with two options, rejecting none and rejecting one each win with probability $1/2$. An optimal cutoff need not be unique. With one option there is only one choice and success is certain; the interior-sum formula is unnecessary.

### When to transfer the reasoning

Carry the setup, the full rule and the counting method to a new horizon. If instead the arrival law changes, uniform-order counts no longer apply. The [later audit workshop](/optimal-stopping-and-the-secretary-problem/06-audit-a-stopping-rule) tests that separate change explicitly.

## Recover the argument before the recap

Without looking back, explain why a winner at position four needs the best of the preceding three to lie inside the prefix. Then reconstruct the five-option rejection fraction and success probability, naming the denominator of each. Finally explain why a logarithm greater than one cannot be a probability factor.

The rule optimizes a narrow best-only objective under a specific information model. It does not establish that the same fraction is suitable for a calendar, a purchase, a relationship or maximizing average quality. Check those objectives and constraints before borrowing its name.
