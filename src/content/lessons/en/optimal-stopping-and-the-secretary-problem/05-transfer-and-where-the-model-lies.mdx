## Hiring and the opening sample

A recruitment process may reveal candidates sequentially, yet allow earlier candidates to remain available. It may reward a strong appointment rather than selecting the unique greatest among a fixed pool. Scheduling may also be purposeful rather than uniformly random. Each feature matters independently; the presence of interviews does not determine the correct policy.

**Constructed policy trace.** Suppose twelve candidates cannot be recalled and a predeclared rule rejects four, then offers to the first later best-so-far. Candidate three leads that opening sample. Candidates five and six do not beat that leader; candidate seven does. The rule offers to candidate seven and ends. This reconstructs an action; it does not show that the cutoff was optimal, that the offer is accepted, or that candidate seven is the greatest globally. Those claims need further assumptions and evidence.

If earlier candidates remain available, record their actual deadlines and availability. Guaranteed free recall until all comparisons are complete supports a different policy: inspect all and choose the greatest under a best-only objective. Partial availability or costly interviews require more modeling. They do not automatically produce a particular longer sample.

## Fast markets and the order of arrival

A rapidly disappearing option can make rejection effectively irreversible. That says something about availability. It does not establish a known pool size, equal probabilities for all orders, reliable rankings or a best-only reward. The same distinction applies to flat viewings, one-time proposals and expiring offers.

**Constructed planning rule.** A draft allows six weekends and expects fifteen viewings, with five initial observations used only for calibration. That is a specified rule to evaluate, not a consequence of the calendar alone. If the best-suited options are systematically scheduled early, rejecting the first five may be particularly costly. If earlier options remain available, a no-recall evaluation is the wrong calculation.

## Numerical offers need a numerical payoff model

Known numerical values create a different information problem from relative rankings. To derive a reservation value, specify the payoff for accepting, the distribution of later offers, the horizon, costs, recall and any outside alternative. Observed comparable prices may inform a distribution; they are not themselves a fully known law of future offers.

A reservation value compares accepting now with the expected value of continuing under the model. Under finite-horizon independent, identically distributed numerical payoffs with forced final selection, the continuation calculation gives time-dependent thresholds. A stationary infinite-horizon problem with appropriate search costs can instead have a constant threshold. “The bar must always fall” is too broad. Ferguson derives the finite numerical case in [section 2.4](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tom/Stopping/sr2.pdf) and a stationary search-cost case in [section 4.1](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tom/Stopping/sr4.pdf).

**Audit a draft calculation.** A draft lists an initial price of 500,000 monetary units and a monthly carrying cost of 4,000, then announces acceptance thresholds of 485,000 and 470,000. Those thresholds do not follow from the two supplied quantities. A price has units of money; the carrying cost has units of money per month. A future-offer distribution and a time/continuation model are missing. The numbers can illustrate a proposed policy, but cannot certify it as optimal.

## The dating headline and its missing assumptions

The familiar headline says to date through a fixed fraction of a lifetime and then choose the next person better than every previous partner. A classical best-choice theorem does not establish that prescription. A known fixed pool, uniform order, no recall, a stable strict ranking, unilateral acceptance and a unique-greatest objective would all need justification. Encounters and mutual choices do not supply those assumptions merely by occurring over time.

The point is a diagnostic one. Ask what an acceptable relationship means to the people involved, what can be known and which choices are mutual. Neither a calendar fraction nor changing the fraction to another number supplies the missing model. A mathematical example is not evidence for a personal timetable.

## When to stop gathering information

Continued research has benefits and costs. A rational continuation calculation asks what further information could change the decision, with what probability and payoff, and at what cost. Depending on the model, that can require evaluating a sequence of future observations rather than only the next one in isolation.

Elapsed time alone does not diagnose procrastination. A three-week comparison may still be missing a decisive requirement, or it may be repeating checks that no longer affect the choice. Name the unresolved question, the observations that could answer it, the cost of delay and the available alternative. That makes the stopping question concrete.

Likewise, “stop an A/B test when it feels good enough” is not a statistical procedure. A test requires its own outcome definitions, decision rule and error assessment. The secretary problem does not supply them.

## Sort assumptions before selecting a policy

Think of a model as a checklist with several required entries. Checking one entry does not fill the others. A sequential irreversible choice may resemble the classical problem while lacking its reward, horizon or arrival law.
