Imagine a game with a known number of designs shown one at a time. You can compare the designs already seen, but receive no absolute quality scores and cannot inspect the future. After each arrival you must accept that design and finish, or reject it permanently. You want the **single greatest** design in the whole set. Assume there are no ties and every arrival order is equally likely. When should you stop?

This is the **classical secretary problem**. It is a particular **optimal stopping** problem: deciding when to act from information revealed sequentially, to optimize a specified reward or cost. The broader field also includes problems with recall, numerical values, costs and other observation rules. Those details determine the solution; irreversibility alone does not determine a sampling fraction.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The classical rule and its boundary">
Under the classical assumptions, an optimal rule rejects an opening group, then selects the first later option better than every option seen. If none qualifies, this course's protocol selects the final one. For large pools, the optimal rejected fraction and success probability both approach $1/e$, approximately 37%. For a finite pool, calculate the integer cutoff rather than require either quantity to equal 37%.
</Callout>

## What the course covers

You will learn to state a sequential decision precisely, calculate a policy under its assumptions, and identify what must change before transferring it. The course map is:

1. **The secretary problem and the 37% rule**: the classical rank-only setup, small cases and the large-pool limit.
2. **Explore, then commit**: an opening sample and a later commitment phase in the cutoff family.
3. **Expected-value objectives**: how rewards and the information scale affect acceptance thresholds.
4. **Variations that matter**: recall, costs, distributional information, unknown horizons and rejectable offers.
5. **Transfer and model limits**: check the real objective, availability and arrival process before borrowing a rule.
6. **[Audit a stopping rule](/optimal-stopping-and-the-secretary-problem/06-audit-a-stopping-rule)**: permitted information, a complete protocol, exact finite scoring and a changed known arrival law.

<Callout variant="info" title="Keep the question precise">
Ask what the chooser can observe, what reward is being optimized and which order law is assumed. Then ask which policies have actually been compared. A precise answer inside one model is useful without being a rule for every search.
</Callout>

## How to use this course

Try the questions before opening the explanations, reproduce worked calculations, and distinguish chooser information from analyst records. The course includes replays, sorting and matching exercises, plus an independent audit workshop. A quiz checks your reasoning; its score is not evidence that a model's assumptions hold in a real decision.

This is an **expert-tier** course. Familiarity with probability, expected value and the value of information will help. A known horizon is the number of opportunities before the decision ends. A record is the current best among what has already been observed; it need not be the greatest globally. After the six teaching lessons, the final exam is one-way: submitting locks each answer and the score appears at the end.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Before borrowing the number">
A deadline or a disappearing offer does not establish uniform random order, a known horizon or a best-only objective. Specify the missing conditions before claiming a numerical optimum. The next lesson derives the classical rule; the audit workshop later checks a complete finite case.
</Callout>
