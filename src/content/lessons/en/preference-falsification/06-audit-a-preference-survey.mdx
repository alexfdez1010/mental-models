Twelve replies arrive from a twenty-person group. Eight say yes. Someone puts “two thirds support the proposal” on a slide. The arithmetic describes the replies; the slide describes the group. Those are different claims, even before anyone has a reason to conceal an opinion.

<Callout variant="info" title="Case conditions">
All people and counts below are invented. The group is fully listed and everyone was invited once. Each person has one fixed private preference: support or opposition to the same unchanged proposal. We first record reports, then state separate assumptions about their truthfulness. There are no survey weights, repeated respondents, random-sampling claims or inferred probabilities of hidden preferences.
</Callout>

## Name the denominator

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="Which description is established by the initial count alone?"
  options={[{"text":"Two thirds of respondents reported support.","correct":true},{"text":"Two thirds of the whole group privately support."},{"text":"All nonrespondents privately oppose."},{"text":"Most nonrespondents privately support."}]}
  explanation="Eight of twelve replies are yes. Whether reports reveal private preferences, and what missing people think, are separate questions."
  pretest={true}
/>

The **population** is the whole group whose preferences you want to describe. A **respondent** is someone who answered. Here the population contains 20 people and the respondent set contains 12. The response fraction is $12/20=3/5=60\%$. Among replies, reported support is $8/12=2/3$. Among all invited people, the fraction who *reported* yes is $8/20=2/5=40\%$.

Changing the denominator changes the question. An unanswered invitation is like an unopened envelope: writing “no” on it does not tell you what was inside. A response rate measures participation, not the size or direction of response bias. AAPOR makes that distinction in its [standard definitions](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/standard-definitions/).

### When to use it

Use the respondent fraction when describing replies. To describe private support in the whole group, you need both a treatment of missing answers and assumptions about what the reports measure.

## Find every compatible total

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="Suppose every returned answer is truthful. Does a respondent majority settle the whole-group majority?"
  options={[{"text":"Yes, because invited people form one group."},{"text":"No, because the unanswered invitations can change it.","correct":true},{"text":"Yes, because truthful answers remove missingness."},{"text":"No, because the returned answers must be false."}]}
  explanation="Truthfulness makes the returned classifications usable. It does not classify the missing people."
  pretest={true}
/>

For this section, **assume returned answers are truthful** and keep the eight missing preferences unrestricted. Let $S$ be the number of private supporters and $z$ the supporters among missing people. Then

$$S=8+z,\qquad z\in\{0,1,\ldots,8\},\qquad 8\leq S\leq 16.$$

The smallest compatible total puts every missing person in opposition. The largest puts every missing person in support. To realize a total of $8+z$ supporters, assign exactly $z$ of the eight missing people to support, with $z$ from zero through eight. These are **identification bounds**: the range of totals consistent with the observations and assumptions. They are sharp here because both endpoints and every intervening integer can be realized. They are not a confidence interval, and compatible totals have not been assigned equal probabilities.

Private support could therefore be 40% through 80%, in increments of one person. A strict majority requires at least 11 of 20; compatible totals exist on both sides. The bounds leave the majority unresolved. Their midpoint is not an estimate supplied by this model.

### When to use it

Use bounds when you can specify what is known without defending a distribution for what is missing. If additional evidence restricts the missing group, derive new bounds and name that evidence.

## Put truthfulness back under examination

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="The questionnaire removes names. What has that change alone established about private preferences?"
  options={[{"text":"That every answer is truthful."},{"text":"That missing people resemble respondents."},{"text":"Neither truthfulness nor representativeness.","correct":true},{"text":"That every answer is deliberately false."}]}
  explanation="Removing names changes part of the reporting setting. It does not validate either assumption from the count alone."
  pretest={true}
/>

Privacy can address a reason to conceal a view. It cannot, by itself, certify sincere answers, complete coverage, unchanged wording or participation. Pew Research Center discusses [socially desirable answers and survey mode](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/); AAPOR recommends [clear questions and allowing people to skip](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/). Neither practice supplies the numerical truthfulness assumptions in our example.

Try a **sensitivity analysis**: change an assumption deliberately and see what conclusions survive. Hypothetically allow at most 2 of the 8 yes reports to come from private opponents, and at most 3 of the 4 no reports to come from private supporters. These caps are stipulated, not estimated from the survey. If $f$ counts the first kind and $g$ the second,

$$S=8-f+g+z,\qquad 0\leq f\leq 2,\quad 0\leq g\leq 3,\quad 0\leq z\leq 8.$$

All counts are integers. The minimum is $8-2+0+0=6$; the maximum is $8-0+3+8=19$. The range widens to 30%–95%. With no restriction on the truthfulness of any report, all totals from 0 through 20 are compatible. More decimal places on the respondent percentage cannot narrow those sets.

### When to use it

Use sensitivity analysis to expose which conclusion depends on a contested assumption. A narrower range is useful only if the stronger assumption is defensible; it is not a prize for choosing convenient caps.

## Resolve a rule without learning the exact total

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="A rule needs support at or above its announced threshold. When can counting stop?"
  options={[{"text":"Only when every person has answered."},{"text":"When every total still compatible gives the same decision.","correct":true},{"text":"As soon as most replies agree."},{"text":"When the respondent percentage stops changing."}]}
  explanation="A decision can be resolved before the exact total is known. The remaining compatible totals must all lie on the same side of the stated rule."
  pretest={true}
/>

Return to the truthful-answer model. The group has already announced a rule: approve if private support is **at least 12**. This lesson audits that rule; it does not justify the threshold. Preferences and wording stay fixed. Each successful follow-up reaches a distinct missing person, obtains one truthful answer, and costs 3 exercise credits (cr). A refusal stays missing.

After $k$ completed follow-ups, of which $x$ report yes,

$$8+x\leq S\leq 16-k+x.$$

A new yes raises the lower bound by one; a new no lowers the upper bound by one. Both shrink the width by one, conditional on truthfulness. Approve when the lower bound reaches 12. Reject when the upper bound is below 12. Otherwise the record does not resolve this rule. Continue only if participation is voluntary and the remaining budget permits it.

After five replies, both 11 and 12 supporters remain possible, so the rule is unresolved. After the sixth reply, which is yes, even the smallest possible total meets the rule. There might be 12, 13 or 14 supporters; each approves. No probability estimate is needed.

Eight successful follow-ups would classify every missing person and cost 24 credits. Some sequences need all eight; the six-reply trace costs 18. This is conditional accounting, not a forecast of response or honesty. With a 15-credit budget, this trace ends unresolved. A larger budget cannot force someone to reply.

### When to use it

Use this stopping condition when the decision threshold is fixed and the bounds are defensible. If preferences change during follow-up or answers are unreliable, rebuild the model rather than treating the same arithmetic as valid.

## Try a different group

A different fully listed group has 15 people, 5 yes reports, 6 no reports and 4 missing answers. Its announced rule requires at least 8 supporters. Assume truthful returned and follow-up answers, one fixed question and fixed private preferences. Three distinct missing people then answer yes, no, yes. Work out the new bounds before opening the solution.
