You know a catalogue tool's menus by heart. Now someone asks you to validate links between files after its rules change. The interface is familiar; the judgment may need different evidence.

Your **circle of competence** is a way to ask which tasks your understanding and experience support, under which conditions. Its drawn edge is an analogy. A radius you select on a diagram cannot measure your ability or make a decision safe. Here you will compare a task record with a forecast about that record, and see why improving one number need not improve the other.

## Separate familiarity, performance and confidence

Knowing a theatre's seating plan does not establish that you can repair its lighting desk. The place is the same, but the claims require different demonstrations. The analogy concerns task scope; it does not say that a skilled person must fail at an unfamiliar task.

| Quantity | Meaning here | Evidence that can address it |
|---|---|---|
| Familiarity | Recognising the tool, words or setting | Exposure history or a recognition task |
| Task performance | What the person produces under stated conditions | Work checked against a declared criterion |
| Stated probability of success | A forecast that this attempt will meet that criterion | A probability recorded before the result, then scored against it |
| Estimated relative standing | Where someone thinks they rank among a specified peer group | A separate ranking estimate and comparable peer results |

“Likely to answer correctly” and “better than most peers” are different claims. A class can find a task difficult while its strongest participant still gets many items wrong. Conversely, a person can get many right without outperforming peers on an easy task.

**Competence is task-specific support for a judgment, not a verdict on a person's worth.** General intelligence, a confident manner or an impressive title does not settle which task conditions are covered. Nor does unfamiliarity guarantee that a person will recognise the gap, decline the task or avoid every consequence.

### When to use the distinction

Use it when a request changes the task while preserving familiar vocabulary. State the result to be checked, the conditions and the allowed support. An explanation can help assess a mechanism; a work sample can assess a performance. Neither should silently stand in for all the other claims.

## Start with a complete task record

Consider **constructed teaching records**, not a study of a real person. Maya attempts two selected sets of catalogue tasks. The familiar set copies a code from a single source row; the different set validates a cross-file code mapping and flags a mismatch. Both use written rules, but involve different operations.

Before the attempts, a reviewer fixes the answer key and the criterion: an output must match the required code and the required match/mismatch flag. Every first attempt is included once. The written manual is allowed; no case-specific hints are given. A correct result is coded **y=1** and an incorrect one **y=0**. The table groups IDs only to save space; it omits none.

| Set | IDs | Correctness y | Number of attempts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Familiar copying | F01–F14 | 1 | 14 |
| Familiar copying | F15–F20 | 0 | 6 |
| Cross-file validation | N01–N08 | 1 | 8 |
| Cross-file validation | N09–N20 | 0 | 12 |

Thus familiar correctness is $14/20=7/10$ and different-task correctness is $8/20=2/5$. The difference is $7/10-2/5=3/10$, or **30 percentage points**. That describes these selected attempts. It does not identify a causal effect of familiarity, a person's true success probability or an exact competence boundary. The operations and cases differ.

### A denominator is not a certificate

A folder containing only successful outputs would not establish this all-attempt rate. A prompted correction would be another support condition. Even a complete record can omit the case types that matter next. Use the counts to locate a question for further assessment, not to label the whole person.

## Score the forecast without changing the work

Before **each** attempt in both sets, Maya recorded probability $p=9/10$ that the output would meet the criterion without case-specific hints. This is a forecast about the attempt, not a confidence rating for her whole career. After recording a result, do not rewrite that forecast.

A probability expresses the assessed chance of a defined event on a scale from zero to one. Zero assigns no chance and one assigns certainty; the stated fraction here is ninety percent. It does not promise that exactly nine of every ten attempts must succeed.

A scoring rule can compare a probability p with the binary result y. Here use the **mean squared probability error**, also called the binary Brier score under this convention:

$$S=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_i-y_i)^2.$$

Here n is the number of recorded attempts; p_i is the probability recorded for attempt i and y_i is its binary result. Sum the squared errors and divide by n.

The score is dimensionless and ranges from zero to one. Lower is better **on the same results under this rule**. It evaluates probability forecasts, not all dimensions of competence or the consequences of a mistake. It is not a calibration diagnosis by itself. [ECMWF's definition of the mean squared probability error](https://confluence.ecmwf.int/spaces/FUG/pages/673551875/Section+12.B+Statistical+Concepts+-+Probabilistic+Data).

Work one result at a time. At the stated probability, a correct output contributes $(9/10-1)^2=1/100$, while an incorrect output contributes $(9/10-0)^2=81/100$. For a pair with one of each, the mean is $(1/100+81/100)/2=41/100$. A confident error receives a larger loss; a possible error does not make the original probability logically impossible.

Now use the different-task record.

| Result group | Records | Error per record | Contribution to the mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| Correct | 8 | 0.01 | (8/20) × 0.01=0.004 |
| Incorrect | 12 | 0.81 | (12/20) × 0.81=0.486 |

Add the contributions before choosing a candidate.

| Candidate | Mean squared error |
|---|---|
| Maple | 0.49 |
| Ash | 0.25 |
| Elm | 0.24 |

## A fitted probability still needs a later check

Before the original results, a reference rule was also fixed: assign probability 0.5 to every attempt. After seeing the different-task results, an analyst chooses another constant, 0.4, because it equals that batch's observed correct share. **This last candidate is fitted after seeing the same records on which it is scored.** It was not Maya's original forecast.

Keep all outputs fixed and compare the candidates:

| Result set | Correct / total | Score at p=0.9 | Score at p=0.5 | Score at p=0.4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Familiar copying | 14/20 | 0.25 | 0.25 | 0.30 |
| Cross-file validation | 8/20 | 0.49 | 0.25 | 0.24 |

At p=0.5, either outcome contributes 0.25. At p=0.4, a correct result contributes 0.36 and an incorrect result 0.16. For the different set, the fitted candidate gives **(8×0.36+12×0.16)/20=0.24**, lower than the other candidates on those records. The work still has eight correct outputs.

Notice another separation: the original p=0.9 scores 0.25 on the familiar set, the same score as the p=0.5 reference there. A relatively high observed correctness rate does not by itself demonstrate better probability forecasts. These two small, selected sets do not identify true long-run calibration.

### A closed transfer to a later batch

Freeze all three candidates before seeing a separate later batch of validation tasks. Its complete record has **L01–L18 correct** and **L19–L20 incorrect**, twenty first attempts in total. No later outcomes were used to change the candidate probabilities.

## What the studies actually tested

The **illusion of explanatory depth** concerns overestimating how fully one can explain a mechanism. In Rozenblit and Keil's 2002 device studies, participants rated understanding, attempted detailed explanations and diagnostic questions, and rated it again. The studied groups' ratings fell after attempts to explain; the paper also reports differences between kinds of knowledge. This supports using a relevant explanation as one probe. It does not show that every familiar object is misunderstood, that one awkward explanation proves practical inability, or that explanation is the only test. [Published article, Yale-hosted copy](https://cogdevlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/rozenblit%20%26%20keil%20%202002.pdf).

**Kruger and Dunning's 1999 studies** compared estimated and actual performance in selected humour, grammar and logic tasks. Some estimates concerned relative standing among peers; others concerned the number of items answered correctly. A percentile ranking is not the same as a probability of success on the next item.

Lower-performing groups showed substantial overestimation in those tasks. A larger **gap between estimate and performance** does not mean the largest **absolute confidence**, and the study is not a universal confidence-versus-knowledge curve. The authors explicitly describe degrees and domain-specific performance rather than a categorical line dividing competent and incompetent people. Their limitations also distinguish tasks and acknowledge other sources of overestimation. A low score on our invented catalogue batch cannot diagnose an individual's psychological mechanism. [Published article, university-hosted copy](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/sasi/wp-content/uploads/sites/275/2015/11/krugerdunning99.pdf).

### Use the finding as a question

When certainty exceeds its supporting evidence, specify the missing check rather than naming a bias from confidence alone. Relevant performance, clear criteria, feedback and suitable review can inform the next claim. A person can also be uncertain while performing well; the aim is warranted confidence, not compulsory self-doubt.

## Choose the next check for the actual task

Nico has copied catalogue codes correctly using a supplied mapping. A colleague now asks Nico to decide whether ambiguous pairs of identifiers refer to the same item, without that mapping, and to overwrite the live links. Nico says the interface feels easy.

Before reading the answer, name the new judgment, the evidence missing from the earlier record, and a way to preserve useful work while getting the required check.

<details>
<summary>Compare your scope decision</summary>

The new judgment is identity resolution without a supplied mapping, not copying known codes. The earlier record does not assess ambiguous matches under those support conditions or authority to overwrite links. Nico can prepare a proposed mapping on a copy and seek review from someone who can assess the matching criteria, before a responsible person authorises a live change. Check that review is available and consider the delay and consequences of unresolved links. Neither familiarity nor a small forecast error grants the missing task evidence or authority.
