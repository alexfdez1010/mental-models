You regularly judge whether a familiar supplier can meet a deadline. Now someone asks you to assess a new manufacturing process. Both questions involve suppliers; the evidence needed to answer them may be quite different. Mapping your **circle of competence** means specifying which decisions your knowledge and experience support, under which conditions, and where you need help.

In his [1996 shareholder letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1996.html), Warren Buffett emphasised knowing the boundary of the businesses he could evaluate. Here we apply that boundary question to everyday judgments. The checks below provide evidence for a provisional map; none is a certificate of expertise.

## Keep score with a comparison

Keep a forecast before the outcome is known. A **forecast** is a statement about an outcome you will later check. A useful entry defines the event, its deadline, your probability, the evidence available now and how you will resolve it. For example: “All parts in order R arrive and pass the agreed acceptance check by Thursday at 16:00; probability 80%.” “The supplier does well” leaves too much room to rewrite success afterwards.

Then record every resolved forecast, including misses. Keep different tasks separate: routine deliveries with an established supplier are not interchangeable with an unfamiliar process or a new supplier. If several forecasts depend on the same factory shutdown, one event can make them all wrong; they are not independent pieces of evidence about your ability.

### Work through the scorecard

In this scenario, a coordinator records ten forecasts in each of two tasks. Before seeing the outcomes, the coordinator also chooses a **reference rule**: predict that every order will arrive on time. Apply both methods to the same cases within each task.

For this table, each method makes an on-time or late call; count a hit when that call matches the outcome. The probability estimates are scored separately below.

| Task | Correct coordinator forecasts | Coordinator hit rate | Correct reference forecasts | Difference in correct forecasts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Routine orders | 9 out of 10 | 9 ÷ 10 = 90% | 9 out of 10 | 9 − 9 = 0 |
| Custom orders | 4 out of 10 | 4 ÷ 10 = 40% | 5 out of 10 | 4 − 5 = −1 |

The routine-order record is accurate in this sample, but it shows no advantage over the chosen rule. The custom-order record is a reason to inspect the judgment and reduce reliance on it; it does not, by itself, locate an exact boundary of competence. Ten cases leave substantial uncertainty, and a difference could reflect the cases selected, shared influences or chance. Statistical methods for proportions explicitly account for sample size; [NIST describes why small samples need care](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section2/prc241.htm).

**Accuracy** asks how often the forecasts were right. **Added predictive skill** asks how they performed against a relevant reference. Forecast evaluation makes this distinction explicitly, for example in [ECMWF's verification guide](https://confluence.ecmwf.int/spaces/FUG/pages/673551834/Section%2B12.A%2BStatistical%2BConcepts%2B-%2BDeterministic%2BData). Neither measure alone establishes competence at diagnosing causes, making repairs or choosing a safe action.

### Compare confidence with outcomes

**Calibration** concerns whether stated probabilities match observed frequencies over comparable forecasts. If many forecasts assigned an 80% probability occur about 80% of the time, that group is well calibrated. That differs from getting every one right. It also differs from beating a reference: a forecast can reflect a common frequency without identifying which individual case will fail.

The short lab below lets you state confidence in answers and then compare it with your accuracy here. Its graph groups answers by confidence; a point below the diagonal means fewer correct answers than that confidence level in this exercise. Even when overall average confidence matches accuracy, separate confidence groups can miss in opposite directions. With so few answers, a point can move sharply after one mistake. It cannot assess your competence in a profession.

The **[Brier score](https://confluence.ecmwf.int/spaces/FUG/pages/673551875/Section%2B12.B%2BStatistical%2BConcepts%2B-%2BProbabilistic%2BData)** displayed alongside the graph averages squared probability errors; lower is better on the same cases. For the binary events here, it ranges from zero to one and has no units. Assigning probability 0.8 to a true claim contributes (0.8 − 1)² = 0.04; assigning it to a false claim contributes (0.8 − 0)² = 0.64. Those two contributions average to (0.04 + 0.64) ÷ 2 = 0.34. The score penalises a confident mistake more than a tentative one; it measures overall probability accuracy rather than calibration alone.

In the lab, confidence refers to your chosen answer. Answering “false” with 80% confidence assigns a 20% probability to the claim being true.

### When to use it

Use scorekeeping for recurring judgments with outcomes you can resolve. Define the reference and success criterion in advance, and review whether the cases resemble the decision now facing you. Avoid inventing a universal pass mark: the evidence needed for a reversible trial differs from that needed for a costly commitment.

## Ask what you would need to know

<MCQ

The **“what would I need to know?” test** exposes gaps before you have a long record. Specify the decision first, then list the facts and methods that could change it. Distinguish questions you can answer, questions requiring data and questions you cannot yet evaluate. Recognising a gap is a reason to seek help, not a reason to abandon learning.

For a supplier's new batch, the question might be “Can we accept it for this use?” Delivery punctuality will not answer that alone. You need the agreed **tolerances**—the permitted differences from the specification—how measurements were taken, whether the sampled parts represent this batch, and who can interpret a failed check. Knowing that “quality matters” does not supply those details.

| Question | Available support | Consequence for the decision |
|---|---|---|
| Did this shipment arrive by the agreed time? | Timestamp and order terms | You can check punctuality directly |
| Do the measured dimensions meet this specification? | Measurement records and an understood specification | You can compare them within the measurement's limits |
| Does the new process create an unfamiliar failure mode? | You lack the relevant process expertise | Ask a qualified specialist before accepting that claim |

### Pitfall and use

A polished checklist can be copied without understanding it. Test your list against a specification, a worked failure case or a knowledgeable review. Use this check to scope an investigation and decide who should join it; avoid promoting “I can ask sensible questions” into “I can independently judge all the answers”.

## Explain a mechanism and test its limits

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="A colleague explains a process clearly. What would strengthen the explanation as evidence of understanding?"
  options={[
    { text: 'A prediction about a relevant change, checked against evidence.', correct: true },
    { text: 'The same explanation delivered with fewer pauses and more certainty.' },
    { text: 'A longer list of specialists who use the same technical vocabulary.' },
    { text: 'A familiar example repeated until the audience can recite it.' }
  ]}
  explanation="A clear explanation is useful, but fluency can hide a gap. A checkable prediction under a relevant change tests whether the proposed mechanism does further work."
/>

The **teach test** asks you to explain an idea in plain language, identify the steps that connect cause to result and say where the explanation stops applying. You can use established knowledge; you do not have to rediscover an entire field from basic physics. Technical terms are helpful when defined and used precisely.

Consider a print queue: one machine processes jobs in order, one at a time. A second queued job cannot finish before the first has released the machine. That explains why a new job can wait even if its own printing time is short. Now change the setting: the shop adds another machine, but only one can handle the required paper size. The claim “two machines halve the wait” needs a new check of which jobs each machine can actually serve.

The explanation identifies a **bottleneck**, the resource limiting the flow of work, and an assumption. To test it, compare the actual job sequence, machine eligibility and timestamps. A smooth story about “more resources” would miss the compatibility constraint. Conversely, someone who struggles to explain this aloud may need a better communication method; one awkward explanation does not prove a lack of practical skill.

### When to use it

Use the teach test when a term sounds familiar but you cannot say what changes the outcome. Write the conditions, predict a consequence of changing one, and check it. For tasks that depend on performance, include a representative task or demonstration alongside the explanation.

## Review the process as well as the result

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="An unfamiliar supplier delivers successfully after someone selected it on a hunch. What does the result establish?"
  options={[
    { text: 'This delivery succeeded; the selection method still needs evaluation.', correct: true },
    { text: 'The hunch reliably detects capable suppliers across future orders.' },
    { text: 'The supplier will fail next time because the selection was informal.' },
    { text: 'A successful delivery removes the need to examine the original evidence.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The result is real evidence about this delivery. It does not reveal whether the hunch had reliable information, nor establish how it will perform on future cases."
/>

**Outcome bias** means letting the eventual result distort your assessment of a decision's quality. Review what was knowable when the decision was made: the alternatives, relevant evidence, uncertainty and possible costs of failure. Then examine the outcome too. A reasonable process can still need improvement if it repeatedly misses something important.

Suppose a planner selects a supplier because a friend liked its logo. The order happens to arrive correctly. Another planner checks the agreed capacity, a representative sample and recent deliveries, but an unexpected transport disruption delays the order. The first outcome is better; that does not make logo preference a more reliable method. Nor does the second planner's documented process make every future delay acceptable. Ask whether the disruption risk could reasonably have been anticipated or reduced.

### Pitfall and use

Do not declare a favourable outcome “pure luck” merely because you dislike the explanation. Investigate what produced it. Equally, do not discard adverse outcomes to protect your preferred method. Compare the written reasons with repeated results and revise the method when evidence warrants it.

## Make the map specific enough to use

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest

  options={[

    { text: 'Apply her backend technical rubric unchanged to the design portfolio.' },
    { text: 'Let her management title substitute for evidence about design judgment.' },
    { text: 'Assume that needing design help removes all value from her hiring experience.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The decision contains different tasks. Relevant hiring experience can support process checks, while evaluating design work needs evidence and expertise specific to that work."
/>

In this scenario, Maya's map is a set of claims about tasks, rather than a badge saying “good at hiring”. Her backend experience includes reviewing representative work, discussing trade-offs and checking later performance against written expectations. She still needs to consider whether a new role or technology changes the task.

| Task in the design hire | Evidence Maya can use | Action now | Reason to reopen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clarify role responsibilities and interview steps | Experience running the hiring process | Coordinate the process with the team | Responsibilities or evaluation criteria change |
| Assess design trade-offs in a portfolio | Limited relevant experience | Obtain a qualified design review | The reviewer cannot explain the assessment or its limits |
| Predict success in this exact role | Incomplete role-specific outcome record | Make expectations explicit and retain uncertainty | Later work conflicts with the expectations |

Bringing in a specialist does not mean accepting an unexplained verdict. Check that their experience matches the task, ask what evidence supports their assessment and resolve disagreements about the criteria. Maya can coordinate these checks while leaving a technical judgment with someone better equipped to make it.

### A review rule that can change an action

Record **the task you will judge, the conditions your evidence covers, the help required and the trigger for another review**. For Maya, a change from a familiar backend role to a design role triggers specialist involvement before the technical assessment. For the supplier coordinator, a new process or conflicting delivery evidence triggers a review before making another firm commitment. These are observable changes, unlike “review when I feel less confident”.
