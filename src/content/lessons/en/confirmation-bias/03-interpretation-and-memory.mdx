The previous lesson examined evidence search with the Wason task. A result’s value came from comparing rival predictions under the stated conditions, rather than counting accepted or rejected examples. An observed ‘does not fit’ is not automatically a refutation: it matters whether that result conflicts with the named candidate.

Now suppose the evidence has arrived. You can copy its numbers accurately, evaluate its methods unevenly, and later remember a different selection of cases. Those are separate things to check. Calling everyone who disagrees with you “biased” saves time, but produces a fairly terrible audit.

This lesson follows two **constructed signage cases**, not findings from a real visitor study. You will compare reports, inspect a complete event log and test whether a later recollection represents it. Alongside them, we will read actual psychological findings with their sampling and measurement limits intact.

## Keep the record separate from its reading

Imagine an archive with documents, marginal notes and an index. The document is what was recorded; the note is what a reader makes of it; the index determines what is easy to retrieve later. An inaccurate index can hide a document without changing the document. The limit of the analogy matters: real records can also contain errors. We must check their accuracy rather than declare paper infallible.

Here are four layers of an audit:

| Layer | Definition | Example in our invented pilot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Factual record | A stated observation, with its source and conditions | Two of twenty visitors assigned A took a wrong turn |
| Interpretation | A claim about what observations mean | A may be easier to follow |
| Evidence quality | How well the design and measurement support that claim | Were visitors assigned randomly, and were all wrong turns counted? |
| Recall | What a person later retrieves about the observations | The reader remembers a visitor who got lost |

The count does not by itself establish why those visitors turned incorrectly. Conversely, “the visitors were inexperienced” is a proposed explanation, not a newly observed fact. Check whether experience was measured and whether the alternative account makes different predictions.

**Worked distinction.** A reviewer writes, “A had fewer wrong turns, so A always works better.” The first part can match a sample record. The second jumps from that record to a universal conclusion. Another reviewer may correctly reject the universal claim while accepting the counts. Their disagreement is about the inference, not necessarily the facts or their honesty.

### When this distinction helps

Use it when an argument jumps between “what happened,” “what it means” and “what I remember.” Ask the speaker to name the layer. A complete log can repair an omitted denominator; it cannot, by itself, settle every explanation of the events.

## Audit the standard as well as the result

**Biased assimilation** means evaluating evidence in a way that systematically favors an existing position beyond what relevant differences in evidence quality justify. **Motivated reasoning** concerns reasoning shaped by a desired conclusion. The first describes a pattern; the second proposes a role for motivation. One methodological objection does not establish either.

Think of two reports passing through a quality checkpoint. The same relevant requirements should apply to comparable designs even when their conclusions swap. This does not mean every report deserves the same verdict: a broken measurement procedure is a good reason to give one less weight.

Our invented reports test signs A and B. In each report, forty different visitors are randomly assigned, twenty to each sign. A wrong turn is defined in advance as entering the marked incorrect corridor before reaching the desk. The observer records every assigned visitor; there are no missing outcomes. The reports use the same procedure and independent visitors. Lower wrong-turn rates are preferable. These assumptions are supplied for the exercise, not verified fieldwork.

| Report | A wrong turns / visitors | B wrong turns / visitors | A rate | B rate | A minus B |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First | 2 / 20 | 6 / 20 | 10% | 30% | −20 percentage points |
| Second | 6 / 20 | 2 / 20 | 30% | 10% | +20 percentage points |
| Combined | 8 / 40 | 8 / 40 | 20% | 20% | 0 percentage points |

Before switching the chart to the combined record, add **events to events and visitors to visitors**. For A, $(2+6)/(20+20)=8/40=0.20$. For B, $(6+2)/(20+20)=8/40=0.20$. The equal denominators also make an average of the two percentages work here; that shortcut need not work with unequal group sizes.

A **sample** is the set of visitors observed. A claim about a **population** concerns the wider set of visits we want the findings to describe. A sample rate and a claim about future visitors are different statements.

**Worked review.** Lee favors A. Lee accepts the first report's sample size as adequate for the comparison, then rejects the second *solely because its identical sample size is too small*. Under the supplied equal-method conditions, that is an inconsistent standard. Requiring larger samples can be sensible; Lee must apply that requirement to both reports. If a missing-outcome problem were discovered only in the second, treating them differently could be justified.

The pattern is compatible with defending a preferred answer. It does not establish Lee's private motive. Nor does it show that every criticism of a small study is biased. Audit the stated reasons, ask about relevant differences, and check whether the standard survives a swap of the favored label.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Different questions can warrant different conclusions">
One reader may care about wrong turns; another may need signs readable from a wheelchair. Our pilot measures the former. Disagreement about an unmeasured requirement is not proof of biased assimilation. First agree on the claim being evaluated and which evidence bears on it.
</Callout>

Training can help you assess technical methods, but credentials do not substitute for checking this particular review. Nor does failing to show immunity establish that more intelligence necessarily causes more bias. Inspect the reasons and the record.

### When to use the swap check

Use it when praise and criticism seem to follow the result rather than the methods. Write the quality criteria before uncovering the direction of the result, where practical. Blinding can help expose a double standard; it does not repair missing data or guarantee an unbiased judgment.

## Read the classic study with its limits

In **Lord, Ross and Lepper's study (1979)**, researchers selected **48 Stanford undergraduates from 151 screened**: twenty-four capital-punishment proponents and twenty-four opponents with strong prior attitudes and relevant beliefs. They examined two purported deterrence studies with opposing conclusions. Study designs, conclusions and presentation order were counterbalanced. This was a selected student sample, and participants did not all receive an identical version of the materials.

On average, participants evaluated congenial evidence more favorably and reported changes toward their own side. Crucially, **polarization was assessed through self-reported changes**: endpoint problems prevented the intended direct before–after comparison on the original scales. The authors discussed whether participants could be reporting changes they thought appropriate rather than actual attitude movement. The article also reports a parallel pattern on running attitude records using enlarged scales. That corroboration does not remove the limits of the selected sample or self-reported measures. [Original article](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/jpsp-1979-Lord-Ross-Lepper.pdf)

**Attitude polarization** is movement toward more opposed or more extreme positions, with the particular measure and comparison specified. It is an outcome, distinct from biased assimilation as a proposed evaluative process. A reader can apply uneven standards without changing their stated position; two readers can disagree after applying defensible standards.

Imagine two thermometers: one measures how a report is rated, the other measures an attitude. A movement on the first dial does not automatically establish movement on the second. The analogy stops there: psychological ratings also depend on the instructions and response scale, not just an underlying quantity waiting to be read.

**Worked evidence boundary.** “These selected groups reported changes toward their own positions under this procedure” is supported. “Every opponent became more extreme” is not established by a group average. “Any pair of readers must polarize after mixed evidence” goes further still. The study also does not establish how any particular person would respond to new evidence today.

Later primary work by **Kuhn and Lao (1996)** distinguished becoming more extreme from articulating a position that started neutral. Its abstract reports genuine polarization as real but infrequent in their research. That is a reason to examine conditions and measures, rather than turn a classic result into a universal law. We use the published abstract here, not an unexamined claim about its full methods. [Primary journal abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.1996.tb00340.x/)

### When this research is useful

Use it to generate audit questions: were comparable reports judged differently, and how was attitude change measured? It does not tell you to split every disagreement down the middle. Stronger evidence may deserve more weight, and a warranted conclusion can be unpopular.

## Compare a memory with the complete log

**Selective recall** is retrieval of some encountered information rather than all of it. Selection can distort a judgment if the retrieved subset is treated as representative. Whether congenial or incongruent information is better remembered depends on the task and conditions; neither direction is a universal memory rule.

Think of reconstructing a day's visits from a few bookmarks. The bookmarks tell you which pages you found, not how common each kind of page was. Unlike a literal bookmark, recollection can also alter a detail, which is why source checking matters.

Here is a **separate invented afternoon**, using signs C and D and the same wrong-turn definition as above. Each row is one visitor and one recorded outcome. For this exercise the log is complete and accurate. C was used in the first time block and D in the second; visitors were **not randomly assigned**. A reader saw every row, then, the next day, recalled exactly **C1, C5, D1 and D2**. This subset is stipulated for arithmetic practice, not a measured memory finding.

**Worked calculation.** Full rates are $4/8=50\%$ for C and $2/8=25\%$ for D. The observed difference C minus D is **+25 percentage points**. Recalled-subset rates are $1/2=50\%$ and $2/2=100\%$: **−50 percentage points**. Restricting the record to recalled visits changes the event counts and denominators, reversing the comparison. It does not change the underlying log.

The full record is the right source for this afternoon's observed rates. It does **not** prove that D caused fewer wrong turns: the time blocks could differ in visitor experience, lighting or other conditions. Neither subset establishes future population rates.

### Which events survived retrieval?

Now change the question. A wrong-turn rate asks, “Of included visits, how many had a wrong turn?” A **recall rate** asks, “Of previously recorded events of a specified kind, how many were recalled?” The denominators are different.

| Event kind | C recalled / originally recorded | C recall rate | D recalled / originally recorded | D recall rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wrong-turn visits | 1 / 4 | 25% | 2 / 2 | 100% |
| Correct-route visits | 1 / 4 | 25% | 0 / 6 | 0% |

For C, $1/4=25\%$ of each event kind was recalled. For D, every wrong-turn visit and no correct-route visit was recalled. That documents the selection in this example. It does not establish why it happened. Even if the reader already preferred C, the same pattern alone could not distinguish motivated retrieval from attention, retrieval context or other explanations.

A claim that D3 took a wrong turn would be a different problem: **a recalled detail conflicts with the stipulated log**. Omitting D3 is a missing event; changing D3's outcome is an inaccurate detail. Check both. In a real audit, also check whether the log itself could be wrong.

### Why the direction of memory bias needs evidence

In **Waldum and Sahakyan's experiments (2012)**, memory for political statements varied with the task and context. Their first experiment used sixty-four undergraduates. In its remember-instruction condition, statements inconsistent with participants' party identification were recalled better on average than consistent ones. The experiments also examined forgetting instructions and recognition conditions. This is a bounded counterexample to the rule “confirming information is always better remembered,” not proof of the reverse rule. [Original author-hosted article](https://sahakyanlab.weebly.com/uploads/1/2/4/2/124249919/waldum_sahakyan_2012.pdf)

Our signage recollection is an invented selection. It illustrates a denominator problem; the political-statement experiments are actual evidence about memory under their tested conditions. Keeping those roles separate avoids laundering an example into a research result.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Use the record without promising a cure">
A dated log with all eligible cases, definitions and sources lets you check a remembered impression against a fuller record. It helps only to the extent that the log and its sampling are adequate. Counting a selectively collected set more carefully can still produce a selective answer.
</Callout>

## Transfer the audit to a new record

Try this without reopening the worked solution. At an invented evening event using the same wrong-turn definition, allocation to signs E and F was not randomized. Assume the recalled events genuinely occurred and the original log is complete and accurate. Each fraction below gives wrong turns divided by included visits.

| Selection | E wrong turns / visits | F wrong turns / visits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Complete log | 4 / 10 | 6 / 20 |
| Recalled subset | 1 / 2 | 3 / 4 |

Write down four rates, the full-log E-minus-F contrast and the recalled-subset contrast. Then answer: which record supports the claim about that evening, and what does it leave undecided?

<details>
<summary>Compare your calculation with the full solution</summary>

The complete rates are $4/10=40\%$ and $6/20=30\%$: E minus F is **+10 percentage points**. F has more wrong-turn events but a lower observed rate because it has twice as many visits. The recalled rates are $1/2=50\%$ and $3/4=75\%$: E minus F is **−25 percentage points**. The selection reverses the comparison. Use the complete log for the evening's observed rates. Nonrandom allocation leaves causal explanations open; neither the arithmetic nor the recall pattern identifies a motive or establishes future rates.

</details>

### When to stop and ask for more evidence

If the source omits outcomes, the comparison uses different targets, or the causal question was never tested, record the gap. You can sometimes repair a calculation immediately. You cannot manufacture the missing observations by being fair-minded. More information may reduce disagreement, leave it unchanged or expose a new issue; none of those outcomes follows from volume alone.
