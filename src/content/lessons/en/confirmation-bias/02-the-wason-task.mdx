A machine tells you that **2, 4, 6** fits a hidden rule. You suspect “even numbers going up by two”. You offer **8, 10, 12**. It fits too. Encouraging—but what did that answer separate your idea from?

The **Wason rule-discovery task** makes that question inspectable. The previous lesson named biased search: gathering evidence in a way that favours a belief. Here we need a finer distinction. A test you expect to pass can be informative. A result labelled “does not fit” can agree with your hypothesis. The useful comparison is between explicit predictions and the evidence actually obtained.

## State candidates before running the test

A **candidate hypothesis** is a proposed rule. A **prediction** is what that candidate says should happen for a specified input. The **observed result** is the hidden rule's actual answer. Keep the three columns separate: proposed rule, predicted answer, observed answer.

For this exercise, the supplied triple fits and the hidden rule is stable. The program gives accurate answers about whether your input fits that rule. You can test finite numbers and then choose from this declared menu:

| Candidate | Precise rule | Why the supplied triple fits |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow evens | All three numbers are even and each is two greater than the previous one | The values are even and both gaps are two |
| Equal gaps | The values strictly increase and both gaps are equal | Both gaps are two and the order increases |
| Increasing | Each value is strictly greater than the previous one | The order increases |
| Even sum | The total of the three values is even; order is irrelevant | The total is twelve |

The menu makes the final choice bounded. It is not a claim that these are the only rules anyone could invent. For instance, “accept these examples but reject an untried triple” could remain compatible with a finite record. We will return to that limit.

Before each test, name your current candidate and write its prediction on paper or say it to yourself. The island records **inputs and observed results**, not your hypothesis or expectations. Its result counts cannot score how you searched.

The exercise uses the increasing rule. Peter Wason's [1960 paper](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/wason-qjep1960.pdf) reported that **six of 29 participants** reached the correct rule without an earlier incorrect announcement. That describes those participants in that study. It does not estimate your competence or show that this lesson guarantees a change in reasoning.

## Distinguish a negative prediction from a negative result

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A candidate predicts rejection and the program rejects the input. Has that result contradicted the candidate?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; the observation agrees with its prediction.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; every observed rejection refutes the current candidate.' },
    { text: 'Yes; choosing a difficult-looking test makes disagreement automatic.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The word negative can describe either an expected category or an observed answer. Refutation concerns a mismatch between a stated prediction and a valid observation, not a particular button label."
/>

A **positive hypothesis test** examines a case the current candidate predicts will fit. A **negative hypothesis test** examines one it predicts will not fit. These labels are relative to the candidate and are fixed **before** feedback. They do not describe whether the numbers are positive, whether the result is useful, or whether the experimenter replies yes.

With the narrow-even candidate, an ascending odd triple is a negative test. With the equal-gap candidate, that same odd triple can be a positive test. Changing the candidate changes the label; changing the observed answer does not rewrite the prediction you made.

Under the exercise's stable rule and accurate feedback, there are four possible prediction–observation relationships:

| Candidate prediction | Observed answer | Relationship to this candidate |
|---|---|---|
| Fits | Fits | Agrees; uniqueness is not established |
| Fits | Does not fit | Contradicts the candidate |
| Does not fit | Fits | Contradicts the candidate |
| Does not fit | Does not fit | Agrees; uniqueness is not established |

A **disconfirming observation** conflicts with a candidate's prediction. It can therefore be an unexpected yes **or** an unexpected no. An expected no is agreement, not disconfirmation. Agreement can still be useful against a rival with a different prediction.

| Triple | Narrow evens | Equal gaps | Increasing | Even sum | Observed answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8, 10, 12 | Fits | Fits | Fits | Fits | Fits |
| 1, 2, 3 | Does not fit | Fits | Fits | Fits | Fits |
| 3, 2, 1 | Does not fit | Does not fit | Does not fit | Fits | Does not fit |
| 2, 4, 5 | Does not fit | Does not fit | Fits | Does not fit | Fits |

### The nested pair

The narrow-even rule is **nested within** the increasing rule: every triple accepted by narrow evens is also accepted by increasing, while the reverse need not hold.

- **8, 10, 12** is accepted by both. A yes does not distinguish this pair
- **1, 2, 3** is rejected by narrow evens but accepted by increasing. The observed yes contradicts the narrow candidate under our stable accurate setup
- **3, 2, 1** is rejected by both. Its observed no does not distinguish this pair; in the full menu, it does contradict even sum

The odd case **3, 5, 7** is also a negative test under narrow evens, not a positive one: it violates the even-number condition. It is a positive test under equal gaps. The observed yes rules out narrow evens but leaves equal gaps and increasing compatible with that row.

### Reproduce a whole test sequence

Keep the initial menu visible and remove only candidates whose predictions conflict with the new result:

| Step | Input and observed answer | Candidates still compatible with all results so far |
|---|---|---|
| Supplied example | 2, 4, 6 fits | Narrow evens, equal gaps, increasing, even sum |
| First test | 8, 10, 12 fits | Narrow evens, equal gaps, increasing, even sum |
| Second test | 1, 2, 3 fits | Equal gaps, increasing, even sum |
| Third test | 3, 2, 1 does not fit | Equal gaps, increasing |
| Fourth test | 2, 4, 5 fits | Increasing |

The number remaining goes **4 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1**. The first new yes is shared by the entire menu; the next yes eliminates narrow evens; the no eliminates even sum; the final yes eliminates equal gaps. “Yes” and “no” each matter when predictions differ.

**Four candidates minus three exclusions leaves one**, within this menu. It does not prove that no other imaginable rule could fit the finite log. The choice at the end of the island is explicitly a choice among its listed options.

For this printed log, an unlisted rule could accept every increasing triple **except 100, 200, 300**. It agrees with the supplied example and every row above, yet differs on that untried case. Choosing among the menu and identifying an unrestricted rule are different tasks.

## An expected yes can be informative

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="You favour the increasing candidate and choose the unequal-gap case that the other listed candidates reject. Is its expected acceptance necessarily uninformative?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; that acceptance agrees with the favoured candidate but conflicts with the named rivals.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; every expected answer has no information value.' },
    { text: 'Yes; only an observed rejection can ever change the candidate list.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The case is a positive test relative to increasing, yet its yes separates increasing from the other candidates in the table. Informativeness concerns the competing predictions, not whether the outcome sounds reassuring."
/>

In the table, **2, 4, 5** is a positive test if your candidate is increasing. The other three menu candidates predict rejection. Its observed acceptance therefore selects increasing from this menu. A positive test is not automatically an attempt to protect a belief.

The reverse situation is possible too: a candidate can be **too broad**, accepting cases that the real rule rejects. Testing one of those expected-to-fit cases can then produce an unexpected no and refute the candidate in the accurate deterministic setup. You do not need to seek a no in order for a no to be informative.

[Klayman and Ha's analysis](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/KlaymanHaPsychReview1987.pdf) separates a positive testing strategy from confirmation bias. Whether that strategy is effective depends on the task. The practical question is which plausible candidates predict different results and how those results will be checked.

### When repeated confirmations help

Several results can strengthen a comparison when rivals predict them differently. Shared yes answers do not distinguish the nested pair above, but this is a property of those candidates, not a ban on confirming evidence. With probabilistic predictions, the relative likelihood of a pattern matters too; a result need not be impossible under a rival to weigh against it. We do not calculate those likelihoods in this lesson.

A constructed book-return sorter accepts or rejects cards with a **red mark**, a **triangle**, both or neither. Marks are read perfectly in this teaching setup and the rule stays fixed. Four candidates are declared: accept red-marked cards, accept triangle-marked cards, require both marks, or accept either mark.

Here are all four card types and their candidate predictions. The final column records feedback from a fixed hidden rule.

| Card type | Red candidate | Triangle candidate | Both candidate | Either candidate | Observed answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Both marks | Accept | Accept | Accept | Accept | Accept |
| Red only | Accept | Reject | Reject | Accept | Reject |
| Triangle only | Reject | Accept | Reject | Accept | Accept |
| Neither mark | Reject | Reject | Reject | Reject | Reject |

Try the decisions before opening the solution. Start with all candidates, then observe **both-marked accepted, unmarked rejected, red-only rejected, triangle-only accepted**. Which observation first removes a candidate? Which remain after the red-only rejection? Does rejecting the unmarked card demonstrate an informative negative test?

<details>
<summary>Check the sorter sequence</summary>

Accepting the both-marked card and rejecting the unmarked card are shared predictions, so all four candidates remain. The red-only rejection conflicts with red and either, leaving **triangle and both**. Choosing the red-only card was a positive test relative to red and a negative test relative to triangle. The triangle-only acceptance then conflicts with both, leaving **triangle** in the menu.

The number remaining is **4 → 4 → 4 → 2 → 1**. Rejecting the unmarked card distinguishes none of these four candidates. Its no is a valid observation, but it does not establish that the search was diagnostic. Relative to red, the expected-to-fit red-only test was the first new result to separate this menu.
</details>

## Know the limits of a contradiction

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A real sorter occasionally misreads marks. One red-only card is rejected. Does that observation logically refute a claim that red cards are usually accepted?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; check the input, measurement and claim before treating a mismatch as exclusion.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; one surprising result always disproves every probabilistic claim.' },
    { text: 'No result may ever count against a claim once measurement is fallible.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Usually accepted allows some rejections. A fallible observation also raises questions about recognition, state and recording. Those checks must be applied fairly to favourable and unfavourable results."
/>

The number task assumes a fixed rule and an accurate readout. Under those conditions, a candidate that predicts yes and receives no cannot be the exact rule. The conclusion also depends on the input being what you think it was. That is a **conditional logical exclusion**.

A working sorter can have dirty marks, a stale setting or an incorrectly recorded answer. A hypothesis may instead predict a probability, allowing both outcomes. One discrepancy is then evidence to investigate, not automatically a logical refutation. Preserve the record; check the actual input and current configuration, test known controls, and obtain further comparable observations where needed. A control itself must be trustworthy for its result to resolve the question.

Apply those checks to both outcomes. Inventing an excuse only after an unwelcome answer protects the belief; discarding every inconvenient observation as noise prevents learning too. State beforehand what would count against each candidate and what checks would make the result interpretable. Record any justified change to the hypothesis, then test the revised version with new evidence.

### When to use this approach

Use it when several explanations fit a current observation and another test is possible. Name meaningful alternatives and their predicted outcomes, including the possibility that your candidate is too narrow or too broad. Choose the comparison for its relevance and feasibility. The aim is to learn what the evidence distinguishes, not to collect an impressive quota of rejections.
