A faster finishing step can deliver your first job sooner without increasing how many jobs a busy production line can finish each hour. Both statements can be true. The trick is to keep the stopwatch and the output counter separate.

**Amdahl's law** calculates the speedup of a fixed job when one portion becomes faster and the remaining time stays unchanged. It connects to the course's concern with whole-system performance, but it is a time calculation, not the minimum-capacity rule for a production line. All timings below are constructed teaching data.

## Choose the quantity before the formula

**Latency** is elapsed time from a declared start to finish for one job. **Throughput** is completed output per unit of time over a declared observation window. **Capacity** is a sustainable rate limit under stated resource and operating assumptions; observed throughput can be below it.

| Quantity | Example unit | Question |
|---|---|---|
| One job's latency | Minutes per job | How long until this job finishes? |
| Observed throughput | Finished jobs per hour | How much output actually finished in this window? |
| Line capacity | Jobs per hour | What rate could the stated resources sustain? |
| Speedup factor | A ratio, with no time unit | How does an old duration compare with the new duration? |

A job's speedup factor is **old elapsed time ÷ new elapsed time**. Reducing eight minutes to seven gives **8 ÷ 7 ≈ 1.14×**. It does not, by itself, establish a new production-line rate. We will work through that exact distinction below.

### When to make this choice

Before judging any improvement, declare the output, quality, workload and time window. A shorter response time, more completed work and less waiting inventory are different objectives. A useful improvement for one need not change the others by the same proportion.

## Rebuild the fixed job's elapsed time

Gene Amdahl's 1967 paper discusses limits imposed by sequential work and practical complications in parallel computing. We will derive the usual fixed-work formula, using a local speed factor rather than assuming a processor count automatically provides that factor. [Amdahl's original paper](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~18742/papers/Amdahl1967.pdf)

A report job takes **100 minutes**: 20 minutes of preparation and finalisation, plus 80 minutes of transformation. The phases do not overlap. The proposal changes only the transformation, making it twice as fast.

| Time contribution | Original | After the proposal |
|---|---|---|
| Unchanged preparation and finalisation | 20 minutes | 20 minutes |
| Transformation | 80 minutes | 80 ÷ 2 = 40 minutes |
| Whole job | 100 minutes | 20 + 40 = 60 minutes |

The overall speedup is **100 ÷ 60 ≈ 1.67×**. Elapsed time falls by **40%**, from 100 to 60 minutes. A 1.67× speedup and a 40% time reduction describe the same change using different ratios.

Define the symbols from the **original timing**:

- $T_0$ is the original elapsed time, in minutes here.
- $p$ is the selected portion's fraction of that original time: $80/100 = 0.8$.
- $1-p$ is the unchanged fraction: $20/100 = 0.2$.
- $s$ is the selected portion's local speedup factor: $s=2$ halves its duration.
- $T_1$ is the new elapsed time; $S(s)$ is the whole job's speedup factor.

The new time adds the unchanged duration and the revised duration:

$$
T_1=T_0\left((1-p)+\frac{p}{s}\right)
$$

Dividing original time by new time gives Amdahl's familiar form:

$$
S(s)=\frac{T_0}{T_1}=\frac{1}{(1-p)+p/s}
$$

Here, **20 + 80 ÷ 2 = 60 minutes**, or equivalently **100 × (0.2 + 0.8 ÷ 2) = 60 minutes**. The two calculations are the same decomposition.

### Assumptions that make the sum valid

Use the same job, input and required output quality. The selected and unchanged contributions must form a non-overlapping decomposition of elapsed time. The unchanged duration must actually remain unchanged, and new coordination or transfer time must be absent or counted separately.

Use $0\leq p\leq1$ and $s>0$; an improvement has $s\geq1$. If $s=1$, nothing changes. If the original job ran on several cores, do not sum their overlapping CPU times and call that elapsed time. Adding processors gives $s$ equal to their count only under an ideal scaling assumption for the selected work.

“Unchanged” means **left unchanged by this proposal**, not impossible to improve by any future proposal.

For the report, the same baseline gives **new time = 20 + 80 ÷ s minutes**:

| Local speed factor | Selected time | Whole-job time | Speedup relative to the original 100 minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 80 minutes | 100 minutes | 1× |

The eight-to-sixteen change saves **5 minutes**, a **5 ÷ 30 ≈ 16.7%** reduction from the current duration. It changes baseline speedup from about **3.33× to 4×**, while its additional factor relative to the current version is **30 ÷ 25 = 1.2×**. Keep the reference version explicit.

Successive local doublings save **40, 20, 10 and 5 minutes**. Those time savings halve. The increments in the baseline speedup number do **not** have to shrink on every doubling: 1× to 1.67× adds about 0.67, while 1.67× to 2.5× adds about 0.83. Say which quantity exhibits diminishing gains.

If the unchanged fraction is positive, making the selected portion arbitrarily fast leaves that unchanged duration:

$$
\lim_{s\to\infty}S(s)=\frac{1}{1-p}\qquad(0\leq p<1)
$$

For $p=0.8$, the limiting speedup is **1 ÷ 0.2 = 5×**. With a finite speed factor and a positive selected portion, the job still takes more than 20 minutes. Infinite local speed is an ideal limit, not an implementation plan.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The ceiling belongs to the stated model">
If $p=0$, the proposal selects none of the time and $S(s)=1$. If $p=1$, there is no unchanged time and the ideal formula gives $S(s)=s$, with no finite ceiling from this term. Changing the work or the supposedly unchanged duration requires revisiting the model. The fraction is measured from the original job, not repeatedly redefined from each new duration.
</Callout>

### When the limit is useful

Use it to reject a target that the proposed scope cannot meet. If the report's selected portion is the only one being changed, a ten-minute whole-job target is incompatible with the unchanged twenty minutes. The limit does not decide whether saving five minutes is valuable; a deadline or operating cost may make those minutes matter.

## Contrast one job with a line of overlapping jobs

A different process gives an exact contrast. Every job needs **stage A for six minutes, then stage B for two minutes**. Each stage has its own dedicated resource. One job cannot overlap its own A and B work, but A can work on the next job while B handles the previous one.

Assume constant service times, one visit to each stage, enough waiting jobs and buffers, instant transfers, no failures or rework, and unchanged quality. The line starts empty at minute zero.

For the first job, **latency = 6 + 2 = 8 minutes**. For a stream, stage capacities are **60 ÷ 6 = 10 jobs/hour** and **60 ÷ 2 = 30 jobs/hour**. The sustainable line capacity is **min(10, 30) = 10 jobs/hour**.

Now make B twice as fast, reducing it from two minutes to one. The first job finishes in **6 + 1 = 7 minutes**, a speedup of **8 ÷ 7 ≈ 1.14×**. B's capacity rises to **60 jobs/hour**, but the line capacity remains **min(10, 60) = 10 jobs/hour**.

For this one job, the selected fraction is $p=2/8=0.25$ and $s=2$. Amdahl gives the same **1 ÷ (0.75 + 0.25 ÷ 2) = 8 ÷ 7** time ratio.

| Job | A runs | B runs before the change | Original completion | B runs after the change | Revised completion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | 0–6 minutes | 6–8 minutes | Minute 8 | 6–7 minutes | Minute 7 |
| Second | 6–12 minutes | 12–14 minutes | Minute 14 | 12–13 minutes | Minute 13 |
| Third | 12–18 minutes | 18–20 minutes | Minute 20 | 18–19 minutes | Minute 19 |

The completion interval stays **six minutes**. Reducing B shifts the finishing times earlier without shortening that steady interval. The completions are at minutes 8, 14, …, 56 before the change and 7, 13, …, 55 after it. The first hour of an empty line completes only nine jobs in either case; a ten-jobs/hour sustainable capacity is not a promise of ten completions in every finite window.

### What changes with one shared resource

If **one worker** must perform both A and B and repeats whole jobs without overlap, the relevant busy time is eight minutes per job. The corresponding long-run capacity is **60 ÷ 8 = 7.5 jobs/hour**. Reducing B to one minute changes that to **60 ÷ 7 ≈ 8.57 jobs/hour**. The two dedicated capacities are not simultaneously available in this arrangement.

Amdahl's time sum can therefore also describe a rate increase when jobs are repeated serially without overlap. It still does not replace the dedicated-line minimum. Name the resource arrangement before translating time into jobs/hour.

## Count overhead and compare realistic alternatives

The fixed-work formula is an ideal prediction only when its contributions remain valid. Let $H(s)$ be additional, non-overlapping coordination time, in minutes, introduced by a particular implementation beyond the original measured work:

$$
T_1=T_0\left((1-p)+\frac{p}{s}\right)+H(s)
$$

For the report at a local factor of eight, suppose a proposed implementation adds **six minutes of coordination**. The new whole-job time is **20 + 80 ÷ 8 + 6 = 36 minutes**, giving **100 ÷ 36 ≈ 2.78×**, rather than the overhead-free 3.33×.

| Implementation | Local factor | Unchanged time | Revised transformation | Additional coordination | Whole job |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First proposal | 8 | 20 minutes | 10 minutes | 6 minutes | 36 minutes |
| More parallel proposal | 16 | 20 minutes | 5 minutes | 12 minutes | 37 minutes |

Under these supplied overhead estimates, the second proposal makes the selected work faster but the whole job **one minute slower** than the first. The table is a calculation to validate, not evidence that real implementations always incur those overheads.

If overhead is nonnegative and the twenty unchanged minutes really remain, the ideal 5× limit remains an upper bound. An implementation may stay well below it. Re-measure if parallelism changes the rest of the time, memory behaviour or the output requirements.

A larger workload is another question. Gustafson's original analysis distinguishes a fixed-size job from work that grows with the available computing resources. Keeping the old fraction while increasing the work can invalidate this particular prediction. [Gustafson, Reevaluating Amdahl's law](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~hari/teaching/paralg/Gustafson88.pdf)

### A small share is not automatically worthless

At the **same local factor and overhead**, accelerating a larger time share saves more time. Real proposals need not have the same factor, effort or consequences.

Suppose the report must finish in **at most 90 minutes**, and two mutually exclusive proposals have equal implementation cost and preserve quality:

| Proposal | Change from the original job | New whole-job time |
|---|---|---|
| Change the original 80-minute portion | Reduce it to 76 minutes; leave the other 20 alone | 76 + 20 = 96 minutes |
| Change the original 20-minute portion | Reduce it to 10 minutes; leave the other 80 alone | 10 + 80 = 90 minutes |

Only the second meets that deadline. “Unchanged” was relative to a proposal: the former twenty-minute remainder can become the selected portion in a different proposal. Amdahl supplies the time comparison, while the objective, achievable changes and costs determine what is worth doing.
