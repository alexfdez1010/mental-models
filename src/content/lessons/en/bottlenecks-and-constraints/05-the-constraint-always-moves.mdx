You improve the slowest station. Its capacity rises, and it remains the slowest station. That can be a useful improvement, not a failed diagnosis. A larger change might create a tie or leave a different station limiting. The next step is to recalculate, rather than assume that a bottleneck must jump after every intervention.

**Bottleneck thinking** is a practice of checking what limits the stated outcome. This lesson separates three questions: what capacity changed, what work is available, and what actually finished. We will also revisit old operating rules, because a rule designed for yesterday's limit may need a new justification today.

Recall the distinction from [the previous lesson](/bottlenecks-and-constraints/04-amdahls-law):

- **Capacity** is a sustainable rate limit under declared resource and operating assumptions.
- **Throughput** is completed acceptable output per unit of time over a stated observation window.
- **Backlog** is accepted work still unfinished at a particular time. It is a count, not a rate.
- **New demand** concerns additional work requested over time; it does not erase older accepted work.

For our first, constructed model, every identical unit visits **Cut, Weld, Paint and Pack once**, in that order. The stages have separate dedicated resources, unchanged quality, compatible capacities in **units/hour**, sufficient buffers and material, and no failures, rework or shared-resource interference. Jobs can overlap across different stages.

The lowest stage capacity gives the **serial capacity ceiling**. It is not the sum or average. Under suitable sustained loading and these ideal operating conditions, the line can approach that ceiling after filling. An empty line's first hour, a blocked stage or a shortage of ready work can produce a lower observed rate. No queue sizes or finite completion schedule have yet been supplied.

| Measurement | Example unit | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Stage or line capacity | Units/hour | What sustainable rate is allowed by the model? |
| Completed output in one hour | Units | What actually finished in that interval? |
| Throughput over that hour | Units/hour | How many completed units per unit of observation time? |
| Backlog at an instant | Units | How much accepted work remains unfinished? |

## Compare partial relief, a tie and a new limiting stage

Start with capacities **40, 30, 32 and 50 units/hour**. Weld has the unique minimum, 30. Change only the quantities named in each row:

| Change or state | Cut | Weld | Paint | Pack | Serial ceiling, units/hour | Stages attaining the minimum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Initial state | 40 | 30 | 32 | 50 | 30 | Weld |
| Partial Weld improvement | 40 | 31 | 32 | 50 | 31 | Weld |
| Weld reaches Paint | 40 | 32 | 32 | 50 | 32 | Weld and Paint |
| Weld rises beyond Paint | 40 | 45 | 32 | 50 | 32 | Paint |
| Paint then improves | 40 | 45 | 48 | 50 | 40 | Cut |
| Cut then improves | 55 | 45 | 48 | 50 | 45 | Weld |
| Weld improves again | 55 | 62 | 48 | 50 | 48 | Paint |

The first two improvements raise the ceiling while Weld still belongs to the limiting set. At the tie, **both Weld and Paint** have the minimum. Raising Weld from 32 to 45 leaves the ceiling at 32 because Paint still limits it. That local increase may be part of a later combined plan, but by itself it adds no serial capacity from the tied state.

Further changes can move the minimum to Cut and then back to Weld. A station returning as the limit does not prove that its earlier upgrade failed: its capacity can be higher than before while the other capacities have also changed.

### When to use this comparison

Use it when a proposal changes one or several compatible stage capacities. Recompute every required stage, list all minima and compare the old and new ceilings. Then check demand, old work, implementation costs, quality and the operating conditions. A useful shorter job time is also possible without a higher line ceiling; the previous lesson shows why latency needs its own calculation.

## New demand is not the whole workload

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="A finishing cell can complete thirty-two ready jobs per hour. New requests fall to twenty per hour, but thirty-six older accepted jobs are already ready. Must it immediately complete only twenty jobs in the next hour?"
  options={[

    { text: 'Yes; a lower arrival rate automatically cancels the older jobs.' },
    { text: 'No; the initial backlog increases the cell’s physical capacity above thirty-two.' }
  ]}
  explanation="New requests and older accepted work are different sources of available jobs. A backlog can keep the cell busy above the new-request rate while it drains. It adds work, not service capacity."
/>

Use a **separate single-resource model** for the queue. This is one final finishing cell, not the preceding four-stage line chart. The older jobs have completed any upstream work and are ready for this cell. There is no additional filling or transport delay, no downstream limit, no failures or rework, and the cell works whenever eligible jobs are available.

Its constant service capacity is **32 jobs/hour**, or **one job every 1/32 hour**. The initial backlog is **36 jobs**. Exactly **20 new accepted jobs arrive at the beginning of each one-hour interval**, before service in that interval. The assumptions make the finite completions reproducible; we are not inferring them from a capacity badge.

For each interval:

**Jobs available = opening backlog + new arrivals**

**Jobs completed = min(jobs available, service capacity × interval duration)**

**Closing backlog = opening backlog + arrivals − completions**

The capacity contribution is **32 jobs/hour × 1 hour = 32 jobs**. Counts belong in the balance; rates must first be multiplied by the interval duration.

| One-hour interval | Opening backlog, jobs | New arrivals at its start, jobs | Available jobs | Completed jobs | Closing backlog, jobs | Observed throughput, jobs/hour |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | 36 | 20 | 56 | 32 | 24 | 32 |
| Second | 24 | 20 | 44 | 32 | 12 | 32 |
| Third | 12 | 20 | 32 | 32 | 0 | 32 |
| Fourth | 0 | 20 | 20 | 20 | 0 | 20 |

For example, the first hour ends with **36 + 20 − 32 = 24 jobs**. The second ends with **24 + 20 − 32 = 12**. The third clears the remaining backlog as it serves **12 + 20 = 32** jobs. Only the fourth hour, with no old work left, completes twenty.

Over all four hours, **36 initial jobs + 4 × 20 arrivals = 116 jobs**. Completions total **32 + 32 + 32 + 20 = 116**, leaving zero backlog. Output exceeds the new arrivals during the catch-up period without violating the service limit. The count balance preserves unfinished work within the declared boundary. The minimum-service calculation here additionally relies on all arrivals being ready at the interval start. Late arrivals or blocked jobs can leave early capacity unused, so interval totals alone would then be insufficient to determine completions.

With no backlog or other work objective and arrivals continuing this way, the sustained completion rate is limited by the new requests. That does not mean an extra-capacity proposal is always worthless: it might change catch-up time, waiting, reliability or another defined outcome. Compare the relevant objective and cost. Do not prescribe selling more simply because there is spare processing capacity.

## Reassess operating rules as well as equipment

The feedback loop is **change → measure the result → reassess capacity, work and rules → choose the next justified action**. Sometimes that action concerns the same resource. Sometimes another stage binds, or a tie calls for a combined plan. Sometimes the current goal is met and no further capacity upgrade is warranted.

[Goldratt Research Labs' focusing steps](https://www.goldrattresearchlabs.com/introduction-to-toc) make the return to identification conditional on a previous constraint being broken and warn about inertia. They do not establish that any partial improvement must relocate the constraint or that every iteration guarantees an output gain.

A policy can also limit useful flow. Consider a constructed approval desk with capacity **8 ready cases/hour** and **6 eligible cases** available at 09:00. The desk is available for the full hour, and no new cases arrive before 10:00. An old rule releases work only in batches of ten, with no timed release. Under that rule, the six cases wait even though the desk could process them. If a validated smaller release preserves the same checks and is authorised, processing all six takes **6 ÷ 8 = 0.75 hour**, or **45 minutes**. The cause of that particular wait is the release rule, rather than insufficient processing capacity for the six cases.

This is a reason to investigate the rule's purpose. It is not permission to remove a necessary safety, quality or legal check. A different release policy can have setup or coordination costs that must be included. Likewise, training or authorisation can change which resource is usable, while a material shortage can leave a capable station without ready work.

## Transfer the reasoning to a shorter window

A library's single digitisation desk has capacity **6 files/hour**, with no other processing stage in this model. It starts with **5 ready files**. **One new file arrives at the start of every half-hour interval**. A file takes ten minutes, the desk works whenever a file is ready, and transfers, failures and rework are absent.

<details>
<summary>Check the full transfer, including the partly busy clearing interval</summary>

| Half-hour interval | Opening backlog | Arrivals at its start | Available files | Completed files | Closing backlog | Throughput, files/hour |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | 5 | 1 | 6 | 3 | 3 | 6 |
| Second | 3 | 1 | 4 | 3 | 1 | 6 |
| Third | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 0 | 4 |
| Fourth | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 2 |

The third interval **starts with a backlog**, but only two files are available. They finish in twenty minutes and the desk then waits. A nonzero opening backlog does not guarantee a full-capacity average over the entire window. The fourth interval serves only the one new file.

Over two hours, **5 initial files + 4 new files = 9 completed files**, matching **3 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 9**. The capacity remains six files/hour throughout; actual rates change with available work. No fraction of a file has been counted as completed.

</details>

## Where this goes next

You can now distinguish a higher ceiling from a changed limiting set, preserve accepted old work when demand changes, and check whether an operating rule still serves the current goal.
