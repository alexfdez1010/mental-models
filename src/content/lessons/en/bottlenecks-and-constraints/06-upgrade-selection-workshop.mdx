A workshop prepares sensor kits for science classes. Orders are waiting, and someone proposes a faster intake station because it has the most visible paperwork. Before choosing an upgrade, connect the proposed change to the number of acceptable kits the whole process can finish.

This workshop uses one **hypothetical** teaching dataset to compare the choices. The objective is to sustain the confirmed new-order rate without increasing unfinished work, while retaining the same quality checks. Reducing the backlog's size is a separate objective; matching new arrivals alone leaves the total number waiting unchanged.

## Define the output and comparable capacities

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest

The process is **intake → calibration → verification → packing**. Every kit visits every stage once. Each stage has its own dedicated resource with **480 available productive minutes per production day**, after planned breaks. That is available time, not time necessarily spent busy: a fast stage may wait for work. There is no shared worker or machine between stages in this model.

Assume one kit type, unchanged quality requirements, sufficient materials and buffers, no rework or yield loss, and stable operation. The listed work minutes include the routine work required at that stage. We model sustainable flow after the pipeline is operating, rather than the exact timing of the first kit in a newly started shift. Additional downtime, batching or shared resources would require revising the model.

**Capacity** is the sustainable processing-rate limit under the stated conditions, not the same as observed output. [MIT's Lean Academy glossary](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-660j-introduction-to-lean-six-sigma-methods-january-iap-2012/cb7b7689f877c88831caea046d125a59_MIT16_660JIAP12_Glossary.pdf) distinguishes actual capacity from a theoretical rate that ignores losses. The [Lean Enterprise Institute's timing definitions](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/cycle-time/) also make clear why routine loading, handling and changeover assumptions matter when estimating a rate.

For each dedicated resource, divide available productive time by required minutes per kit:

| Stage | Productive minutes available per production day | Work minutes per kit | Capacity in kits per production day |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intake | 480 | 10 | 480 ÷ 10 = 48 |
| Calibration | 480 | 20 | 480 ÷ 20 = 24 |
| Verification | 480 | 16 | 480 ÷ 16 = 30 |
| Packing | 480 | 12 | 480 ÷ 12 = 40 |

The serial capacity ceiling is **min(48, 24, 30, 40) = 24 kits per production day**. Every kit needs calibration, so the other stages' spare capacity cannot bypass it. Adding or averaging the stage capacities would count capabilities that the same kit must pass through, rather than independent streams of finished kits. If resources were shared, even these separately calculated capacities might not be simultaneously available.

## Diagnose with load and event evidence

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="Work is waiting before calibration. What would make that queue stronger evidence of the current constraint?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Matched arrival, service and availability records showing a persistent shortfall.', correct: true },
    { text: 'A single photograph showing more boxes there than at a different workstation.' },
    { text: 'A manager’s preference for treating the busiest-looking team as the slowest.' },
    { text: 'A high isolated machine rating without checking work or interruptions.' }
  ]}
  explanation="A queue can reflect batch release, a temporary interruption, downstream blocking or a true sustained capacity limit. Rates and time records help distinguish these explanations."
/>

Confirmed new demand is **36 kits per production day**. At that demand, calculate the work each resource would need to perform. The **demand-load ratio** is required work minutes divided by available productive minutes; it is not actual utilisation. A ratio above 100% means the requested work does not fit, not that a resource can be busy for more than all its available time.

| Stage | Work needed for the new daily orders | Demand-load ratio |
|---|---|---|
| Intake | 36 × 10 = 360 minutes | 360 ÷ 480 = 75% |
| Calibration | 36 × 20 = 720 minutes | 720 ÷ 480 = 150% |
| Verification | 36 × 16 = 576 minutes | 576 ÷ 480 = 120% |
| Packing | 36 × 12 = 432 minutes | 432 ÷ 480 = 90% |

Calibration has the lowest capacity, but verification also cannot sustain the full new-order rate. It may not look overloaded yet because calibration feeds it fewer kits. At the current flow of 24 kits a day, verification requires 24 × 16 = **384 minutes**, or **80%** of its available time. That spare time does not prove verification could handle all the waiting demand after calibration improves.

In the teaching observation period of five production days, the logs show **180 kits admitted to intake**, **120 passing calibration** and **120 finished and packed**. Waiting work before calibration rises from **20 to 80 kits**; the other in-process totals have the same opening and closing levels. The change is consistent with 180 − 120 = **60 additional waiting kits**, or an average increase of **12 per production day**. Time records show calibration has eligible work available and is not spending the supposed processing time blocked by downstream stages or waiting for material.

Together, the load calculation and those observations support investigating calibration first. A queue alone would not. For example, a scheduled batch handover can create a temporary queue before a resource whose daily capacity is adequate. MIT's glossary describes batch-and-queue as a processing pattern; its presence is not, by itself, proof of an inadequate capacity ceiling.

## Compare upgrades and follow the next limit

<MCQ

    { text: 'The earlier output figure, keeping the original limiting stage fixed.' }

  explanation="The next-lowest stage can take over as the internal limit. Actual planned output also depends on work available and the goal, so a local speedup is not the system gain."

Four proposed upgrades have been scoped to retain the same quality criteria. Their capacities are estimates to validate in a trial, not demonstrated guarantees. Effort is measured in **technician-days of implementation work**. A technician-day is one person's planned day of upgrade work; it is not a production day, nor necessarily one calendar day of delay. The available improvement budget is seven technician-days.

| Proposal | Change to capacity | Implementation effort | Serial capacity ceiling afterwards |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Intake from 48 to 72 | 2 technician-days | min(72, 24, 30, 40) = 24 |
| B | Calibration from 24 to 36 | 4 technician-days | min(48, 36, 30, 40) = 30 |
| C | Verification from 30 to 45 | 3 technician-days | min(48, 24, 45, 40) = 24 |
| B and C | Both changes together | 4 + 3 = 7 technician-days | min(48, 36, 45, 40) = 36 |
| D | Calibration from 24 to 48 | 8 technician-days | min(48, 48, 30, 40) = 30 |

B raises the serial ceiling by 30 − 24 = **6 kits per production day**, not by calibration's local increase of 12. Verification becomes the limiting stage. C alone does not change the ceiling, but C **after B** permits another increase of 36 − 30 = **6**. Its value depends on the rest of the plan. D alone reaches the same ceiling as B, requires more effort and exceeds this budget; that comparison does not establish whether D could be useful under a different demand or backlog objective.

## Keep demand and old waiting work in the calculation

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="The internal line can process more kits than new customers order. What else must you know before capping planned output at new demand?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Whether older orders or an explicit inventory target still require output.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Whether the highest-rated station can be made faster once again.' },
    { text: 'Whether adding the stage capacities gives a more impressive number.' },
    { text: 'Whether a capacity chart can replace the confirmed order record.' }
  ]}
  explanation="New-order demand is not all work available when an older backlog remains. The output plan must distinguish serving new demand, clearing old orders and deliberately building inventory."
/>

For sustained new-order demand of **36 kits a day**, the baseline can finish at most **24** under the model. B alone raises that to **30**, still leaving 36 − 30 = **6 new orders a day** accumulating. The B-and-C bundle reaches **36**: it can stop further growth if the estimated capacities are achieved, but matching arrivals does not reduce the total backlog. Older orders can be completed while new ones replace them in the waiting count.

Now consider a separate operating condition in the same workshop: there is no backlog to reduce, demand is lower, and there is no inventory-building target. In that condition, a planned demand-limited rate is given by the smaller of demand and the serial capacity ceiling:

**Planned sustainable output = min(confirmed demand, serial capacity ceiling)**

| Operating condition | Confirmed new demand in kits/day | Serial ceiling in kits/day | Planned sustained completions in kits/day |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline with waiting orders | 36 | 24 | 24 |
| B with waiting orders | 36 | 30 | 30 |
| B and C with waiting orders | 36 | 36 | 36; the total backlog does not shrink |
| B and C, no backlog or inventory target | 20 | 36 | 20 |

The last row's spare capacity may be useful for resilience or later demand, but the capacity chart's higher line is not observed output. If old orders were still waiting, the line could use capacity above new demand to reduce them. State which objective you are evaluating rather than treating every case as “produce at the minimum”.

### Pitfall and use

The goal here concerns a sustainable rate, not a guaranteed delivery date for an individual kit. Variability, outages, batches and finite buffers can reduce achieved output or increase waiting even when the simple capacity ceiling looks adequate. A proposal that just matches average demand leaves no margin in this model; validate actual performance and acceptable waiting before making a service promise.

## Choose a trial and the evidence that could change it

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="The bundle meets the rate target on paper. Which trial result would directly challenge that conclusion?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Its effective calibration rate is below the trial estimate at unchanged quality.', correct: true },
    { text: 'The intake station has spare time while finishing the required work.' },
    { text: 'A non-limiting packing station could process more than it receives.' },
    { text: 'The vendor reports a higher nameplate rating without changing the measured work time.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The conclusion depends on achievable capacities for acceptable output. Spare capacity elsewhere is expected in a serial line and does not by itself invalidate the plan."
/>

For the stated objective and budget, B and C are the model's candidate: together they meet the new-order rate using seven technician-days. Test B's capacity estimate before relying on the bundle's promised result, and plan how C would be validated without weakening verification. Implementation downtime and sequencing need their own plan; effort totals alone do not give the rollout's elapsed duration or near-term lost production.

Suppose a representative trial instead measures **15 productive minutes per acceptable kit** at the upgraded calibration stage. With the same available time, its capacity is 480 ÷ 15 = **32 kits per production day**. Even after C, the revised ceiling is min(48, 32, 45, 40) = **32**, leaving 36 − 32 = **4 additional waiting orders per day** if demand continues. Recompute the decision rather than continuing to use the advertised calibration rating.

| Evidence to collect | What it could change |
|---|---|
| Active processing, setup and unavailable-time records | Whether the assumed productive time and per-kit effort describe achievable capacity |
| Confirmed demand, order mix and older backlog | Whether added capacity serves the stated objective |
| Accepted output and rework at unchanged checks | Whether a faster local rate delivers acceptable finished kits |
| Queue histories, blockages and material availability | Whether the apparent constraint is persistent or a symptom of another interruption |
| Upgrade effort, downtime and dependency estimates | Whether the candidate fits the budget and rollout conditions |

Use the Theory of Constraints focusing steps to look for recoverable losses before adding equipment, but treat that work as an intervention with effort, safety and quality requirements. It is not automatically costless. A stage with low observed output because it receives no eligible work needs a different investigation from one with a persistent excess load.
