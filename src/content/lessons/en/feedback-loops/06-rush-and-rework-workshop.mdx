A print shop has a wall of overdue jobs. The manager asks the team to spend less time checking proofs and get more work out of the door. The first weekly report looks excellent. Then customers start asking for reprints.

The extra work has a return address: the same team. In this workshop you will trace that return, separate a temporary improvement from a durable one, and decide what evidence would justify keeping the faster policy. The print shop and all its numbers are constructed teaching examples.

The objective is **to reduce unfinished customer work without shifting the burden into defects and repeat visits**. Completed jobs per week is a useful activity count, but it leaves part of that objective out.

Start with two competing paths. More waiting work creates deadline pressure. If the team responds by completing more jobs, waiting work falls: this is a **balancing response**, opposing the original increase. If the same pressure reduces proof-checking time, defects may rise. Reprints then add work and renew the pressure: this is a **reinforcing response**, amplifying the original problem.

A **proof** is the version checked before printing; **rework** is effort spent correcting work already attempted. The return path must reach a quantity that influences the response again. “Pressure causes errors” alone is a causal chain, not yet a closed feedback loop.

The two paths can operate together. Their labels do not tell you their strength or the resulting trajectory. A reinforcing rework mechanism need not produce an exponential backlog, and a balancing delivery response need not empty it. The [government systems toolkit](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/systems-thinking-for-civil-servants/toolkit) treats loop maps as provisional structures to test and refine.

### When to use this map

Use it when a fix brings quick relief and repeated work later. Check whether pressure actually changes checking behaviour, whether the reported defects cause repeat work, and whether that work renews the original pressure. A plausible loop is a research question, rather than a diagnosis established by a rising line.

Use these explicit assumptions. The shop starts with **120 unfinished print jobs**. It receives **30 new jobs each week**. A job is either waiting or has been delivered; reprinting brings it back into the waiting count. There are no cancellations, transfers or other movements. Every delivery attempt consumes one work slot, including a reprint. Work remains available throughout the comparison.

A **completion cohort** groups delivery attempts made in the same week. Link later reprints to those delivery dates; the same job can appear in a later cohort when it is attempted again.

Under ordinary working, the team delivers **40 jobs per week** and exactly **10%** require reprinting **one week later**. Under the rush policy, it delivers **50 jobs per week**, with exactly **40%** returning one week later. These are fixed model inputs, not measured capacities or claims about real printers. Each return is attributed to its delivery cohort; repeat attempts follow the policy in force when they are delivered again.

| Weekly policy | Delivery attempts per week | Share returning the next week | Reprints generated per week |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary checks | 40 jobs | 10% | 40 × 0.10 = 4 jobs |
| Rush policy | 50 jobs | 40% | 50 × 0.40 = 20 jobs |

The week before the comparison used ordinary checks, so **4 inherited reprints arrive in the first week under either policy**. Thereafter each policy receives the returns generated by its own previous week. All counts are deterministic here; a real share would vary and need estimation.

The brief stock identity is:

**End-of-week waiting work = starting waiting work + new jobs + reprints arriving − delivery attempts**

Ordinary working in the first week gives **120 + 30 + 4 − 40 = 114**. Rushing gives **120 + 30 + 4 − 50 = 104**. The faster policy really does leave fewer jobs waiting at that snapshot. Its own reprints have not arrived yet.

This is a delay before rework re-enters the queue. Reporting sooner can reveal defects earlier, but does not itself remove the effort of reprinting. [Meadows distinguishes delays, information flows and the strength of feedback as different intervention points](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/). In this comparison, we hold each policy fixed to isolate its consequences; we have not specified an automatic rule that changes the policy whenever the queue changes.

Now carry the same rules forward:

| End of week | Ordinary: reprints arriving | Ordinary: waiting jobs | Rush: reprints arriving | Rush: waiting jobs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start | — | 120 | — | 120 |
| First | 4 | 114 | 4 | 104 |
| Second | 4 | 108 | 20 | 104 |
| Third | 4 | 102 | 20 | 104 |
| Fourth | 4 | 96 | 20 | 104 |

The second rush week is **104 + 30 + 20 − 50 = 104**. More deliveries are being made, but the queue stops shrinking. This is not an oscillation: delayed repeat work arrives and the waiting count then stays flat under the fixed inputs. Delay does not, by itself, prove a particular pattern.

For this model, define the **durable completion rate** as delivery attempts minus the reprints they generate. If delivery attempts are C jobs per week and the returning share is r, that rate is **C × (1 − r)**. It measures net removal of work after the return window while work remains available, not the number of different customers served.

- Ordinary checks: **40 × (1 − 0.10) = 36 jobs per week**. Against 30 new jobs, waiting work falls by **6 per week** once the return pattern is established.
- Rushing: **50 × (1 − 0.40) = 30 jobs per week**. It only matches new arrivals, leaving **no continuing reduction**.

### Do not stop the clock with work still returning

At the fourth snapshot, the ordinary policy has **96 waiting jobs and 4 scheduled returns** from its latest deliveries. Rushing has **104 waiting jobs and 20 scheduled returns**. Including that known return exposure gives **100 versus 124 jobs**, respectively. This extra comparison prevents the cutoff from rewarding work whose defect cost has merely moved beyond the reporting date.

In the toy model, every scheduled return is known. In a real shop, some are still unreported: follow delivery cohorts for a justified observation window and show uncertainty about later returns. Jobs of different size, costs, urgency and customer inconvenience would need additional measures.

## Choose the change and its falsification test

Suppose a revised rush process claims it can maintain **50 attempts per week** while reducing the returning share to **20%**. Conditional on both claims, durable completion becomes **50 × 0.80 = 40 jobs per week**. That would exceed ordinary working’s 36 and new arrivals of 30. This is a candidate to test, not a result already achieved.

The comparison also gives a useful threshold. To beat ordinary working’s durable 36 at a pace of 50, returns must be **below 28%**, because **50 × (1 − 0.28) = 36**. Merely keeping returns at or below **40%** would prevent growth at the stated arrival rate; it would not necessarily beat the alternative. Equality is not improvement.

Protecting proof-checking time may weaken the error link. Giving the team cohort-linked defect feedback may improve its information. Evaluating durable completions alongside customer repeat work may change the goal. These are different proposals with different costs; a rung on a leverage ladder does not tell you their actual benefit. Even a parameter can matter when it changes a feedback relationship.

### Turn the causal map into a test

Record delivery dates, checking effort, defect reasons, reprint dates and work difficulty. Compare suitably similar work under the candidate and ordinary process; random allocation may help when feasible, but shared staff, learning and spillovers can contaminate the comparison. Allow cohorts to complete the expected return window and check for later returns. A busy week before the change and a quiet week afterwards are not interchangeable conditions.

The [official impact-evaluation guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book/quality-in-policy-impact-evaluation-qpie-html) explains why a before-and-after change alone gives weak evidence about what an intervention caused. Mechanism records help test the proposed links; a credible comparison helps assess the resulting difference.

Agree review criteria before extending the candidate: it should improve durable completions against the alternative without unacceptable repeat visits, waiting for urgent work or extra staff burden. Revise the model if the pace falls when proofing is protected, the return share exceeds its proposed bound, or returns arrive later than assumed. Reassess the choice if the decision-relevant comparison changes.

### Check the calculation

The first week ends at **72 + 18 + 4 − 30 = 64**. The second ends at **64 + 18 + 6 − 30 = 58**. In the third, the first cohort sends back **30 × 0.40 = 12** clips, so the result is **58 + 18 + 12 − 30 = 58**. Row A follows the schedule. Row B omits the delayed returns; C repeats the first inherited return count; D applies the eventual net rate before the transition has played out.

Here durable completion is **30 × 0.60 = 18 clips per week**, equal to new arrivals. That explains the later flat queue, but it cannot reconstruct the early weeks without the inherited return schedule. If correction complexity or the returning share changes, recompute rather than treating this illustrative rate as a guarantee.

### Sources and boundaries

The two shop policies and the subtitling case are deterministic demonstrations, not observed performance data. The linked primary accounts support the distinctions between feedback structure, delay, intervention and evaluation. No automatic controller, cost optimum or general claim that rushing always backfires is being estimated. Real work can vary in size, urgency, return timing and how pressure affects behaviour.
