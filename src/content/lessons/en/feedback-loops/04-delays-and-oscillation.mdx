An unfamiliar shower is cold. You turn the dial, feel little change and turn it again. The first adjustment may still be travelling through the plumbing when the next one joins it. Repeated corrections can overshoot hot and then cold. The knob becomes a rather poor conversation with the past.

That is a possible delayed-feedback mechanism, not a prediction from the word “delay” alone. A **balancing loop** opposes a deviation; its timing, correction rule and other dynamics determine whether the resulting path approaches, overshoots or keeps swinging. We will make those conditions explicit.

A **delay** is a specified lag in a link: transport takes time, a command takes time to act, or a report describes an earlier state. Identify the link before deciding what to change. A thermometer beside the outlet can reduce measurement lag; it cannot make water travel instantaneously through a long pipe.

A stock also needs time to move because finite flows accumulate. Suppose a tank starts with **50 L**, drains immediately at **10 L per minute**, has no inflow and is observed without reporting lag. After one minute it contains 50 − 10 = **40 L**; after two, **30 L**. The response began immediately. Emptying still takes five minutes at that rate.

| Timing mechanism | What changes first | What has to wait |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finite accumulation | The net flow and the level begin changing | A large total change takes time |
| Transport or actuation lag | A command or order is issued | Its physical effect arrives later |
| Information lag | The actual state can already change | The decision-maker receives the updated reading later |

### When to use the distinction

Timestamp the command, the first physical response and the report used for the next decision. That separates waiting for an effect from waiting to learn about an effect that already occurred. The distinction also prevents describing every slow-moving stock as motionless until a delay expires.

The simulator below uses an abstract level, not a calibrated shower temperature. Its balancing rule is:

$$S_{t+1}=S_t+g(G-\widehat S_t),\qquad \widehat S_t=S_{\max(0,t-d)}.$$

Here **S** is the current level in model units, **G** is the fixed target, **g** is the fraction of the reported gap added at each update, and **d** is the reading’s age in whole update steps. The hat marks the reading the controller uses. A positive correction adds units; a negative one removes them. All history before the start uses the initial level.

The visual fixes the starting level at **20**, the target at **70**, and displays **46 updates**. It has no external disturbances, capacity limits or measurement noise. One update is not automatically one second or one minute. Those choices are part of the model.

**Overshoot** means crossing the target in the direction of approach. **Oscillation** is repeated movement back and forth around a level. A **damped oscillation** has shrinking deviations; growing oscillation has increasing deviations. A crossing once is not enough to establish repeated oscillation, and a short plot is not by itself a general stability proof.

B and C have the same delay but different paths. A, C and D keep strength fixed and change delay. These comparisons establish what those parameter changes do **within this rule**. The loop remains balancing: its correction still opposes the gap it reads, even when that reading is old.

### Work through the first overshoot

For C, strength is 0.5 and the reading is one step old. At the first update, the initial history supplies the starting reading. Each row adds its correction to the current level:

| Update t | Current S | Reading used | Correction | Next S |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 20 | 20 | 0.5 × (70 − 20) = 25 | 45 |
| 1 | 45 | 20 | 0.5 × (70 − 20) = 25 | 70 |
| 2 | 70 | 45 | 0.5 × (70 − 45) = 12.5 | 82.5 |
| 3 | 82.5 | 70 | 0.5 × (70 − 70) = 0 | 82.5 |
| 4 | 82.5 | 82.5 | 0.5 × (70 − 82.5) = −6.25 | 76.25 |
| 5 | 76.25 | 82.5 | 0.5 × (70 − 82.5) = −6.25 | 70 |
| 6 | 70 | 76.25 | 0.5 × (70 − 76.25) = −3.125 | 66.875 |

At update 2, the actual level is already at the target, but the older reading still requests an increase. That is the first overshoot mechanism. Subsequent corrections create the swing. **Half of the reported gap is not the same as halving the current deviation at every row.** Follow the stated rule rather than inventing a smooth sequence of reactions after seeing the curve.

## Choose a change against the actual problem

Smaller corrections can reduce overshoot in a delayed model. Waiting can prevent duplicate commands while an earlier one is still taking effect. Neither is a universal prescription. The response may be too weak, the target may move, an external input may dominate, or waiting may violate the task’s requirements.

Consider a separate, constructed rule with **fresh information**, target **70**, current level **20** and a known loss of **10 units per update**:

$$S_{t+1}=S_t+g(70-S_t)-10.$$

| Strength | Gross correction from the initial level | Net first change after the loss | First next level | Steady level under this rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20% | 0.2 × 50 = 10 | 10 − 10 = 0 | 20 | 70 − 10 ÷ 0.2 = 20 |
| 50% | 0.5 × 50 = 25 | 25 − 10 = 15 | 35 | 70 − 10 ÷ 0.5 = 50 |

A **steady level** has zero net change; it need not equal the desired target. Setting the net change to zero gives g × (70 − S) = 10. Here stronger correction reduces the remaining target gap, while gentler correction leaves the system stuck much further below it. Neither strength removes the steady error completely. Accounting separately for a known loss would be another controller design to compare.

This loss is **not included in the simulator**. The example shows why “never add corrective strength” is too broad. [Meadows discusses feedback strength relative to the disturbance it must counter, and delay relative to the pace of change](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/). Suitable changes depend on those relationships.

The **bullwhip effect** concerns order variability becoming amplified relative to sales as signals move upstream, towards producers. It is not a claim that every delay creates a bigger swing at every tier.

Take a small inventory decision. A retailer has **8 units on hand**, **10 in confirmed outstanding orders**, **no backorders**, and a policy targeting an **inventory position of 20**. Inventory position counts on-hand stock plus outstanding orders minus backorders. In this constructed decision, no other commitments enter the account.

The position is 8 + 10 − 0 = **18**, so the policy orders 20 − 18 = **2 units**. Ignoring what is on the way would instead order 20 − 8 = **12**, raising inventory position to **30**. The extra ten have not become consumer demand merely because the retailer put them on an order form. This calculation is about the ordering account, not the physical stock at a future date.

## Test an explanation rather than diagnosing the curve

A wavy line can reflect delayed overcorrection, alternating deliveries, a moving target or several interacting mechanisms. Consider two hypotheses for a warehouse level: its controller reacts to yesterday’s report, or its delivery calendar alternates large and small batches. Both can coexist. Calling the staff impatient does not choose between them.

| Check | Delayed-correction hypothesis predicts | Delivery-calendar hypothesis predicts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timestamp reports and commands | Corrections depend on older levels | That relationship is not required |
| Inspect incoming quantities and dates | Swings can persist with steady arrivals | Alternating arrivals help explain the timing |
| Compare a fresher reading with the same response rule | The predicted path changes if the reading age matters | The arrival pattern remains unless scheduling changes |

These are discriminating predictions, rather than guarantees that one test isolates every cause. A faster report can also change behaviour; a changed input schedule can alter the gap the controller sees. Keep relevant conditions comparable, record what actually changed, and revise the model if its predicted mechanism fails.

### When to use this approach

Use the delay lens when repeated corrections arrive after the state or information has changed. Reconstruct outstanding actions and the update rule, compare plausible timing explanations, and specify acceptable overshoot and response time before testing a change. A fixed instruction to “wait one delay” cannot replace that work.

### Sources and limits

The numerical cases are deterministic teaching models. Their rules, history, units and omissions are stated so the calculations can be reproduced. The simulator has a fixed target and finite display; its regime labels are descriptive summaries rather than a general stability analysis. The supply-chain source supports possible amplification mechanisms, not a diagnosis from a curve.

## Where this goes next

The examples show how feedback strength and timing can produce growth, settling or oscillation. A loop's direction and the presence of delay do not alone determine the trajectory. The next lesson, **Leverage Points**, asks where an intervention might change these relationships and how to assess its likely effect.
