## Common knowledge is an exact model condition

Common knowledge of a fact means everyone knows it, everyone knows everyone knows it, and so on at every finite level. The definition is exact within a specified model. It does not require people to consciously recite infinitely many thoughts. A public event can establish the whole hierarchy in an idealised model when its observation and meaning satisfy the required common assumptions.

A real channel may have uncertain audiences, delays or interpretation. Those limitations can prevent the ideal conclusion. But “a few levels plus faith” is not a general mathematical definition of **almost-common knowledge**. Finite-depth knowledge, probabilistic assurances and knowledge within a time window are different ways of weakening a requirement. Which one is adequate depends on the task and its losses, timing and failure tolerance.

For example, learning that a colleague received instructions may be enough to hand over a routine file. Requiring two actions to happen at exactly the same instant with no possible lone action is much stronger. Do not give both tasks the same communication specification simply because both involve two people.

## The coordinated-attack problem: why confirmation never closes

Consider a particular task: a protocol must guarantee that both generals attack together or neither does, and without delivered messages neither attacks. No bound on message losses is guaranteed. Without a return message, a sender must still consider it possible that none of their messages arrived; no other observation certifies delivery. Under this loss model, finite communication cannot create the required new common knowledge.
Two allied generals sit on hilltops on opposite sides of a valley, with the enemy
in between. If they attack **together** at dawn, they win. If only one attacks, that
lone army is destroyed. They must agree on the timing — but the only way to
communicate is to send a messenger *through* the valley, where the enemy might
intercept them.

General A sends: *"Attack at dawn."* But A cannot move until she knows the message
arrived — a lone charge is suicide. So B, on receiving it, sends an
acknowledgement: *"Got it — dawn."* Now B has the plan… but B cannot move either,
because B doesn't know *his* acknowledgement got through. If it was intercepted, A
never marches, and B charging alone dies. So A must acknowledge the acknowledgement:
*"Got your confirmation."* And now **A** is in exactly B's earlier position — A
can't be sure *that* message landed. Every acknowledgement creates a new final
message whose delivery is uncertain, and whoever sent the last one can't safely act
on it.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The trap in one line">
Under this failure model, the last sender cannot know the final message arrived.
No finite exchange establishes the required new common knowledge. The tower
needs an acknowledgement for every acknowledgement, forever.
</Callout>

Let's tabulate the regress so the impossibility is undeniable. Each row adds a level
of knowledge, and each row still leaves *someone* holding an unconfirmed final
message:

| Message | After it (if delivered) | Who still can't be sure |
|---|---|---|
| A → B: "Attack at dawn" | B knows the plan | A — did B receive this message? |
| B → A: "Confirmed" | A knows B knows | B — did A receive this acknowledgment? |
| A → B: "Got your confirm" | B knows A knows B knows | A — did B receive this last confirmation? |
| … forever | one more level of knowing | always the last sender |

No finite number of rounds ever removes the doubt; it only pushes it up one level.
For this protocol specification and failure model, the required new common knowledge is **unreachable** through finite messaging. A successful-looking exchange does not supply a zero-failure coordination guarantee.



### What the impossibility result leaves open

The result concerns guarantees under the stated loss model, not every instance of useful coordination. Extra acknowledgments can improve a particular person's knowledge. They do not eliminate the possibility of an unconfirmed last message in that model. Different delivery guarantees, timing assumptions or tolerable error require a fresh analysis. [Halpern and Moses](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0006009) distinguish these weaker tasks explicitly.

## A false claim can be commonly believed

**Knowledge is factive**: knowing a proposition entails that it is true. So “false common knowledge” is misleading technical language for a false proposition that people widely or commonly believe. A false claim can still influence action; its influence does not make its content known.

Separate two propositions in a rumour case: “the service will close” and “the announcement said the service will close”. If the first is false, nobody knows it in the factive sense. The second can be true and, with the relevant public-observation assumptions, common knowledge. Shared belief in the first may influence choices, but predicting those choices also requires incentives and expectations.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Audit the proposition">
A public event does not verify its content. Ask exactly what is true, what was observed and what participants believe. Coordinated behaviour alone proves neither the original claim nor the entire knowledge hierarchy.
</Callout>

## Match the knowledge requirement to the action

| Tempting inference | What still needs checking |
|---|---|
| Everyone received the same notice, so it is common knowledge | What each participant can observe about others' receipt and observation |
| Everyone knows preparation, so everyone will act | Availability, payoffs, expectations and any commitment protocol |
| Exact common knowledge fails, so no coordination is possible | Whether this task requires exact simultaneity or tolerates weaker assurances |
| The group acts on a false claim, so it knows the claim | Whether the claim is true; distinguish belief from factive knowledge |

Common knowledge of **preparation** and common knowledge of **intent** concern different propositions. Even when preparation and payoffs are common knowledge, a coordination game can retain more than one equilibrium. A public fact supplies information; it is not, by itself, a rule selecting everyone's action.

<summary>What would change the assessment?</summary>
A verified shared display might remove uncertainty about receipt; a stale or inaccessible display might restore it. An enforceable commitment might change choices or payoffs; a readiness notice alone does not. Specify which claim each piece of evidence supports before declaring the coordination problem solved.
</details>

## Connect the model to the latticework

- **Critical mass and tipping points:** participants' expectations about participation can interact with thresholds. A public signal may change those expectations under a specified behavioural model.
- **Nash equilibrium:** knowledge about others matters for best responses, while multiple stable outcomes can remain even with a commonly known factual background.
- **Reflexivity:** beliefs can affect behaviour and thereby change an outcome. This does not turn a false starting proposition into knowledge.
- **Incentives and preference falsification:** silence can hide preferences; a public statement alone need not reveal everyone's true preferences or willingness to act.
- **Information cascades:** inferring facts from others' actions is a separate problem from auditing who knows that others know. Check what an action reveals under the incentives in place.

### When to use the model

Name the group and proposition first. Then inspect observations, channel reliability and higher-order uncertainty. Separately specify the required action, timing, payoffs and tolerable failures. This keeps a useful knowledge audit from becoming a universal explanation for every crowd that moves or stays still.

Common knowledge is exact within a model; practical tasks may admit weaker requirements. Finite acknowledgments do not create new common knowledge under the coordinated-attack failure assumptions, but they can provide useful information.
