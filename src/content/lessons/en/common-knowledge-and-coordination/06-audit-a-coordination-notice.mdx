Ana and Ben run a rehearsal booth. Both receive the message “The room is prepared”. The organiser's log shows both deliveries. Can Ana rely on Ben knowing that she knows? Can the organiser conclude they will start together?

A delivery log answers the organiser's question about deliveries. Ana and Ben may never see that log. This workshop follows their actual observations before making a claim about their knowledge or their choices.

## List the situations each observation leaves open

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"Both notices arrived. What can the organiser conclude about the recipients?"}
  options={[{"text": "Each knows preparation under the truthfulness rule", "correct": true}, {"text": "Each knows the other has received the same notice"}, {"text": "Each knows the other intends to start the rehearsal"}]}
  explanation={"Truthful receipt rules out every unprepared situation for its recipient. The delivery log is not observed by the recipients, and receipt establishes no intention."}
  pretest={true}
/>

Let **R** mean “the room is prepared at the decision time”. Treat preparation as fixed throughout this snapshot. A **possible world** is a complete description allowed by our model; it is not an extra room or a future outcome. We use five situations, with no probabilities attached:

| Situation | R | Ana receives the notice | Ben receives the notice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Closed | False | No | No |
| Silent | True | No | No |
| Ana only | True | Yes | No |
| Ben only | True | No | Yes |
| Both | True | Yes | Yes |

The notice is truthful and sent only when R is true. Delivery may fail separately. Each participant observes only their own receipt, not preparation directly, the other's receipt or the organiser's log. The catalogue, truthfulness and observation rules are assumed to be common knowledge in the model. We stipulate that these are all the possibilities; the table is not a sample or a frequency forecast.

**Knowledge is factive:** if Ana knows R, R must be true. More precisely, Ana knows R when R is true in every situation compatible with her observation. Her actual situation is always among them. A belief can be mistaken; this knowledge test cannot certify a false proposition. A false rumour can be commonly believed, while the true fact “that rumour was announced” can be common knowledge under public-observation assumptions.

Work the first case. In **Both**, Ana sees “notice received”. Exactly **Ana only** and **Both** fit. R is true in both, so Ana knows R. She cannot identify which receipt Ben has. Ben's corresponding set is **Ben only** and **Both**, so he also knows R.

If Ana receives nothing, her compatible set is **Closed, Silent, Ben only**. It includes a false R, so she does not know R. No receipt is not proof of no preparation: Silent and Ben only are also possible. This is when the test helps: check what the observation rules exclude, rather than how reassuring the message feels.

## Follow what the other person might know

  client:visible
  question={"Ana has her notice. What would show that she knows Ben knows preparation?"}

  explanation={"Inspect Ben’s information in every world compatible with Ana’s observation. Evidence only the organiser sees does not remove Ana’s alternatives."}

In **Both**, everyone knows R. Call this **mutual knowledge**, or “everyone knows”. It does not require anyone to know that everyone knows. Nor does it require such higher knowledge to be absent: common knowledge implies mutual knowledge too.

Ana still considers **Ana only** possible. There Ben sees no notice and cannot distinguish that world from **Closed**, where R is false. Therefore Ana does not know that Ben knows R. Swap the names for Ben's parallel failure. A private notice provides a fact without exposing the other person's information.

Choose **Group reachability** and expand one link at a time. Two situations are linked for a participant when that participant has the same observation in both. The links mean “could not tell these apart”, not “the system moves here”.

| Expansion from Both | Situations reached | What it establishes about R |
|---|---|---|
| Start | Both | R is actually true |
| One link | Ana only, Ben only, Both | Everyone knows R |
| Two links | All five | Someone's possible information still leaves R false |

One counterexample path is **Both → Ana only → Closed**. Ana cannot distinguish the first pair; Ben cannot distinguish the second. The false endpoint defeats **common knowledge**: R is not true throughout the situations reachable by any finite sequence of either person's indistinguishability links. In this finite model, expanding until no new situation appears checks the whole infinite knowledge hierarchy without writing infinitely many sentences.

Do not delete Closed because the organiser knows R is true. Ana and Ben do not have the organiser's information. If every allowed world had R true from the start, the model would already make R common knowledge before any notice. That would assume away the question we meant to audit.

## Test what a public announcement really adds

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"A notice is shown on a shared screen. Which missing detail could defeat the public-announcement model?"}
  options={[{"text": "Participants may be unable to tell who can observe the screen", "correct": true}, {"text": "Participants may retain uncertainty about old private receipts"}, {"text": "Participants may have different incentives despite seeing the screen"}]}
  explanation={"Uncertainty about access or shared observation undermines the stipulated public event. Unknown private history and different incentives are compatible with common knowledge of the announced fact."}
  pretest={true}

Now stipulate an **ideal public announcement** of R: it is true; both receive and interpret it correctly at the modelled instant; and its occurrence, receipt, meaning and truthfulness are common knowledge within this group. This is a strong model assumption, not something established by a “sent” timestamp.

Everyone can now remove **Closed** from the catalogue. Restrict each observation relation to the four remaining situations. Every situation reached from Both satisfies R, so R is common knowledge in the updated model. Even the participants who previously received no private notice learn R from this announcement.

The public version can still leave uncertainty about private receipt histories. R being common knowledge does not mean everyone knows everything. It also says nothing yet about Ben's willingness, diary or ability to start the rehearsal.

For an actual communication plan, inspect the audience and the failure modes. A private acknowledgment visible only to the organiser is extra information for the organiser. A shared receipt board gives participants more evidence only if they can access it, interpret its entries and rely on the relevant delivery/observation rules. A large view count does not establish those facts. Review the model if a participant was absent, a message could be false, or the display could be stale. For practical tasks, specify the allowed delay or failure risk; some tasks can work with weaker assurances than exact common knowledge.

## Check incentives before predicting action

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"Suppose preparation really is common knowledge. What else must you inspect before predicting a joint start?"}
  options={[{"text": "The available actions, payoffs and expectations about choices", "correct": true}, {"text": "Only how many times the preparation notice has been repeated"}, {"text": "Only whether the fact remains common at deeper knowledge levels"}]}
  explanation={"The knowledge claim concerns preparation. It does not specify a best response, an intention or an equilibrium-selection rule."}
  pretest={true}
/>

Keep R common knowledge and now specify a separate decision. Ana and Ben choose **Start** or **Wait** simultaneously. Both are available, understand the same fixed preference points and choose to maximise expected points. These are stipulated payoffs conditional on preparation; no action rule is hidden in the notice.

| Ana's choice | Ben starts | Ben waits |
|---|---|---|
| Start | Ana 4, Ben 4 | Ana −2, Ben 1 |
| Wait | Ana 1, Ben −2 | Ana 1, Ben 1 |

If Ben starts, Ana prefers Start: 4 exceeds 1. If Ben waits, Ana prefers Wait: 1 exceeds −2. Ben has the same comparisons. So **Start/Start and Wait/Wait are both pure Nash equilibria**: neither player improves by changing alone. Common knowledge of preparation leaves this equilibrium-selection problem intact.

If Ana assigns probability p to Ben starting, her expected Start payoff is **4p − 2(1 − p) = 6p − 2**. Wait gives 1 regardless. Start is strictly preferred when **p > 1/2**, tied when p = 1/2. For example, p = 0.4 gives 0.4 points from Start versus 1 from Wait; p = 0.75 gives 2.5 versus 1. These are separately stipulated beliefs for illustrating a threshold. The five-world table supplied no p, and counting its rows cannot supply one.

A mutually understood commitment or action protocol could change expectations or available choices. Its reliability needs its own evidence. If Ben is unavailable, or dislikes starting even together, revise the availability or payoff assumptions. A louder readiness announcement does not repair either problem. Use this separation whenever a team says “everyone was informed, so everyone must act”.

## Transfer the audit to a private acknowledgment

Alex can inspect a room's preparation directly; Bea cannot. If prepared, Alex always sends a truthful notice. Delivery may fail. On receiving it, Bea always sends a truthful acknowledgment; that acknowledgment may fail to reach Alex. Neither receives other evidence. The following four situations are the complete catalogue; these observation rules are assumed to be common knowledge.

| Situation | Prepared | Alex observes | Bea observes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Closed | No | Closed; no acknowledgment | No notice |
| Undelivered | Yes | Ready; no acknowledgment | No notice |
| Delivered | Yes | Ready; no acknowledgment | Notice received |
| Acknowledged | Yes | Ready; acknowledgment received | Notice received |

Actual situation: **Acknowledged**. Before expanding the visual, decide what Alex can know about Bea, what Bea can know about Alex's receipt of the acknowledgment, and whether preparation is common knowledge.

<details>
<summary>Work through the acknowledgment</summary>

Alex's observation identifies **Acknowledged** alone. In that situation Bea's possibilities are **Delivered, Acknowledged**, both prepared. Thus Alex knows that Bea knows preparation. Bea also knows that Alex knows preparation: in both her compatible situations, he observes preparation directly. This is stronger than the first notice case, but still leaves another knowledge layer to inspect.

Bea sees her notice in both **Delivered** and **Acknowledged**. In Delivered, Alex still considers **Undelivered** possible and therefore does not know Bea received her notice. So Bea does not know that Alex knows she knows preparation. She did send the acknowledgment; she cannot see its delivery.

The group path is **Acknowledged → Delivered → Undelivered → Closed**. Bea cannot distinguish the first pair, Alex the second, Bea the third. The false endpoint means preparation is not common knowledge. An extra private acknowledgment improved a particular layer of knowledge without closing the whole hierarchy.

The next useful evidence depends on the task: perhaps Alex only needs reliable confirmation that Bea received instructions; perhaps both need an agreed start window and a visible commitment. Specify that requirement before adding another message. This finite audit does not prove that every protocol with different clocks, delivery guarantees or timing rules has the same possibilities.

</details>
