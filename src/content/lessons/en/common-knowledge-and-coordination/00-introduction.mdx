Picture a stalled commuter train. Many passengers privately want someone to ask for an update. They may hesitate because they are unsure what the others think, whether anyone will join them, or whether speaking up would help.

A public question can change what passengers believe about one another. Whether it changes their action depends on who hears it, what they can observe and what speaking up costs. **Common knowledge** gives us a precise way to inspect one part of that problem: what everyone knows that everyone knows, through every finite level.

## A quick taste of the idea

Return to the train. A passenger who wants to ask a question knows their own preference. That does not establish everyone else's preference. If everyone separately learns a true service update, the update is **mutual knowledge**: everyone knows it, without necessarily knowing that everyone knows it.

An ideal public event can make an update common knowledge when its truth, audience, receipt and meaning satisfy the relevant common assumptions. A real announcement needs an audience audit. It also need not make everyone ask the same question or take the same action. The workshop makes those limits concrete rather than relying on the crowd story alone.
