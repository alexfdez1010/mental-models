A search can make an improvement at every accepted step and still miss the best state on its map. To see why, we need an exact rule. “Try things and keep what works” is too vague: which alternatives are tried, what counts as better, and when does the search stop?

This lesson studies a declared optimisation algorithm, **strict steepest-ascent hill-climbing**. It does not model population frequencies, inheritance, drift or a changing environment. Those processes cannot be inferred from an arrow on this diagram.

## Specify the current state and its neighbours

Imagine a walker who checks every permitted next foothold and takes the highest one only if it is above the current foothold. That is a useful analogy for local comparison. Its limit matters: a person could consult a map or choose to descend, while the algorithm below does neither unless we change its rule.

The constructed design space is a line of seven states. Each state is linked only to the state immediately before or after it; endpoints have one neighbour. The fixed performance score is in points, with higher better. The horizontal arrangement identifies allowed changes, not elapsed time.

A **state** is the complete candidate design currently held by the search. A **neighbour** is a candidate obtainable in one permitted change. The **score** is the declared quantity being maximised. Changing the neighbour rule can change which states are locally optimal even when every score stays the same.

## Follow the strict steepest-ascent rule

At each step:

1. Inspect the scores of all permitted neighbours of the current state.
2. Find a highest-scoring neighbour. If several tie, use a declared tie-break; our worked comparisons have no such tie.
3. Move there only if its score is strictly greater than the current score.
4. Otherwise stop.

**Greedy** means the choice uses the immediate score improvement, without ranking the later routes from that neighbour. **Local** describes which candidates it compares. Neither word says that the process is cheap in every application: evaluating neighbours may itself be expensive, and this example does not model those costs.

Work the original strip from A. A scores 3 and has only B at 5, so move to B. B's neighbours are A at 3 and C at 6, so move to C. C's neighbours are B at 5 and D at 4; neither improves its 6, so stop. The complete trace is **A→B→C**, with scores **3→5→6**.

From G, the only neighbour F scores 9 instead of 8, so move to F and stop: E scores 7 and G scores 8, both lower. That trace reaches the globally best score on this fully known strip. “Local search” does not mean it can never find a global optimum; it means its rule alone does not guarantee one.

## Identify what stopping establishes

A **local optimum** has no permitted neighbour with a higher score. A **global optimum** has a highest score across the whole declared design space; several states could tie for that score in a different example. A global optimum is also locally optimal. A **nonglobal local optimum** is locally unbeatable while a higher score exists elsewhere.

On this strip C is a nonglobal local optimum, and F is both local and global. To reach F from C along the line requires first taking C→D, a decrease from 6 to 4. The strict rule forbids that move. This proves a limitation of this rule on this map; it does not prove that every optimisation method or real evolutionary process is unable to cross such a region.

### When to use the stopping condition

Use it to make a precise report: no improving neighbour remains under the stated neighbourhood. Report global optimality only with additional evidence, such as an exhaustive finite comparison or an appropriate proof. A flat measured metric alone does not show that every possible neighbour was checked.

## Keep equal moves and score scales separate

Strict improvement means greater, not greater-or-equal. Consider another small line L–M–N, with scores 2,2,4. From L, the only neighbour M has the same score. The strict rule stops at L even though N is higher. A rule permitting equal-score moves could follow L→M→N, but it is a different rule; without a policy for equal moves it might also cycle between equal states on other maps.

### When another search variant is needed

Define its choices and stopping rule explicitly. Allowing a worse move, allowing an equal move, adding a distant neighbour and restarting at another state are different changes. None by itself guarantees a better result within a deadline. The later audit workshop will compare permitted paths with intermediate constraints and a terminal objective.

## Prove only the finite guarantee you have

If a map has $N$ states with fixed scores, every accepted strict move raises the score. A strict trace cannot revisit a state, because that would return to its old score after a sequence of strict increases. It can therefore accept at most $N-1$ moves. On this seven-state strip that bound is six; the actual trace from A accepts only two.

The stopping state is locally optimal under the inspected neighbours. It might be globally optimal, as F is, or nonglobal, as C is. The finite proof supplies no seconds-per-step bound, no success probability over unspecified starting states and no universal guarantee for continuous spaces with infinitely many possible states.
