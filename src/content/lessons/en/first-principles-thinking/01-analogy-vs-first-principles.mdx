A meeting, a product price and a new recipe can each be approached by borrowing a comparable solution or by reconstructing what the task requires. This lesson compares the starting points, the evidence each needs and the errors each can introduce. The two methods can also be used together: an existing solution suggests a candidate, and a reconstruction checks whether its important conditions fit.

Let's start by checking what you already think.

## Reasoning by analogy: borrowing the answer

**Reasoning by analogy** uses similarities between cases to suggest a solution or prediction. Copying a familiar procedure is one everyday form. A careful analogy also asks whether the similarities concern the mechanism that matters. Two products having the same colour is weaker support for matching their price than serving the same customers with similar features.

Think of adapting a recipe you have already made. A similar dish provides quantities, timing and a sequence you can reuse. If the new ingredients cook at different speeds, the comparison needs an adjustment. The recipe gives you useful knowledge without requiring you to rediscover how every ingredient behaves.

A recipe can preserve experience from many previous attempts. Reusing it is sensible when your ingredients, equipment and desired result are close enough. It becomes unreliable when you carry an answer across a difference that changes the outcome. The point of examining an analogy is to identify that difference before relying on the result.

<Callout variant="info" title="Check what makes the cases comparable">
List the similarities that support reusing the answer, then the differences that might change it. Familiarity alone does not tell you which differences matter.
</Callout>

**Worked example.** Suppose you're asked, *"How long should a meeting be?"* The analogy answer arrives instantly: *"An hour. Meetings are an hour. The calendar app even defaults to an hour."* You didn't reason about how much actually needs to be discussed — you copied the convention baked into every calendar invite you've ever received. The hour came from a default, not from the meeting.

**Common misconception about analogy:** people assume that if "everyone does it this way," the way must be optimal. But conventions survive for *all sorts* of reasons — habit, historical accident, who had power when the rule was set — not just because they're the best answer. A copied answer is only as good as the original problem it was copied from, and that original problem may have been *different from yours*.

### When to use analogy

Use analogy when a previous solution matches the important conditions and provides a result good enough for the decision. Reusing a report format or adapting a tested meeting agenda can save time. In a high-stakes task, established expert procedures may be especially valuable; check their scope and required checks rather than treating high stakes as a reason to discard them.

## First-principles thinking: rebuilding the answer

**First-principles thinking** decomposes a problem into relevant evidence, constraints and explicit assumptions, then constructs a solution from them. You do not assume the conventional answer is correct, and you do not assume it is wrong. The reconstruction gives you a way to test it.

For this practical method, a **first principle** is a starting point justified well enough for the question you are trying to answer. A definition may be fixed within a model; a measurement has conditions and uncertainty; a requirement depends on the task. Record which kind you are using. Calling a premise “fundamental” does not make it permanently true.

Back in the kitchen, suppose a new ingredient needs a longer cooking time than the rest. Reasoning from the requirement that each ingredient reach its intended texture may lead you to cook it separately and combine it later. You have reconstructed a step from what the dish needs. An experienced cook may combine that reasoning with remembered recipes.

Reconstruction can uncover a different solution, but it also exposes the limits of your evidence. If you do not know how the ingredient behaves, testing a small portion may be the next step. Reasoning carefully cannot replace information you do not yet have.

<Reveal client:visible summary="Check the support for a step">

Ask: “What observation, requirement or rule supports this step, and under what conditions?” If the support is a similar case, check the comparison. If it is a measurement, check what was measured. Both can be useful evidence.

</Reveal>

**Worked example — same question, two methods.** *"How much should this product cost?"*

- **By analogy:** a comparable product sells for \$120. Check whether its features, customers and service obligations match yours before using that as a reference.
- **By first principles:** materials cost \$30, assembly \$20, testing \$10 and an allocated share of equipment and support \$15. The estimated unit cost is 30 + 20 + 10 + 15 = \$75, under those production assumptions. That estimate helps you judge possible prices, but you still need demand, taxes and any costs left outside the calculation.

The \$45 difference between the reference price and the estimated cost is a question to investigate. It could include a return for risk, costs you missed or room for an improvement. It is not automatically recoverable profit. In the battery lesson, we will make the same distinction between a material subtotal and the cost of a finished pack.

## The two side by side

Here is the whole distinction in one view. Read it row by row — each row is a different question you can ask to figure out which mode you're in.

| | Reasoning by analogy | First-principles thinking |
|---|---|---|
| **Starting point** | An existing answer (convention, competitor, last time) | Evidence and constraints justified for the task |
| **The move** | Copy and adapt | Decompose, then rebuild |
| **Where the answer comes from** | The outside — someone already solved it | Reconstruct from supported components |
| **Speed & cost** | Fast, cheap, low effort | Slow, effortful, expensive |
| **Strength** | Reliable on well-trodden problems | Can reach answers convention can't |
| **Failure mode** | Inherits hidden wrong assumptions | Wastes effort re-deriving the obvious |
| **Useful when** | A previous case matches the important conditions | A relevant requirement or assumption needs investigation |

The failure modes can overlap. An analogy can import a wrong assumption; a reconstruction can omit a necessary cost or build on a bad measurement. Either can waste time if it answers the wrong question. Choosing a method includes checking what would make its conclusion unreliable.

## The misconception that ruins everyone's first attempt

Here is the trap, and almost everyone falls into it: people think first-principles thinking means **ignoring all prior knowledge and reinventing everything from scratch, every time.** It does not. That would be insane — you'd spend a week re-deriving multiplication before buying groceries.

The method distinguishes what you have evidence for from what you are provisionally treating as true. Established mathematics, tested physical models and reliable measurements can all support a reconstruction. Their scope still matters: the strength of a material depends on its specification and condition, while a price quote depends on date, quantity and terms.

So there's a line down the middle of "what everybody knows":

- **Supported observations or rules:** a supplier quoted \$8/kg for a specified material, quantity and delivery date; a test measured a component under stated conditions. Use them within that scope.
- **Assumptions to check:** the meeting needs an hour; the product must sell for \$120; a launch requires a press release. Ask what evidence or requirement would justify each one.

<Callout variant="warning" title="First principles ≠ from scratch">
  You are not rejecting knowledge. You're sorting it. Facts go in the foundation; assumptions go on the inspection bench. Reinventing arithmetic isn't thinking from first principles — it's just wasting a Tuesday.
</Callout>

**Worked example.** An engineer evaluating a new structure can use established material data, design standards and required inspections. If the material grade or condition is uncertain, additional testing may be necessary. The question worth reconstructing might be whether a different geometry carries the required load with less material. Reusing sound knowledge and checking its applicability belong to the same careful analysis.

## So which one should you use?

Both. The skill isn't "always think from first principles" — that's exhausting and usually wasteful. The skill is **knowing which problem deserves which tool.**

Use **analogy** when a previous solution matches the relevant conditions. Check important differences before borrowing its result.

Reach for **first principles** when one of three alarms goes off:

1. **The standard answer is failing.** Everyone does it the usual way and it keeps not working — that's a clue the usual way encodes a bad assumption.
2. **The stakes justify investigation.** An important decision may deserve more analysis, but high stakes also increase the value of expert review and tested standards. Focus the reconstruction on the uncertainty that could change the choice.
3. **You smell an inherited assumption.** Something is "just how it's done" and nobody can tell you *why*. That missing "why" is exactly where convention hides a copied answer that may not fit your problem.

(We'll turn these alarms into a sharper decision rule in lesson 5 — for now, just know that picking the tool is itself a skill, separate from using either one.)
