Labels or shapes might satisfy that requirement too. The **why ladder** follows a claim back to its support so that you can distinguish the goal from one familiar way of meeting it.

## The "why" ladder: dig until you hit rock

Take a claim, ask **“What makes this necessary or true?”**, and ask the same question about the answer. Stop when you have starting points justified well enough for the decision, or when further detail would not change the next action. Record unanswered questions as assumptions to test. A measurement, a customer requirement and a contractual constraint can all matter; they do not need to be laws of nature to be real constraints.

Think of tracing a chain of support. “We need colour” leads to “we need to distinguish results”, which leads to a test: can readers distinguish them with labels? Each link needs evidence. A plausible answer can generate a hypothesis, but asking why repeatedly does not turn a story into proof.

The **5 Whys** is a related method for tracing a problem beyond its visible symptom. Five is a prompt to investigate rather than a required count: [ASQ allows fewer or more questions](https://asq.org/quality-resources/five-whys). Causes may branch; the [Institute for Healthcare Improvement notes that a problem can have multiple causes](https://www.ihi.org/library/tools/5-whys-finding-root-cause). Follow the branches supported by records instead of forcing every problem into one chain.

<Callout variant="info" title="Test a proposed cause">
A machine stopped after its fuse failed. A maintenance log also records a blocked lubrication line. That suggests a chain to investigate; it does not prove the blockage caused the failure or that cleaning it prevents every recurrence. Check the mechanism, repair it appropriately and monitor the next runs.
</Callout>

### A quick ladder, rung by rung

| Rung | Claim | "Why is that true?" |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | "We have to print these reports in color." | Why? |
| 1 | "Because the charts need to be readable." | Why does color make them readable? |
| 2 | "Because we use red and green to show pass/fail." | Why red/green specifically? |
| 3 | "Because that's the template we inherited." | ← inherited, not bedrock |
| Bedrock | The reader needs to tell pass from fail at a glance. | (a real human need — stop) |

Notice that the *real* requirement — "distinguish pass from fail" — can be met with shapes, labels, or bold text. Color was an assumption riding on a genuine need. The ladder separated the two.

## Assumptions and facts

The ladder is useful only if you keep track of the status of each claim. Separate what is observed, what is required and what you are assuming. These categories can change as you gather evidence.

- A **fact** is a claim supported as true within a stated scope. “The last three deliveries took five working days” can be checked against records. It does not prove the next delivery must take five days.
- An **assumption** is a premise you provisionally use without sufficient support for the present decision. It may be inherited or deliberately chosen. “Future deliveries will also take five days” is an assumption to examine.
- A **requirement** states what the solution must satisfy. A delivery deadline in a signed agreement is a real constraint even though people chose it.

A common error is to carry a justified observation beyond its scope: because the last deliveries took five days, five days becomes an immutable minimum. Another is to assume that a requirement is optional because it was set by a person. Ask what evidence supports the claim and who can legitimately change a requirement.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Two checks for each claim">
1. **What supports it?** Identify the record, measurement, rule or agreed requirement.
2. **Under what conditions does it apply?** Check date, quantity, purpose and uncertainty.

A claim can be true here and false under different conditions. “Could it be otherwise?” helps uncover alternatives; it is not a test that only false claims fail.
</Callout>

## Worked example: "I can't start a business"

A founder wants to test a small remote editing service. They already have a suitable laptop and internet connection, eight hours available next week and three potential clients. “I need an office, staff and a large loan” is a list of claims to investigate. The business type and the proposed test matter: a factory would have different requirements.

### Check the office requirement

**Claim:** clients need a leased office before they can use the service.
**Why?** The founder thinks a meeting is needed to agree the work.
**Check:** ask whether these clients can review a sample and agree a brief by video or email, including any privacy requirements.
**Result to seek:** an agreed way to specify the job. Existing equipment may cover this test, while time and ongoing connection costs still exist.

### Check the staffing requirement

**Claim:** the test needs another employee.
**Check:** each of the three pilot jobs is estimated at two hours, with one extra hour for shared administration.
**Calculation:** 3 × 2 + 1 = 7 hours, against 8 available. That leaves one hour of slack under the estimate.
**Next check:** record actual times and confirm whether the founder has all the required skills. This capacity calculation does not prove every task can be done alone.

### Check the loan requirement

**Claim:** a large loan is needed before the test can begin.
**Check:** list the cash payments for this pilot separately from the value of the founder's time. Include any tools, permissions or outside help that the actual service requires.
**Next decision:** compare that scoped budget with available funds before choosing whether and how to finance it. Removing an office from the pilot does not make the work costless or establish the economics of a full business.

| Original claim | Requirement or question that remains | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Need an office | Agree the job with clients | Client acceptance and privacy requirements |
| Need staff | Complete the pilot with available skills and time | Task estimates, actual timings and skill checks |
| Need a large loan | Cover the pilot's cash needs | A scoped budget and funding comparison |

The ladder has narrowed the claims into checks you can perform. A successful pilot would answer some questions about this service; it would leave others about demand, repeat work and longer-term costs open.

## Make the stopping rule observable

The staffing estimate points to a decision: **can the founder accept all three pilot jobs within the eight available hours?** Assume that the jobs can run one after another, shared administration takes one hour, and no other work falls inside this time budget. The founder has the skills and equipment required; client approval and privacy arrangements still need their own checks.

With one hour reserved for administration, editing has 8 − 1 = **7 hours** available. Across three jobs, the average editing time must be at most 7 ÷ 3 = **2 hours 20 minutes per job**. This is a limit for this schedule, not a prediction of how fast anyone works.

| Scheduling case | Calculation | Time left within eight hours |
|---|---|---|
| Each job takes the estimated two hours | 3 × 2 + 1 = 7 hours | 1 hour |
| Each job takes two and a half hours | 3 × 2.5 + 1 = 8.5 hours | A shortage of half an hour |

Suppose a timed sample takes two and a half hours. That observation concerns the sample. Treating it as representative of the paid jobs adds an assumption, but it is enough to expose a plausible schedule failure: if all three take that long, the work will not fit. Before accepting the full pilot, check whether the sample matches the jobs in length, difficulty and revision scope. A shorter brief, fewer accepted jobs or a later deadline could change the capacity calculation.

### When to stop and when to reopen

For the capacity question, you can stop tracing the office's history once clients have accepted the remote arrangement and its relevant requirements are satisfied. That answer will not free editing time. Focus the next test on job duration because the plausible alternatives fall on different sides of the schedule limit.

Before committing, write down what you are relying on: a defined job scope, evidence for the time estimate, a deadline and a contingency plan if work runs long. Reopen the calculation when the brief expands, revisions increase or available hours shrink. A reversible pilot can proceed with monitored uncertainty; a firm promise to deliver all jobs needs stronger support. The method chooses what to investigate next, rather than certifying a whole business from one sample.

## A second example: the 40-page report

> "This report has to be 40 pages. Our reports are always 40 pages."

**Why 40 pages?** → "Because that's the length of a proper report."
**Why does 'proper' mean long?** → "...because a thin report looks lazy."
**Who is this report *for*, and what do they do with it?** → "The director reads it to decide whether to fund the project."
**What does the director actually need in order to decide?** → "The expected return, the cost, the main risk, and a recommendation."

**Requirement identified:** the director needs enough information to decide whether to fund the project. A one-page decision summary might contain the expected return, cost, main risks and a recommendation, with calculations and evidence in an appendix. The ladder questions the fixed length; it does not prove that all supporting detail is padding.

## Don't dig through the floor

The ladder can stop at a confident story or continue into irrelevant detail. Both miss the decision it was meant to support.

### Pitfall 1: "I can't explain it" ≠ "it's baseless"

If you cannot explain a rule, information about its purpose is missing. Look for records, requirements and responsible people before changing it. **Chesterton’s fence**, developed in [the lesson on choosing a method](/first-principles-thinking/04-when-not-to), helps distinguish an obsolete rule from a protection whose effect you have not observed.

### Pitfall 2: digging past bedrock into paralysis

Choose a stopping point by the decision. If a measured limit is relevant, repeatable and sufficient for the next design step, use it with its uncertainty stated. If changing that limit would alter the choice and its evidence is weak, investigate further. You can stop before reaching an ultimate explanation of the physical world.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Two ways to stop badly">
**Too soon:** accepting a plausible answer without checking its support. **Too late:** continuing into details that cannot change the decision. Stop with enough justified information to act or to design the next test, and mark what remains uncertain.
</Callout>

### Pitfall 3: a single chain hides another cause

Consider a print shop investigating a late poster order. The order history shows two branches:

| Record in this scenario | What to investigate | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| The approved file arrived after the agreed printing slot | How approval timing affected the start | Whether printing would otherwise have finished on time |
| A machine log records a paper shortage during the run | How replenishment affected the finish | Whether earlier file approval would have prevented the shortage |

An explanation ending at “the client was late” drops the production interruption. One ending at “we ran out of paper” drops the missed start. Establish the timeline and time lost on each branch before choosing a remedy. Fixing one contributing factor may help without preventing every late order.

### When to use the why-ladder

Reach for it when:

- **A plan contains unexamined requirements.** Ask which purpose each item serves, how that purpose was established and whether another approach would satisfy it.
- **A process keeps failing.** Trace possible causes using records and tests; leave room for more than one cause.
- **An inherited constraint may not fit.** Investigate the original reason and the conditions that have changed before proposing a replacement.

Stop when the next question costs more than the information is likely to change. Recheck an established measurement if its conditions have changed; reuse it if the scope and uncertainty still fit. The value lies in resolving a relevant uncertainty, not in accumulating why questions.

## Where this goes next

You can now trace a claim to its evidence, distinguish an observation from a projection and identify which requirements a proposal must still satisfy. An unanswered question becomes a specific investigation or test, rather than permission to drop the constraint.

The battery lesson applies the same method to a quoted price. You will add a material subtotal, calculate the difference and then investigate the costs still needed to turn materials into a finished pack. The subtraction identifies what remains to explain; it does not show that the whole difference can be saved.
