A supplier quotes 600 dollars per kilowatt-hour for a battery pack. Your first material estimate is much lower. The useful task is to reconstruct what the quote includes, check whether the estimates describe the same product and identify which parts of the difference can actually change. This lesson keeps the arithmetic visible all the way from materials to a fuller cost estimate.

## Start with a quoted price and a defined requirement

A **battery pack** combines cells with the connections, protection, control and other components needed for its specified use. A **kilowatt-hour (kWh)** measures stored energy; a price per kWh lets us compare amounts of storage, provided the specifications and included services are comparable. In this example, a quote of **600 dollars/kWh** would imply **36,000 dollars for a 60 kWh pack**: 600 × 60 = 36,000.

A quote is evidence of what a supplier offers under its terms. The unsupported step would be “this quote proves every possible pack must always cost this much”. Reasoning by analogy might use comparable packs as a reference. First-principles reasoning asks what the specified pack requires and what it would take to supply those requirements.

Begin with one part of that investigation: **what materials does this design use, in what quantities, at what purchase terms?** Then ask what processing, integration, testing and support turn those materials into the finished product. The material list is the first subtotal of the reconstruction.

The seven selected contributions sum to the **material subtotal**. They describe the example calculation, rather than a complete bill of materials for every battery chemistry:

|---|---|---|
| Nickel | Bulk of the cathode metal | 22 |
| Cobalt | Stabilises the cathode | 18 |
| Lithium & electrolyte salts | Carries the charge | 14 |
| Graphite (carbon anode) | The other electrode | 11 |
| Aluminium | Current collectors & casing | 7 |
| Separator & polymers | Keeps electrodes apart | 5 |
| Steel can & casing | Holds it together | 3 |
| **Raw materials total** | | **~80** |

The calculation is 22 + 18 + 14 + 11 + 7 + 5 + 3 = **80 dollars/kWh**. Relative to the quote, the material subtotal is 80 ÷ 600 ≈ **13.3%**. The difference is 600 − 80 = **520 dollars/kWh**. At this stage, that is an amount still to explain.

| Line item | Illustrative cost ($/kWh) |
|---|---|
| Material subtotal | 80 |
| Difference still to explain | 520 |
| **Quoted finished-pack price** | **600** |

The 520-dollar difference has not been demonstrated to be waste, profit or avoidable expense. To investigate it, suppose a production trial supports the following estimates under a stated design, volume and quality requirement:

| Trial estimate component | Dollars per kWh |
|---|---|
| Selected material subtotal | 80 |
| Fabrication, assembly and production losses | 180 |
| Control, thermal management and integration | 100 |
| Testing, delivery and warranty provision | 80 |
| Allocated equipment and other fixed costs | 40 |
| **Listed cost estimate** | **480** |

The entries are scenario assumptions to validate in the trial. They show what a fuller reconstruction must include; their values are not evidence of present-day market prices.

## The general recipe this illustrates

The method is useful whenever an aggregate number hides decisions you could examine separately. It asks for a scoped reconstruction and a test of the alternative, not simply a smaller-looking total.

1. **Define the requirement and reference.** State what must be delivered, the unit, quantity, time and the quote or limit being questioned.
2. **Decompose the whole scope.** List materials, transformations, integration, testing and obligations. Mark unknown entries instead of treating them as zero.
3. **Estimate each part with evidence.** Record quantities, prices, yields, capacity and uncertainty. A material price is conditional evidence, not an immutable physical law.
4. **Rebuild and test the alternative.** Add comparable components, check dependencies and compare the result with the reference. Investigate the difference before describing it as a saving.

This works far away from batteries:

- **A restaurant meal:** a 28-dollar menu price and 6 dollars of ingredients leave 22 dollars to explain through labour, premises, equipment, service and other costs. The ingredient subtotal does not establish that you can reproduce the complete service for 6 dollars.
- **A scheduled task:** pulling data takes 2 hours, drafting 3 and review 2: 2 + 3 + 2 = 7 hours of work. A five-working-day delivery may also contain waits or dependencies. Reducing those waits requires checking schedules and responsibilities, rather than declaring four days unnecessary.

### Pitfall: a subtotal is not a finished-product estimate

A subtotal is a lower bound on a fuller total only under the assumptions that its components are required, correctly measured, comparable and non-overlapping. Even then, a lower bound does not establish an achievable finished price. Manufacturing losses, capital, skilled work and risk can be necessary costs.

The trial estimate here is 80 + 180 + 100 + 80 + 40 = **480 dollars/kWh**. Compared with 600, it suggests a possible **120-dollar difference**, or 20% of the quote, if the trial assumptions scale and no costs are missing. That 120 still needs interpretation: for example, whose overhead, financing and return for risk does each figure include? A lower estimate is a reason to validate the design and budget, not a purchase offer.

</Callout>

## Where this goes next

You have reconstructed two different totals: **80 dollars/kWh for the selected materials** and **480 dollars/kWh for the listed trial-cost scope**. You can explain the 520-dollar gap from the first comparison and the 120-dollar gap from the second without treating them as the same opportunity.

The work includes checking material quantities, production steps, capacity, losses and the meaning of the supplier quote. A rough calculation can identify the next useful investigation quickly; a decision to build or buy needs evidence at the level of its consequences.
