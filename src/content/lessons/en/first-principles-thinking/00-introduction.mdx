**Reasoning by analogy** starts from a comparable case: the previous meetings needed an hour, so that is a useful first estimate. The estimate is more convincing if this meeting has a similar agenda, number of people and purpose. A familiar answer gives you a starting point; the comparison still needs checking.

**First-principles thinking** starts by reconstructing the requirement. If each decision needs five minutes of context, ten minutes of discussion and five minutes to assign actions, two decisions suggest 40 minutes. That is a checkable plan, not a guarantee: a difficult discussion may take longer, and you should compare the estimate with what happens.

## What first-principles thinking is

First-principles thinking breaks a problem into relevant facts, constraints and explicit assumptions, then reasons towards a solution. A **first principle** is a justified starting point for that analysis. You do not need to prove every claim from the beginning of philosophy. You do need to know why a starting point is suitable: a measured task time, a legal requirement and an assumption about customer demand deserve different kinds of support.



## Why copying usually works (and why that's the trap)

Reasoning by analogy saves effort when the relevant conditions match. If your usual meeting format reliably supports the same decisions with the same participants, copying and adapting it may be sufficient. Established procedures can also encode knowledge you do not yet have. Understanding their purpose helps you use them well.

Re-examine the comparison when an important condition changes, the usual answer keeps failing, or a requirement has no clear justification. The missing explanation is a reason to investigate. It does not establish that the old rule is pointless, or that your first reconstruction will be better.

## The map of the course

The four teaching lessons develop the method from comparison to application:

1. **Analogy versus first principles** compares borrowing a solution with constructing one from relevant evidence.
2. **The why ladder** follows a claim back to its support and gives you a stopping rule.
3. **The battery pack** rebuilds a material subtotal and then asks which costs are still missing from a finished product.
4. **When to use each method** weighs the value of more analysis and examines the purpose of existing rules.

The **final exam** presents one question at a time. Submitting locks an answer; there is no back button or retry, and the final score appears after the last question. The teaching exercises before it are for practice.

### How to use this course

**Try an answer before opening the explanation.** Include the step that led you there: a calculation, a condition or a reason for rejecting another option. Afterwards, compare your reasoning with the feedback. If you were wrong, identify the particular assumption or step to change; if you were right, check that the same reasoning would work in a different situation.

The next lesson compares the two methods using the same questions, so that you can identify where each answer comes from and what would make it unreliable.
