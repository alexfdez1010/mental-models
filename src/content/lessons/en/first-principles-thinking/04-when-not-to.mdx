## The cost: first principles is slow and expensive

Suppose your team receives the same routine reporting request each month. Last month’s format already meets the reader’s needs. Reusing it after checking what has changed may take a few minutes. Reconstructing the format from the beginning would need a reason: perhaps the reader, decision or required evidence has changed.

**First-principles thinking** has costs: time to define the problem, expertise to identify relevant constraints and effort to obtain missing evidence. A short reconstruction can be quick; a full design investigation can be expensive. Count that work against the value of the decision and the alternatives you are postponing.

**Reasoning by analogy** can save work by using a comparable case. The prior solution is not free knowledge: someone developed it under particular conditions, and you need to check whether those conditions still fit. Reusing an established calculation is also legitimate, but using arithmetic itself is not an analogy; it is applying a rule.

<Callout variant="info" title="Reuse with a scope check">
Ask what changed since the earlier case and which changes matter. Tested expert procedures and mandatory standards can remain necessary in high-stakes work. A new explanation does not replace the checks the task requires.
</Callout>

### When to use it

Compare the likely value of resolving an uncertainty with the cost of investigating it. You will rarely know either perfectly. A small reversible test can provide a useful next step when a full reconstruction would take too long.

## When analogy is the right call

A previous solution is useful when it gives evidence about the present case. For a familiar reporting task, that could be a format the same reader has already used successfully. The relevant comparison is the reader’s task and evidence needs, rather than the fact that many reports look alike.

Reach for analogy — i.e. trust the conventional answer — when:

- **The previous method fits.** Its assumptions, requirements and known limitations match this case well enough.
- **The result is sufficient.** Further optimisation is unlikely to change a meaningful outcome.
- **Time is limited.** A checked prior solution is available, and unresolved differences can be managed.
- **Expertise matters.** Use suitable expert guidance and required standards rather than substituting an unsupported personal reconstruction.

Here's the decision table:

| Factor | Reach for first principles | Trust the analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **Decision value** | A relevant uncertainty could change an important outcome | A checked solution already meets the requirement |
| **How settled the problem is** | New, broken, or no good answer exists | Well-trodden, conventional answer works fine |
| **Inherited assumption?** | You suspect "the way it's done" is just convention | No reason to doubt the standard approach |
| **Time available** | You have time to dig | You need an answer now |
| **Expertise** | Obtain the expertise needed to assess the changed part | Use appropriate expert guidance and required standards |

**Worked example.** Your monthly report has the same recipient, decision and data definitions as last month. The recipient confirmed that the format made the decision clear. Reuse the format and check this month’s figures. If the recipient now needs to compare three options rather than approve one, that change gives a specific reason to revisit the structure. Familiarity supports reuse when the relevant conditions remain similar.

## When first principles earns its cost

A reconstruction earns attention when it can resolve a consequential uncertainty that the previous case leaves open. Look for a specific question to answer:

- **A consequential assumption is unresolved.** More evidence could change an important choice.
- **The standard answer fails under the present conditions.** Investigate what differs and whether the old solution can be adapted.
- **A requirement is inherited without a clear purpose.** Find its origin and test whether it still applies.
- **The task has a genuinely new feature.** Identify which part needs new reasoning and which established parts can remain.
- **Agreement lacks support.** Shared confidence is not a substitute for the evidence the claim needs.

**Worked example.** A recurring report takes longer because each team manually recreates the same chart. You estimate that investigating an automated version would take 6 hours and could save 2 hours per month. Over 12 months, that is 2 × 12 = 24 hours of gross time saved, or 18 after the initial 6-hour investigation, **if** the saving holds and maintenance adds no further cost. This estimate can justify a small test. It does not prove the automated design is feasible or that every inherited step should go.

## Chesterton's fence

A rule can appear unnecessary because you have not observed the problem it prevents. Before removing it, identify its purpose and what evidence would show that the current process handles the same risk another way.

**Chesterton’s fence** is a principle associated with writer G. K. Chesterton: understand why a rule or structure was introduced before removing it. The missing explanation is a reason to investigate records, responsible people and earlier failure cases. Understanding the original purpose informs the decision; it does not automatically require keeping the rule.

The trap is using your lack of an explanation as evidence that no explanation exists. Old rules can become obsolete, and they can also prevent failures that are now rare precisely because the rules work. The investigation needs to distinguish those possibilities.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Investigate the function before changing the rule">
Find the original problem, check whether it still exists and compare the proposed replacement. A rule can be kept, revised or removed when the evidence supports that decision and the relevant responsibilities are respected.
</Callout>

**Worked example.** Two production lines share an electrical supply. A rule prevents both running at full power together. You find the original load calculation and confirm that the present equipment still exceeds the shared limit when run that way. The rule is addressing a current constraint. A redesign could change the constraint, but removing the instruction alone would not increase the supply’s capacity.

### Misconception: "first principles is always superior to analogy"

The methods are complementary. Analogy proposes a candidate from an earlier case; a reconstruction checks requirements and supports targeted changes. Either can go wrong if it relies on poor evidence. Neither method guarantees originality, speed or a correct result.

A practical sequence is to inspect the existing solution, identify a difference that could affect the outcome and investigate that difference. If no material uncertainty remains, act. If one remains, choose a test or seek the expertise needed to resolve it. That sequence avoids rebuilding parts whose justification is already adequate.

### Another pitfall: reinventing the wheel and analysis paralysis

There's a failure mode at the bottom of the why-ladder: you keep asking "but why?" forever, re-deriving things humanity settled centuries ago, and never actually ship anything. This is **analysis paralysis** — drilling so deep into fundamentals that you never resurface to act.

Set the stopping rule before the investigation expands: which uncertainty are you resolving, what evidence is enough for the next action and when will you review the result? Some discoveries justify widening the work. Others are interesting but do not change the present choice.

<Callout variant="tip" title="A practical stopping rule">
Stop when the relevant requirements are covered, the critical premises have adequate support and remaining uncertainty is acceptable for the next step. If that is not yet true, state the missing evidence and choose a proportionate test or review.
</Callout>

## Putting it together

Combine reuse and investigation. Start from an available solution, check its scope and focus new analysis on the assumptions that could change the outcome. In high-stakes settings, preserve required standards and obtain the expertise needed to evaluate a change.
