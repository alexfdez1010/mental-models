## Define the kit before calculating its minimum

The hypothetical brief is a tabletop puzzle exhibition: retain the six rigid panels, straight alignment rail and closed token module listed below. One organiser must transport the packed kit and set it up correctly without tools. The organiser chooses a packed-mass limit of **4.80 kg** and a setup limit of **eight minutes**, including the final completeness check. These are teaching requirements, not lifting guidance or standards.

All dimensions, masses and trial records in this workshop are **constructed inputs**. Treat each listed module as a rigid rectangular occupied region. Modules cannot pass through each other or nest inside the closed token box. For these rectangular regions, volume is length × width × height. Dimensions use centimetres; **1,000 cubic centimetres equal one litre**. A design that folds panels or splits the rail changes the starting assumptions and needs its own checks.

| Required initial module | Dimensions per module | Combined occupied volume | Combined mass |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Six panels | Each 25 × 20 × 1 cm | 6 × 500 = 3,000 cm³ = 3.00 L | 1.50 kg |
| Straight alignment rail | 60 × 2 × 2 cm | 240 cm³ = 0.24 L | 0.80 kg |
| Closed token module | 10 × 8 × 5 cm | 400 cm³ = 0.40 L | 0.40 kg |
| **Initial module subtotal** | | **3.64 L** | **2.70 kg** |

The combined volume is a **conditional lower bound**: any container holding those unchanged, disjoint modules must have at least that much interior volume. It does not say a container of that size exists, that the gaps can be used, or that the complete kit needs nothing else.

A **necessary condition** is something a successful proposal must satisfy. Failure can reject a proposal. Passing one necessary condition can leave other requirements unresolved. A complete set of sufficient conditions would establish the stated result under its assumptions; this volume sum is not such a set.

### When to use the bound

Use a cheap bound to reject an impossible candidate before spending time on a detailed layout. If the initial modules occupied more volume than the case, there would be no need to try every orientation. When the volume test passes, continue with shape, clearances and the rest of the brief.

## A volume surplus can conceal an impossible shape

Case A has an unobstructed interior of **40 × 30 × 10 cm**, or **12 L**. The initial modules occupy 3.64 ÷ 12 × 100 ≈ **30.3%** of that volume. That percentage describes an amount of space, not its arrangement.

The rail is longer than every side of the case. That fact alone would not settle the question: a thin object can sometimes fit diagonally. We need a bound that still applies when rotations are permitted.

The greatest possible distance between two points inside a rectangular case is its **space diagonal**, between opposite corners. Any two interior points differ by at most the case’s length, width and height along the corresponding axes. The three-dimensional distance formula therefore gives this maximum:

$$d = \sqrt{40^2 + 30^2 + 10^2} = \sqrt{2600} \approx 50.99\text{ cm}.$$

For intuition, the base diagonal is 50 cm; adding the height gives the longer space diagonal. The [three-dimensional distance formula in OpenStax](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-3/pages/2-2-vectors-in-three-dimensions) provides the geometry. Two rail endpoints are 60 cm apart, so they cannot both lie inside a box whose maximum interior separation is about 51 cm. **The unchanged rail cannot fit in case A in any orientation.** Its thickness cannot rescue it.

Passing the diagonal comparison would not guarantee that a thick part fits: its whole shape, not only two endpoints, must remain inside. Nor would fitting the rail alone establish room for every other module.

A jointed rail is a possible redesign, rather than a rebuttal of this result. It changes the retained-part assumption and adds questions about alignment, assembly time and reliable joints. Investigate those if that alternative is worth pursuing.

## Demonstrate a placement instead of inferring one

Case B has an unobstructed interior of **65 × 30 × 12 cm**. One centimetre of wall padding on every face leaves a usable rectangular region of **63 × 28 × 10 cm**, or **17.64 L**. The declared lining occupies only that wall layer. This proposal has no internal dividers; any real hinge, latch or added separator intruding into the usable region would require an updated layout.

We now include an instruction wallet omitted from the initial module list. It measures **25 × 18 × 1 cm**. A volunteer cannot set up the exhibition correctly if the instructions have been left on someone’s desk.

A **constructive placement** specifies an arrangement you can check. Use x for length, y for width and z for height, measured from the same lower corner of the usable region. Stack the six panels flat. Here is one arrangement, with all coordinates in centimetres:

| Module or stack | Origin (x, y, z) | Size along x, y, z | Occupied intervals |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rail | (0, 0, 0) | 60 × 2 × 2 | x: 0–60; y: 0–2; z: 0–2 |
| Panel stack | (0, 3, 0) | 25 × 20 × 6 | x: 0–25; y: 3–23; z: 0–6 |
| Token box | (27, 3, 0) | 10 × 8 × 5 | x: 27–37; y: 3–11; z: 0–5 |
| Instruction wallet | (0, 3, 6) | 25 × 18 × 1 | x: 0–25; y: 3–21; z: 6–7 |

Every interval remains within the usable region. The rail is separated from the other items along y; the token box is separated from the stack and wallet along x. The wallet touches the top of the panels, while their interiors remain separate along z. **Touching faces are allowed; overlapping interiors are not.** This is a placement witness under the declared dimensions. It does not claim the smallest possible case.

### What the geometric result leaves open

The layout shows ideal modules contained without collisions. It does not establish the real measurements, damage resistance, access to parts, carrying mass or setup time. A cardboard or physical mock-up can expose clearances that an ideal rectangular model left out. If the real objects differ, revise the declared inputs rather than treating the drawing as stronger evidence than the objects.

Before the trial, specify what will count as a pass: the correct kit, one organiser, no tools, starting with the case closed and ending with a complete, correctly arranged exhibition within eight minutes. Identify the version and record the steps. The [NASA product-verification handbook](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/5-3-product-verification/) describes setting requirements and acceptance criteria, selecting a check method and investigating discrepancies. The method is useful here; the museum kit is our own teaching example.

A constructed trial record for B reads:

| Setup operation | Recorded time |
| --- | --- |
| Unpack and remove protection | 2.0 min |
| Position panels and rail | 3.0 min |
| Place tokens and instructions | 2.0 min |
| Check completeness | 1.5 min |
| **Total** | **8.5 min** |

The last step is work the product requires even though it adds no piece. **B misses the eight-minute criterion in this trial**: 2 + 3 + 2 + 1.5 = 8.5. Geometry and mass passing do not cancel that failure.

Check whether the procedure and record represent the intended task. A single trial does not establish every future setup time, nor does a miss identify its own cause. It does give a particular proposal, under particular conditions, something to explain and change.

### A revision changes more than the attractive number

Candidate C adds a retainer to keep tokens ordered. Assume it adds **0.20 kg**, increases the token box height from **5 to 6 cm**, and is predicted to reduce the token-and-instruction step from **two minutes to one**. These are proposed changes, not observed performance.

The revised mass is 4.50 + 0.20 = **4.70 kg**, leaving **0.10 kg** below the limit. At the same origin, the token box now occupies z from 0 to 6 cm and remains inside the usable height without intersecting the other modules. The predicted setup total is 2 + 3 + 1 + 1.5 = **7.5 min**.

C therefore passes these mass and layout calculations, while its timing remains a hypothesis. Repeat the complete procedure with the revised kit. A time above the criterion, a jammed token or a missing instruction would challenge acceptance. Fixing the easy-to-measure number while breaking the activity is a rather elaborate way to lose the puzzle.

The [NASA product-validation guidance](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/5-4-product-validation/) also asks whether the integrated product serves its intended use. For this kit, check that organisers can actually read the instructions and play the intended puzzle, alongside the brief’s measurable criteria. Record who and what conditions were tested; do not extend a pass to every user or environment automatically.

## Transfer the reasoning to an offline package

Try the same method without boxes. An offline image exhibition has an **80 MB** storage quota. In this constructed example every size uses the same MB unit, categories do not overlap, all files are required, and no additional compression or deduplication is available. The image subtotal is only part of the usable package.

| Required category | Original package | Proposed revision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Six images | 6 × 8 = 48 MB | 6 × 6 = 36 MB |
| Viewer | 30 MB | 30 MB |
| Captions | 4 MB | 4 MB |
| Metadata and index | 6 MB | 6 MB |

Calculate each complete package before answering. Keep the storage result separate from whether the exhibition can be used.

The original needs 48 + 30 + 4 + 6 = **88 MB**, exceeding the quota by **8 MB**. The revision needs 36 + 30 + 4 + 6 = **76 MB**, leaving **4 MB**. Neither the original image subtotal nor the revised storage total establishes that the exhibition works correctly.

A useful rejecting test loads the full revised package offline and checks the captions, navigation and image legibility on the intended display. If smaller images obscure details needed by the activity, passing the storage calculation has not solved the whole problem. Changing compression or requirements creates another proposal whose assumptions must be explicit.
