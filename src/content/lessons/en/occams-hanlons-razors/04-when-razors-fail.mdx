## A heuristic does not supply a probability

A razor can suggest an explanation or a check. A **prior** is a probability distribution before incorporating the data under analysis. These are different things: a model or justified data must support those probabilities.

In the Hanlon lesson, the constructed catalogue supplied the counts. A pattern selected a subset and changed the calculation's denominator. The razor did not invent those frequencies. A preference without numbers can also be revised, but that does not make it a calibrated probability.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Keep the check open">
State an observation that could make you abandon the explanation you prefer. If you only seek ways to defend it, you are feeding confirmation bias.
</Callout>

## Where Occam over-trims: reality isn't always simple

Occam's Razor says: prefer the explanation that needs the fewest assumptions. Great rule. But it comes with fine print that people love to ignore. The full quote (often attributed to Einstein) is the whole point:

> Make things as simple as possible — **but no simpler.**

That second clause is the entire safety mechanism. Simplicity is only a virtue **when the simple explanation actually fits the facts.** If the lean story leaves evidence unexplained, it isn't elegant — it's just wrong, wearing a nicer suit. A simple explanation that doesn't fit beats a complex one that does *zero* of the time.

And some corners of reality are genuinely, irreducibly complex. The simplest explanation is often plain incorrect in:

- **Biology and the human body** — riddled with feedback loops, redundancy, and exceptions.
- **Economies and markets** — millions of agents, no single lever.
- **Climate systems** — coupled, nonlinear, full of lag.
- **Large software systems** — where "the obvious one-line cause" is famously a liar.

<Callout variant="info" title="The assumption-counting con">
Occam only works when the explanations genuinely fit equally well *and* you count assumptions honestly. But "an assumption" is slippery. You can hide a mountain of complexity inside one confident word ("it's just *fraud*") and call it simple, or split one clean idea into five steps and call it complex. People game the count — usually without noticing — to make the answer they already liked look "simpler."
</Callout>

There's a third way Occam over-trims: it tempts you to force **one** cause when the truth is a **conjunction**. Multiple things can be true at once. The real story behind an outage is often "a bug *and* a bad config *and* a retry storm that turned a small failure into an avalanche." Insisting on a single root cause there doesn't make you rigorous — it makes you incomplete.

## Occam's counterweight: Hickam's dictum

Doctors learned this lesson the hard way, so they minted a counter-razor. **Occam in medicine** says: find the single diagnosis that explains all the symptoms — elegant, satisfying, often right in a young, otherwise-healthy patient. But against it stands **Hickam's dictum**:

> A patient can have as many diseases as they damn well please.

Reality is not obligated to be tidy for your convenience.

**Worked example.** An 82-year-old arrives with shortness of breath, swollen ankles, a mild fever, and confusion. The Occam-elegant move is to hunt for *one* disease that ties it all together. But in an older patient, multiple chronic conditions are the *norm*, not the exception — heart failure (the swelling and breathlessness), a separate brewing infection (the fever), and dehydration or a medication interaction (the confusion) can all be true at once. Chase the single unifying diagnosis and you can treat one problem while two others quietly kill the patient.

The lesson isn't "Occam is bad." It's that Occam's prior — *bet on one cause first* — must yield the instant the facts demand more than one. **Base rates** decide which razor leads: in a healthy 20-year-old, lean Occam; in a frail 82-year-old, Hickam is waiting in the wings, because the prior probability of multiple simultaneous conditions is just *higher*.

## A pattern needs explanation and may call for a response

A persistent error that benefits one party deserves checking. Direction, benefit and persistence can be relevant evidence. They can also arise from an accidental configuration, an incomplete reporting process or mixed incentives. The outcome alone does not determine which cause operated.

**Worked example:** a rounding rule always overcharges. Its mechanism can be deterministic even if it was introduced by mistake. Examine the configuration, its history and what happened after notification. Records may distinguish specific stories; correcting the overcharge addresses an observed effect. Benefit alone does not establish that someone intended it.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Check the mechanism">
A possible innocent story does not require you to accept it. A favourable pattern does not establish intention either. Seek observations that distinguish explanations and address the damage you can observe.
</Callout>

Error and intent can coexist. A deliberate action can be clumsy; an error can cause serious harm. The previous lesson's E/D catalogue separated them only by stipulation to make one calculation transparent.

### When to use it

When incidents repeat, examine the full pattern and mechanisms that could generate it. Do not multiply incident probabilities as if they were independent without supporting that assumption. Keep attribution open while evidence is missing.

## Separate consequences from probability

The cost of being wrong affects which response is appropriate. It supplies no evidence of intent on its own. In the previous catalogue, S selected eight D and nine E cases. With unchanged-operation losses of zero and twenty, expected loss was one hundred and sixty seventeenths; review cost three. Review had lower expected loss while E remained more probable.

If loss under D fell to two with everything else unchanged, unchanged expected loss became sixteen seventeenths, below three. The preferred response changed while the posterior stayed the same. More serious consequences do not make an explanation more certain.

To decide, state the available responses, their effects and all their costs. Compare them over the same cases and under a declared criterion. A reversible measure can be justified before the motive is known; an accusation needs its own support. If probabilities or consequences are missing, identify the gap instead of filling it with a razor.

## How to hold a razor correctly

So how do you keep the blade pointed away from yourself? Hold every razor as a **starting bet you actively try to disconfirm.** Not a conclusion to protect — a hypothesis to attack.

A practical loop:

1. **State the hypothesis and its scope.** “The configuration could explain the pattern.” Name expected observations and what remains unexplained.
2. **Try to break it.** What evidence *would* prove this wrong? Go looking for that evidence on purpose — this is the direct antidote to **confirmation bias**, where you'd otherwise only collect what flatters the guess.
3. **Check fit.** Does the lean explanation account for *all* the facts, or is it ignoring some? Unexplained facts mean it doesn't fit — and fit beats simplicity.
4. **Compare responses by consequences.** Evidence informs attribution; costs, effects and alternatives also matter when deciding how to respond.
5. **Drop it without ego.** When the lean answer stops fitting, let it go. You were never defending it; you were testing it. Updating a prior on new evidence isn't a defeat — it's the entire skill.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The razor's real job">
A good razor doesn't end the conversation. It *starts* it — by giving you the smartest first thing to check. Used that way, it makes you faster *and* harder to fool.
</Callout>
