“Every additional condition can fail; therefore the explanation with fewer sentences is more probable.” The first part needs a well-defined event. The conclusion compares objects that might not be parts of each other. Let's check where the jump occurs.

## Define the event before counting

An **outcome** is one specific possibility in the model. An **event** is a set of outcomes. **Probability** assigns each event a number between zero and one under the specified model. Only when all outcomes are equally probable can we divide the event's outcome count by the total to obtain its probability.

**Digit card.** Constructed case: generate an ordered triple of decimal digits. Each position takes a value from 0 to 9 with equal probability, and positions are mutually independent. There are $10\times10\times10=1,000$ equiprobable outcomes, including triples beginning with zero. These are not a thousand surveyed people or an observed sample.

| Name | Complete condition | Example meeting it | Example missing it |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | First digit below 9 | 082 | 982 |
| B | Second digit below 9 | 982 | 092 |
| C | Third digit below 9 | 982 | 089 |
| D | First digit equal to 9 | 982 | 082 |

A filter is a useful analogy: keep outcomes that meet a requirement. Its limit is that filtering does not assign probabilities; we need the model's probability allocation.

### When to use this

Before multiplying a list of assumptions, define the event, possible outcomes and the model that supplies probabilities. “Seems quite likely” is not numerical data.

## A conjunction lives inside its parts

The **conjunction** “A and B” requires both conditions in the same outcome; write it as $A\cap B$. Every triple meeting A and B also meets A. Keeping only outcomes passing another filter cannot enlarge the set.

| Event | Complete count | Outcomes | Probability |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | $9\times10\times10$ | 900 | $900/1,000=0.9$ |
| A and B | $9\times9\times10$ | 810 | $810/1,000=0.81$ |
| A, B and C | $9\times9\times9$ | 729 | $729/1,000=0.729$ |

The general rule is:

$$A\cap B\subseteq A\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(A\cap B)\leq P(A)$$

This **does not require independence**. Equality is possible: if A already guarantees the extra condition, nothing is removed. The rule concerns nested events under the same probability allocation. It does not say any explanation written with more conditions is less probable than any rival.

### When to use this rule

Use it to catch an impossible comparison: “A and B” cannot exceed “A” under the same model. Do not transfer it to two hypotheses without first checking whether one implies the other.

## When probabilities multiply

Two events are **independent** if learning that one occurs does not change the other's probability. On the card, independent positions justify:

$$P(A\cap B\cap C)=0.9\times0.9\times0.9=0.729$$

In general, for $P(A)>0$, the two-event rule is:

$$P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)$$

The bar reads “given.” Only if B is independent of A may we replace $P(B\mid A)$ with $P(B)$. The mutually independent digit positions justify the product of the three marginal probabilities on this card; calling three sentences “separate” does not establish that independence.

**Counterexample to unchecked multiplication.** Call “first digit below 9” R. R is exactly A under another letter. A and R retains A's 900 outcomes:

| Calculation | Result |
|---|---:|
| Correct probability of A and R | $900/1,000=0.9$ |
| Unjustified product of marginals | $0.9\times0.9=0.81$ |
| Probability of R given A | $900/900=1$ |

### When to use the product

When independence is stipulated or justified by the model. Otherwise, you need conditional probabilities or other joint-distribution information. Inclusion remains valid; arbitrary multiplication does not thereby become a useful approximation.

## Counting sentences does not compare different explanations

Now compare A and B and C with D. The first requires three conditions and contains 729 outcomes. D uses one condition and contains 100. The three-condition event has greater probability: $0.729>0.1$.

D does not contain the earlier conjunction. They are incompatible: the first digit cannot be both below 9 and equal to 9. Inclusion never ranked this pair by condition count.

Call “all three digits are below 9” **E**. E is exactly A and B and C. Bundling it into one sentence does not make it more probable.

### When to return to Occam

When comparing explanations, ask which commitments are unnecessary for the job. To assert a prior probability, supply a probability model that justifies it. The digit example supplies no probabilities for a deployment, intrusion or human intention.

## Adding a condition and observing evidence

Requiring “A and B” keeps outcomes meeting both conditions inside the original set. **Conditioning** on B means evaluating probabilities inside outcomes compatible with B. The denominator changes.

Observing evidence can therefore increase a probability. In the R=A example, before observing R we have $P(A)=900/1,000=0.9$; given R, $P(A\mid R)=900/900=1$. No contradiction: these are different conditional measures, not a conjunction becoming larger than A.

**Transfer card.** Another constructed case: equiprobable ordered pairs $(u,v)$. Independently, $u$ takes values 0,1,2,3 and $v$ takes 0,1,2,3,4. There are 20 pairs. Define F: $u<3$; G: $v<2$; H: $v=4$.

| Event | Count to check after calculating |
|---|---:|
| F | $3\times5=15$ |
| G | $4\times2=8$ |
| F and G | $3\times2=6$ |
| H | $4\times1=4$ |

<details>
<summary>Check the transfer probabilities</summary>

$P(F\cap G)=6/20=0.3$; $P(H)=4/20=0.2$. The two-condition conjunction exceeds H, a different single condition. $P(G\mid F)=6/15=0.4$, equal to $P(G)=8/20=0.4$ under stipulated independence. Every fraction states its denominator.

</details>

### When to check the denominator

When someone says evidence “adds an assumption, so lowers probability,” ask whether they mean a conjunction, an update using evidence or a different hypothesis. Counting words cannot substitute for those operations.
