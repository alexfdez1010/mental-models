## Exam probability card

Constructed model: all 1,000 ordered triples of digits from 0 to 9 are equiprobable, with mutually independent positions. Include triples beginning with zero. X: first digit below 9. Y: all three digits below 7. By definition Y is included in X; these are not rival causal hypotheses.

| Event | Outcome count | Probability |
|---|---:|---:|
| X | $9\times10\times10=900$ | $900/1,000=0.9$ |
| Y | $7\times7\times7=343$ | $343/1,000=0.343$ |

For the renaming question: A, B and C mean first, second and third digit below 9 respectively. E means “all three digits below 9”: exactly A and B and C, with 729 outcomes out of 1,000.

<Callout variant="success" title="Key takeaways">
A **razor** can guide a provisional comparison; it neither establishes an
explanation nor assigns probabilities by counting sentences. **Occam** avoids
unnecessary commitments for the purpose. A **conjunction** is included in each
part; the card’s independence justifies its product of marginal probabilities.
These rules do not rank any pair of hypotheses by assumption count. **Hanlon's razor** is Occam pointed at people — reach for
incompetence before malice, while treating carelessness as an alternative to check. Counting a description's features assigns
no probabilities and does not exclude malice. But both razors *lie* when you
trust them blindly: Occam over-trims genuinely complex situations (remember
Hickam's dictum), and Hanlon excuses patterned, one-directional, self-serving harm.
Hold each as a starting bet you actively test, update on evidence, and lean on less
as the stakes rise. Used that way, the razors make you faster and less wrong; used
as verdicts, they're just a blindfold you sharpened yourself.
</Callout>
