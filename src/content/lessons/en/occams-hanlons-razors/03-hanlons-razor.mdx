A shared folder has been renamed. Your link breaks, your deadline does not move, and your brain opens a courtroom. The defendant has not even seen the notification.

**Hanlon's razor** is a rule of thumb about explanation: consider an adequate explanation involving error or carelessness before attributing harm to malicious intent. Here, **malice** means an intention to cause harm; **error** means that harm was not intended. Neither word measures how serious the damage is. A careless action can do enormous damage, and a deliberate action can be badly executed.

The useful question is: *what observations would distinguish the explanations?* A second question follows: *what response is justified while the explanation remains uncertain?* They need different answers.

## Separate what happened from why

A parcel arrived late. That is an observation. A missed collection, an incorrect address and a deliberate delay are hypotheses about its cause. Calling someone careless is still an inference; it does not become a fact because it sounds kinder.

The phrase **adequately explained** asks whether a proposed cause accounts for the relevant observations. Merely imagining a possible accident does not establish that it happened. Compare what each explanation predicts: does the record show an address change? Were other parcels on the same route delayed? What would remain unexplained?

### A worked example

The folder's change record shows that a bulk rename affected every folder matching a naming rule, including the operator's own folder. That observation fits a broad configuration error. It weakens the particular story that only your folder was targeted, because the recorded changes contradict that story's claimed selectivity. It does not establish every fact about the operator's motives. A deliberate bulk change, a misunderstanding and a mixture of motives remain possible.

A malicious action need not involve a conspiracy, competence or secrecy. Conversely, an error needs a causal explanation too. Counting the words in the two stories calculates no odds. A setting can also contain both deliberate conduct and mistakes; the two-column toy model later in this lesson deliberately excludes that possibility so that we can inspect one calculation.

### When to use it

Use this separation when the first explanation of an inconvenience arrives suspiciously quickly. Record the action and its effects, list plausible causes and choose a check that could change your mind. You can also set a boundary or repair a process while the motive remains unknown.

## Give circumstances a place without inventing innocence

The **fundamental attribution error** names a tendency to give personal characteristics too much explanatory weight and circumstances too little when interpreting another person's behaviour. Explaining your own conduct by circumstances while an observer explains it by your traits is the related **actor–observer effect**. They are distinct descriptions; neither establishes how every person reasons in every setting.

Suppose a colleague sends a curt message during a migration. “They dislike me” attributes an attitude; it does not establish an intention to harm. “They are under time pressure” offers a situational explanation. The deadline and message are observations; the cause and any intention remain open. A proportionate check might be to ask what the message requires and clarify the deadline. You do not need to diagnose a personality to agree how work will proceed.

The [APA definitions of fundamental attribution error](https://dictionary.apa.org/fundamental-attribution-error) and the [actor–observer effect](https://dictionary.apa.org/actor-observer-effect) describe those concepts. They provide no measured accident-to-malice ratio for this colleague or workplace.

### When to use it

Ask which situational information you lack when you notice yourself turning a brief action into a stable character verdict. Considering circumstances expands the investigation; it does not require accepting a harmless explanation or tolerating repeated damage.

## A pattern can change the odds without proving intent

Consider a rounding setting that always rounds a charge upward. It can repeatedly benefit the recipient because of an accidental configuration. Deliberate conduct is another possible explanation. The direction of the errors matters, but an accidental mechanism need not scatter errors evenly in both directions.

To see exactly what evidence can establish, use this **constructed catalogue**, not observations about real people. It contains one hundred equally weighted cases. Each case has exactly one stipulated cause: E, an error without an intention to harm, or D, an action with an intention to harm. These causes are exhaustive and exclusive only inside this exercise. S is one specified pattern of records, defined before checking the catalogue.

| Stipulated cause | All cases | Cases with pattern S | Cases without S |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| E | 90 | 9 | 81 |
| D | 10 | 8 | 2 |
| Total | 100 | 17 | 83 |

Before observing S, D occupies ten of the hundred cases. S appears in eight of ten D cases and nine of ninety E cases: conditional rates of eighty and ten per cent. The pattern is eight times as frequent within D as within E. That ratio describes this catalogue, not human nature.

Now keep only cases with S. There are seventeen: eight D and nine E. The **conditional probability**, the fraction within cases compatible with what was observed, is therefore $P(D\mid S)=8/17$. This is about forty-seven per cent, compared with ten per cent before S. E is still slightly more frequent within that selected group. S is evidence favouring D relative to its starting probability; it is not proof of D.

The probability before incorporating S is the **prior**; the probability after incorporating it is the **posterior**. Those probabilities come from the stipulated catalogue. Hanlon does not generate the catalogue. We also have not assumed that repeated errors are independent: S is a whole pattern with its own declared counts. Multiplying the rates of individual incidents would require another model.

### The reference set can change the conclusion

In a different constructed population there are thirty E cases and seventy D cases, with the same within-cause rates of S. The selected cases are three E and fifty-six D, so $P(D\mid S)=56/59$, about ninety-five per cent. The observation rule is the same; the starting composition differs.

### When to use it

Use a pattern to choose and assess further checks. Ask whether a persistent process error, reporting filter or incentive could also produce it. If you lack credible rates, say so: a numerical posterior is not available merely because a story feels familiar. The pattern can still justify investigating and addressing its effects.

## A response has a different threshold from a verdict

Return to the first catalogue. We stipulate a separate decision model with two available responses and these **complete losses per case**, in fictional loss credits (cr). Smaller is better. The decision-maker uses expected loss, the probability-weighted mean, and is neutral to risk on this scale.

| Response | Loss if E | Loss if D |
|---|---:|---:|
| Leave the process unchanged | 0 | 20 |
| Temporary review | 3 | 3 |

For this calculation only, review prevents the twenty-credit loss, costs three credits in either state and has no other costs or effects. E has zero loss under unchanged operation by stipulation; real errors need not be harmless. These are constructed alternatives, not a general instruction about access or security.

Before S, leaving the process unchanged has expected loss $0\times 90/100+20\times 10/100=2$. Review costs three. Under the declared criterion, leaving it unchanged has the smaller mean loss.

After S, unchanged operation costs $0\times9/17+20\times8/17=160/17$, about nine point four one credits per case. Review still costs three. Review now has lower expected loss **even though E remains the more probable explanation**. Choosing a precaution does not assign guilt.

The same comparison can be made without rounded probabilities. Across the seventeen S cases, unchanged operation loses $8\times20=160$ credits. Review loses $9\times3+8\times3=51$. Divide both totals by seventeen to recover their per-case means. Add both review groups below; a partial sum is not the full policy cost.

For an arbitrary probability $p$ of D, unchanged expected loss is $20p$, versus three for review. They tie at $p=3/20$. Review has lower expected loss above that threshold; unchanged operation has lower expected loss below it. At the threshold the two responses tie under this criterion. A probability of one half has no special role in this decision.

### Change consequences without changing the evidence

Keep the posterior eight seventeenths. If the loss under D were two instead of twenty, unchanged expected loss would be $16/17$, below three. The preferred response would change. The posterior would remain eight seventeenths: changing what an error costs supplies no new evidence about its cause.

### When to use it

Separate the evidence needed to attribute intent from the evidence and costs relevant to a reversible response. State what the response actually prevents, what it costs and which alternatives you compared. An expected-loss winner in a limited menu is not a moral verdict or proof that the model includes every relevant consequence.

## Try a new catalogue before opening the solution

This exercise changes the counts, signal and losses. Its one hundred equally weighted cases contain eighty E and twenty D. Pattern T appears in eight E and twelve D cases. The response losses are unchanged operation: zero under E and eight under D; review: two under either cause. Review removes the D loss and has no other effects in this model. Use expected loss and the complete two-response menu.

### When to use it

Try this workflow on a low-stakes process problem: distinguish facts and possible causes, check the evidence against alternatives, then compare feasible responses. If you cannot support probabilities or complete losses, leave the numerical result unresolved and name what information is missing. You can still repair an observed problem without pretending to know the motive.
