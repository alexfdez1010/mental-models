An archive wants its catalogue backlog reduced. It also wants difficult records handled, rather than left for someone else's shift. The first proposal is wonderfully simple: award a credit for every accepted record. The shortest records immediately become very popular. The mission statement remains beautifully worded.

This workshop audits the **payoff rule**: the procedure that turns an action and its recorded result into a reward. We will calculate what it favours, change it, and then examine a way the revised measurement can be manipulated. All times, rewards and records are **invented teaching data**. The credits are scores in this exercise, not a proposed employment contract.

Our archive gives one clerk **120 minutes** and a fixed queue:

| Record type | Available | Time per accepted record |
|---|---|---|
| Routine | 24 | 5 minutes |
| Complex | 8 | 15 minutes |

The declared service goal has an order: **first complete at least four complex records, then complete as many accepted records in total as possible**. This minimum is a policy choice for the exercise, not a claim that four is the correct target for real archives.

Use whole records only. The clerk cannot work overtime, split one record into several rewarded units or count the same ID twice. Times are fixed and include the clerk's own checking. Every completed record in the initial calculation passes an independent acceptance check. A separate checker can handle up to 24 records; that capacity is assumed available and does not use the clerk's 120 minutes. The model does not optimize the checker's costs.

The intake register assigns each record's routine or complex category before selection. For now, those categories are fixed. Assume the clerk chooses the feasible plan with the largest credit score. This isolates the rule's effect; real people can also value craft, fairness, helping and other rewards.

### A complete first plan

Four complex records take **4 × 15 = 60 minutes**. The remaining **120 − 60 = 60 minutes** fit **60 ÷ 5 = 12 routine records**. The plan accepts **4 + 12 = 16 records**, including the required four complex ones.

A fifth complex record would use time otherwise available for three routine records. The total would fall to **5 + 9 = 14**. Once the minimum is met, doing more complex records reduces total accepted records in this fixed-time example. The service goal is therefore met best by **four complex and twelve routine**.

### When to write the goal this precisely

Do it before paying for a number. “Clear the backlog” leaves open whether difficult work, total volume, quality or a deadline takes priority. The reward cannot align with an objective you have not specified.

## Calculate what the flat reward selects

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="Every accepted record earns the same credit. Which work is most attractive per minute in this model?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Routine work, because each accepted record takes less time.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Complex work, because management says it matters.' },
    { text: 'Both types are equally attractive per minute because the per-record credit is equal.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Equal reward per record does not mean equal reward per unit of scarce time. One complex record occupies the time of three routine records."
/>

Under the **flat rule**, each accepted record earns **1 credit**. All-routine work accepts **24 records** in 120 minutes and earns **24 credits**. The service-goal plan accepts 16 records and earns only **16 credits**. A score-maximizing clerk can meet the reward rule perfectly while completing **no complex records**.

That response requires no false claim or poor workmanship. It is **task selection**: choosing the short jobs that the reward makes attractive. A good incentive audit looks for legitimate shortcuts as well as cheating.

Here is the complete comparison. For each possible count of complex records, the table fills the remaining time with routine work. Since every extra accepted record earns a positive credit and all times are multiples of five minutes, leaving room for another routine record cannot improve the score.

The next two score columns anticipate redesigns we will examine below. An uncapped premium pays four credits for every complex record. A bounded premium pays four credits for each of the first four complex records, then one credit for every additional complex record. Routine records always earn one credit.

| Complex records | Routine records | Total accepted | Flat credits | Uncapped-premium credits | Bounded-premium credits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 24 | 24 | 24 | 24 | 24 |
| 1 | 21 | 22 | 22 | 25 | 25 |
| 2 | 18 | 20 | 20 | 26 | 26 |
| 3 | 15 | 18 | 18 | 27 | 27 |
| 4 | 12 | 16 | 16 | 28 | 28 |
| 5 | 9 | 14 | 14 | 29 | 26 |
| 6 | 6 | 12 | 12 | 30 | 24 |
| 7 | 3 | 10 | 10 | 31 | 22 |
| 8 | 0 | 8 | 8 | 32 | 20 |

Every row uses **120 minutes**. The flat score is simply the total-accepted column, so its maximum is the first row. We have not observed a clerk's behaviour; we have found the maximizing action under a stated model.

Gibbons distinguishes a worker's total contribution from the performance measure on which a contract pays. They need not move together. Our accepted-count reward omits the mix requirement even when every counted record is genuinely correct. [Gibbons on performance measurement](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.12.4.115)

### When to inspect the whole choice set

Ask whether people can choose customers, cases, task sizes or reporting units. Correctly counting completed work can still encourage neglect of the work your objective requires. More reward on the same count strengthens that comparison rather than changing it.

The **uncapped premium** pays **4 credits per complex record** and 1 per routine record. Four complex plus twelve routine earns **4 × 4 + 12 = 28 credits**. Eight complex earns **8 × 4 = 32 credits**. The reward now favours all-complex work, which meets the minimum but produces only eight accepted records rather than the feasible sixteen.

The **bounded premium** pays 4 credits for each of the **first four complex records in the shift**, then 1 credit for each further complex record. Routine work still earns 1 credit per record. Four complex plus twelve routine earns **16 + 12 = 28 credits**. A fifth complex displaces three routine records but adds only one credit, dropping the score to **16 + 1 + 9 = 26**.

The full table shows a unique maximum at four complex and twelve routine. This matches the declared objective **under the stated categories, times, verification and choice assumptions**. The calculation does not prove that a real workforce will adopt that plan or that the rule covers every valuable activity.

### What this redesign costs and leaves open

The bounded plan awards more credits than the old flat rule's maximizing plan. If credits carry a real cost, that budget matters. Alternatively, the archive could assign a minimum task mix directly rather than try to induce it through points. Both approaches need workable classification and acceptance procedures.

## Audit the category that earns the premium

Consider a flawed implementation of the bounded rule. It accepts self-reported categories instead of joining the completed IDs to the intake register. An invented audit finds **one routine record labelled complex**. All eighteen completed records are correctly catalogued; the error is their rewarded classification.

| View of the same completed IDs | Complex | Routine | Time implied by fixed task times | Bounded score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Self-reported dashboard | 4 | 14 | 4 × 15 + 14 × 5 = 130 minutes | 4 × 4 + 14 = 30 credits |
| Joined to original intake categories | 3 | 15 | 3 × 15 + 15 × 5 = 120 minutes | 3 × 4 + 15 = 27 credits |

The dashboard appears to beat the legitimate maximum of 28 credits and to meet the complex minimum. The intake record shows **three complex completions**, so the service minimum is missed. The apparent extra ten minutes of output is another warning sign under this exercise's fixed-time assumptions.

One false category adds **4 − 1 = 3 credits** to the recorded reward. It does not turn a five-minute routine record into fifteen minutes of complex service. In real work an unexpected time total might also reveal that the assumed task times were wrong; here the original categories and the mislabelled ID are supplied as facts.

### A bounded response to the loophole

Use stable IDs and the original intake category when calculating the reward. Record exceptions and have someone outside the rewarded role review category changes. Check for duplicate IDs, split records and transferred work too. This closes the demonstrated relabelling route; it does not prove every possible route has disappeared.

## Look for work the redesigned score can displace

Our calculation covered accepted records and a mix minimum. A real archive also needs assistance for new colleagues, preparation of incoming work and care of shared equipment. Those duties were outside the finite exercise. If the reward makes them costly to the clerk's score, they can become the next neglected margin.

Holmström and Milgrom's multitask model examines this problem: incentives can redirect attention between duties, particularly when some are measured more readily than others. It does not say that every bonus always causes neglect; its conclusions depend on its model assumptions. [Original multitask analysis, introduction](https://www.sfu.ca/~allen/HolmstromMilgrom.pdf)

Before a small pilot, specify what to record and what would require review. Use the same definitions and independent checks under both the current rule and the candidate rule.

| Question | Evidence to retain | What a warning would mean |
|---|---|---|
| Was the service minimum really met? | Original IDs and intake categories of accepted records | Claimed complex progress may be relabelling or selective counting |
| Did completion quality hold? | Independent acceptance results and correction work | A higher score may accompany lower-quality work |
| Was work displaced to other people? | Assigned queue, transfers and remaining records by type | One clerk's result may move the burden rather than solve it |
| Did the new rule consume more resources? | Clerk time, checker time and credits awarded | The apparent gain may require extra review or reward cost |
| Were unscored duties preserved? | A direct log of scheduled help and preparation duties | The completion score may conceal neglected work |

Do not pay a second bonus for every warning measure immediately. Adding a target also changes what people have reason to manipulate. Some checks can remain independent audit evidence or inform task assignment rather than become another score.

### What a pilot can establish

A single better shift does not establish a reward's causal effect. Work mix, experience and workload may differ. A fair pilot should compare like queues and observation windows, preferably assigning the rules by lottery where feasible, and retain all assigned workers and outcomes. Judge the named service goal, side effects and resource costs together. The arithmetic above justifies a candidate for testing under its assumptions, not automatic adoption. A small comparison can still differ by chance. People sharing a queue can also affect one another, so choose comparison groups and assignment at a level that limits transfers between the groups. [J-PAL on random assignment, uncertainty and spillovers](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/randomization)

Try an independent variation. The shift is still **120 minutes**, routine records still take **5 minutes**, but complex records now take **20 minutes**. Keep the same queue, the same four-complex minimum and the same bounded premium.

Compare these two feasible plans. Before opening the solution, calculate their scores and ask whether the declared service goal is uniquely favoured.

| Plan | Complex records | Routine records | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| All routine | 0 | 24 | 120 minutes |
| Minimum complex service, then routine | 4 | 8 | 4 × 20 + 8 × 5 = 120 minutes |

<details>
<summary>Check the changed-time comparison</summary>

<Callout variant="tip" title="A tie is not unique alignment">
All-routine work earns **24 credits**. Four complex and eight routine earns **4 × 4 + 8 = 24 credits** too. In fact, each of the first four complex records now displaces four routine records while earning those same four credits. After the first four, an extra complex record still displaces four routine records but earns only one credit, so it cannot raise the score above 24.

The reward therefore permits a maximizing plan that misses the complex minimum. A clerk may choose the service-goal plan for other reasons, but the points alone do not select it uniquely. Revisit the reward or task assignment and check the measurement again.
</Callout>

### When to rerun the audit

Recalculate when task times, available work, category definitions, verification or reward values change. Incentive alignment is a claim about a specified choice set and measurement process. Protecting intrinsic motives and consulting the people doing the work still matter; a points table cannot replace those parts of design.
