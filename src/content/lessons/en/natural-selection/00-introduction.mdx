A population can change even when no individual decides to change. If some heritable types leave more surviving offspring than others, their contributions can alter the composition of the next generation. To use that idea, specify what gets copied, who contributes and which population you are counting.

This course introduces **natural selection** as a biological process and a carefully bounded thinking tool. A successful product or a common idea can suggest a selection hypothesis. Explaining its history still requires evidence about the process.

A **trait** is a characteristic you can record, such as a colour type. **Variation** means that the population contains differences in that characteristic. **Inheritance** means that a characteristic is transmitted between parents and offspring.

In a specified environment, differences in heritable traits can affect reproductive success and thereby contribute to changes across generations. A **selection explanation** identifies that link; it needs more than a change in the final count. Other processes, including arrivals and chance differences, can also affect a population. [The Open University introduces selection alongside other causes of evolutionary change](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/nature-environment/natural-history/evolution-through-natural-selection/content-section-1/?printable=1).

## Work through a declared example

Imagine sixteen organisms of two types: four A and twelve B. In this constructed model each A parent leaves three surviving A offspring and each B parent leaves one surviving B offspring. The new census counts offspring only. No individuals arrive and no labels change.

Four times three gives twelve A offspring. Twelve times one gives twelve B offspring. The next census therefore contains twenty-four individuals, half A. Initially A represented four of sixteen, one quarter. These conclusions follow from the stated rule; they are not observations of a real species.

## Specify a transfer before using the analogy

A copied document template offers a simple analogy: different templates can leave different numbers of copies, and copied features can persist. Human choice can deliberately change those features or the copying rule. Name those changes instead of assuming that every domain behaves like a biological population.

Ask what counts as a type, what counts as an offspring or copy, and how the counted feature is transmitted. Survival alone does not supply every one of those answers. Neither does calling the eventual winner “fit.” In this course, reproductive contributions are always tied to a defined population, environment and stage.

## The route through the course

Five teaching lessons build the model and its limits:

1. [The recipe](/natural-selection/01-the-recipe) introduces variation, reproduction and inheritance.
2. [Fitness and pressure](/natural-selection/02-fitness-and-pressure) considers reproductive success in an environment.
3. [Selection beyond biology](/natural-selection/03-the-algorithm-everywhere) explores proposed analogies and their copying processes.
4. [What evolution is not](/natural-selection/04-what-evolution-is-not) examines claims about goals, group benefit and progress.
5. [A generation-census audit](/natural-selection/05-audit-a-generation-census) reconstructs exact records and compares histories with matching final counts.
