A type of organism occupied a quarter of a population. In the next census it occupies half. Someone announces: “Selection favoured it.” Before accepting the explanation, ask for the records behind those two fractions. Births, arrivals and changes to the recorded trait can produce similar totals.

This workshop uses **constructed records**, with every individual accounted for. You will first calculate a population under a declared reproduction rule, then compare histories that end with the same counts. The distinction matters whenever you use natural selection as a mental model: a pattern to explain and a demonstrated mechanism require different evidence.

## Fix the population and the census

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="A recorded type doubles its share between two censuses. What does that establish before you inspect births or arrivals?"
  options={[{text:'Its share changed in the recorded population.',correct:true},{text:'Its members must have left more inherited offspring.'},{text:'Each original individual changed its own trait.'}]}
  explanation="The two shares describe the censuses. Explaining their difference requires records about the processes between them."
/>

A **census** counts the individuals included at a specified stage. Here one **generation** consists of parents producing offspring that replace them in the next census. The parents are absent from that second count. A **trait** is the characteristic used to classify an individual as A or B.

Think of two folders of numbered forms being copied into a new filing cabinet. Count the originals, then the new copies; keep a link from each copy to its original. A copier makes transmission easy to stipulate. In a biological investigation, parentage, inheritance and surviving offspring require evidence.

Our initial population has sixteen parents:

| Parent group | Identifiers | Parent count | Surviving offspring from each parent |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | A1 through A4 | 4 | 3 |
| B | B1 through B12 | 12 | 1 |

Reproduction is **asexual** in this model: each offspring has one parent. Every A parent produces three surviving A offspring; every B parent produces one surviving B offspring. Count them at the same offspring stage. No other individuals enter, no offspring leave, and no label changes. These are the stipulated rules of the example.

### When this boundary matters

Specify the stage before comparing generations. Counting eggs in one period and breeding adults in another changes the quantity. Including both parents and their offspring would also change our denominator and invalidate the calculation below.

The **absolute count** of A has risen from four to twelve. Its **share** asks a second question: how much of the whole census is A? At the start that is $4/16=1/4$; afterward it is $12/24=1/2$. Equal final counts arose from unequal original group sizes and unequal contributions per parent.

### A useful total, with a limited meaning

The population grew by a factor of $24/16=3/2$. That factor compares total counts. It does not tell you A's share, which needs A's count and the offspring total. Use a complete count to check whether a claimed denominator contains everybody it should.

## Separate a census identity from a prediction

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="An investigator calculates each group's mean offspring count from completed family records. Does that alone give a reliable rule for the next generation?"
  options={[{text:'Yes, a completed average is automatically the future rule.'},{text:'Only the larger group can supply such a rule.'},{text:'A future prediction needs support for carrying those contributions forward.',correct:true}]}
  explanation="A mean can summarize this generation exactly. Predicting a later generation requires assumptions or evidence about reproduction and the new circumstances."
/>

A **mean contribution per parent** divides a group's surviving offspring by its parent count. Write these observed means as $M_A$ and $M_B$. Our main construction also stipulates every family's count in advance, so its means are three and one by design.

| Quantity | Operation | Value |
|---|---|---|
| A mean contribution | 12 offspring / 4 parents | 3 offspring per parent |
| B mean contribution | 12 offspring / 12 parents | 1 offspring per parent |
| Whole-population mean | 24 offspring / 16 parents | 3/2 offspring per parent |

Let $p$ be A's share of the parent census. The population mean weights each group's contribution by its share:

$$\overline M=pM_A+(1-p)M_B.$$

Here $\overline M=(1/4)3+(3/4)1=3/2$. Under our closed population and faithful transmission, A's share in the offspring census is:

$$p'=\frac{pM_A}{pM_A+(1-p)M_B}.$$

Write $N_A$ and $N_B$ for the numbers of A and B parents. To derive the formula, start with A offspring divided by all offspring: $N_AM_A/(N_AM_A+N_BM_B)$. Divide numerator and denominator by the original total $N_A+N_B$. The resulting parent shares are $p$ and $1-p$. With our data, $p'=(3/4)/(3/2)=1/2$.

The formula is a **bookkeeping identity** when the contributions come from completed records and the transmission/closure assumptions hold. It becomes a prediction when you supply a reproduction model whose contributions are justified before observing the next census. A larger realized family, by itself, does not establish a heritable causal advantage.

Biologists distinguish individual reproductive outcomes from summary measures of expected reproductive contributions. The vocabulary depends on the model and life cycle. Here $M_A$ and $M_B$ name the defined census contributions; they do not estimate an intrinsic, permanent property of a type. [Orr's discussion of fitness measures and selection equations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2753274/).

### When to use the formula

Use it to reconstruct a closed census or to work through a specified reproduction rule. Check transmission and arrivals first. The offspring census must contain at least one individual: if neither group leaves surviving offspring, its total is zero and A's share is undefined. If a mean denominator is zero because a parent type is absent, that group's observed mean is undefined; do not manufacture a number by dividing by zero. Our worked groups both contain parents.

## Compare relative share with absolute growth

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="Both groups' stipulated offspring contributions double while the parent census stays the same. What happens to A's offspring share?"
  options={[{text:'It doubles because each A parent contributes twice as much.'},{text:'It stays the same because both contributions share the multiplier.',correct:true},{text:'It must fall because the total population grows.'}]}
  explanation="The common multiplier appears in both numerator and denominator of the share. It cancels, while the absolute counts grow."
/>

Keep the same four A and twelve B parents. Now every A parent leaves six offspring and every B parent leaves two. You obtain $4\times6=24$ A and $12\times2=24$ B, totaling forty-eight. A's share remains $24/48=1/2$.

| Rule | A offspring | B offspring | Total offspring | A share | Total / parent count |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Three and one | 12 | 12 | 24 | 1/2 | 3/2 |
| Six and two | 24 | 24 | 48 | 1/2 | 3 |

### What this comparison buys you

When someone says a type “did better,” request the quantity: more individuals, a larger share, or a larger contribution per parent. A common scaling of contributions preserves the share in this particular model. Real crowding, migration or changed reproduction could break the premises; the arithmetic does not establish that scaling leaves a real ecosystem unaffected.

## Check the history behind a matching total

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="Two histories have the same final counts of A and B. Which additional record could distinguish inherited descendants from new arrivals?"
  options={[{text:'The final A share written as a percentage.'},{text:'The final total written in larger type.'},{text:'Links to local parents and a separate arrival register.',correct:true}]}
  explanation="Changing the notation preserves the same information. Parentage and arrival records add information about how the census was assembled."
/>

Keep the original four A and twelve B parents. Compare three deliberately different histories. Each finishes with twelve A and twelve B individuals.

**History Copy** is our main rule: three same-type offspring per A parent, one per B parent, no arrivals. All twenty-four individuals are locally born offspring with their parent's label.

**History Arrival** gives every parent exactly one same-type offspring. That produces four A and twelve B offspring. Eight new A individuals then arrive from elsewhere before the census. You still count twelve A and twelve B, but the local families have equal contributions per parent. The population is open to arrivals.

**History Mark** uses the main family sizes, producing twelve offspring from each parent group. An external marking rule then assigns six A labels and six B labels within each ancestry group. The label is assigned afresh instead of faithfully copied. This is an artificial record-auditing example; it does not describe a biological mutation mechanism.

| History | Locally born A at census | Locally born B at census | A arrivals | B arrivals | Final A | Final B |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copy | 12 | 12 | 0 | 0 | 12 | 12 |
| Arrival | 4 | 12 | 8 | 0 | 12 | 12 |
| Mark | 12 | 12 | 0 | 0 | 12 | 12 |

The next table follows **ancestry**. Rows identify the parent's group; columns identify the child's recorded label. Arrivals are excluded from these family counts.

| History | A-parent → A-child | A-parent → B-child | B-parent → A-child | B-parent → B-child |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copy | 12 | 0 | 0 | 12 |
| Arrival | 4 | 0 | 0 | 12 |
| Mark | 6 | 6 | 6 | 6 |

In Mark, each ancestry row totals twelve, just as in Copy. Their child labels differ. A family-size record and a transmission record answer separate questions.

### What records to request

An equal final fraction does not choose among these histories. Request parentage, locally born offspring, arrivals and transmission information at the defined census stage. In a real investigation, resemblance in one generation alone also needs care: a shared environment can make parents and offspring resemble one another. Our examples specify their mechanisms so that you can audit the consequences.

Natural selection is one process that can contribute to evolutionary change. Other processes can change population composition, and selection can also maintain a pattern rather than produce a directional trend. [The Open University's introduction distinguishes evolution, selection and chance changes](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/nature-environment/natural-history/evolution-through-natural-selection/content-section-1/?printable=1).

## Transfer to a declining population

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="A type occupies a larger share of a smaller population. What must you check before saying that its number increased?"
  options={[{text:'Its absolute count in both censuses.',correct:true},{text:'Whether its percentage is written with a decimal.'},{text:'Whether its name sounds like a successful trait.'}]}
  explanation="A larger fraction can accompany a smaller count when the total falls. Calculate both quantities."
/>

Start again with four A and twelve B parents. This is a **separate completed family record**:

- Two A parents leave one surviving A offspring each; the other two leave none.
- Two B parents leave one surviving B offspring each; the other ten leave none.
- Offspring inherit the recorded type. Parents leave the next census, and no individuals arrive.

Before opening the solution, calculate the offspring counts, A's share, each group's mean contribution and the whole-population mean. Then decide whether A's increased share requires an increase in its absolute count.

<details>
<summary>Check the declining-census calculation</summary>

| Quantity | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A offspring | 2 × 1 + 2 × 0 | 2 |
| B offspring | 2 × 1 + 10 × 0 | 2 |
| Whole offspring census | 2 + 2 | 4 |
| A share | 2 / 4 | 1/2 |
| A mean contribution | 2 / 4 | 1/2 offspring per parent |
| B mean contribution | 2 / 12 | 1/6 offspring per parent |
| Whole-population mean | 4 / 16 | 1/4 offspring per parent |

A's count fell from four to two. B's fell from twelve to two. A's share rose from a quarter to a half because its count fell proportionally less.

The group means are fractions because they average whole offspring counts across parents. No parent leaves a fraction of an offspring. The ratio $(1/2)/(1/6)=3$ matches the main example, while the whole-population means differ: one quarter here, three halves there.

Checking the identity gives:

$$p'=\frac{(1/4)(1/2)}{(1/4)(1/2)+(3/4)(1/6)}=\frac{1/8}{1/4}=1/2.$$

These realized group means summarize the listed families. They do not specify exact future family sizes for a new population. In particular, applying a mean of one sixth to a later pair of B parents yields one third as an algebraic product. Interpreting that product as an average of future outcomes would require an additional model; it does not count a third of an actual offspring.

### When the distinction changes an interpretation

Use both count and share when a population expands or contracts. A growing share describes relative representation. It does not by itself establish population growth, future persistence or a cause for the observed difference.

## Choose records that can test the explanation

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="You want to investigate whether a heritable difference explains a change in population composition. Which plan adds relevant information?"
  options={[{text:'Rank the types by how impressive they look.'},{text:'Track parentage, offspring traits, arrivals and conditions across the declared interval.',correct:true},{text:'Treat the final proportion as a complete explanation.'}]}
  explanation="Those records help distinguish the processes in the workshop. They still require a defensible design and interpretation in a real investigation."
/>

A census is useful precisely because its quantities have clear limits. It can establish how many counted individuals carry a label and where documented offspring or arrivals came from. Explaining a heritable causal advantage requires more than noticing the label's final frequency.

| Question | Record or assumption to inspect | A finding that would change the calculation |
|---|---|---|
| Who belongs in each census? | Stage, time and inclusion rule | Parents and offspring were counted together |
| How many descendants came from each group? | Linked surviving offspring at the same stage | Several counted individuals were arrivals |
| Was the recorded trait transmitted? | Parent/offspring traits and their circumstances | An external rule assigned the offspring marks |
| Can a contribution be carried into a forecast? | Reproduction model and relevant conditions | The environment or family-size rule changed |
| What does “increased” mean? | Both absolute counts and shares | The total population contracted |

Consider one edge case. Start with only B parents, keep faithful copying, and allow no arrivals or new types. Every offspring is B. A hypothetical advantage for an absent A type cannot produce an A individual under that rule: its contribution to the count is $0\times3=0$. You do not calculate an observed A-family mean from zero A parents. The main rule needs an existing type to copy; changing how new types arise would be a different model.

Biological fitness comparisons refer to reproductive contributions in a particular population and environment. Whole organisms and their life cycles matter; a single conspicuous characteristic need not supply the complete explanation. [OpenStax introduces those limits in its discussion of adaptive evolution](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/19-3-adaptive-evolution).

### When this audit is useful

Use it when a claim jumps from “this type became common” to “this trait caused its success.” Begin with the census boundary and the records that could distinguish the proposed mechanism from alternatives. In domains such as products or ideas, specify what is copied and what counts as a descendant; simply calling a successful item “fit” supplies no new measurement.

## Reconstruct the record before the recap

Without looking back, write down the original parent counts, the two Copy family contributions and the two final offspring counts. Calculate A's share and total population growth separately. Then name the record that distinguishes Copy from Arrival and the one that distinguishes Copy from Mark.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copy | 4 | 12 | 12 | 12 |
| Scaled | 4 | 12 | 24 | 24 |
| Declining | 4 | 12 | 2 | 2 |

Copy and Scaled transmit the parental type and have no arrivals. The declining record also transmits types, with actual families of one or zero offspring. Arrival instead has four locally born A, twelve locally born B and eight A arrivals.
