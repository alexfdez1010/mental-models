Every model is a map, and every map lies a little — it leaves things out so it
can show you the road. **Comparative advantage** is one of the best maps in all
of economics: it tells you to specialize where your **opportunity cost** (what
you give up to do one thing instead of another) is lowest, and trade or delegate
the rest. We have watched it work. Maya and Sam reorganized the *same* 20 hours
of work around comparative advantage and conjured 10 extra shirts out of thin air.
The office example compares \$400 of Nadia’s forgone work per brief with \$80 of Owen’s, under equal quality and zero handoff cost.

But a model you can only recite is a model that will eventually burn you. The
person who truly *uses* comparative advantage knows exactly where it stops being
true. This lesson examines four limits of the simple model. The following workshop turns them into a numerical production and exchange audit.

## The #1 trap: confusing absolute with comparative advantage

This is the central error of the entire course, so let's state it as sharply as
possible. The sentences **"I'm better at it,"** **"I'm faster,"** and **"I'd do
it well"** are all claims about **absolute advantage** — being more productive in
raw terms. And absolute advantage does not by itself settle whether you should do the task. Production rates still matter when checking time and capacity.

Why? Because the question is never "Am I good at this?" The question is "What do I
give up by spending my time *here* instead of somewhere else?" That is opportunity
cost, and opportunity cost is what comparative advantage runs on.

Re-drill the complete task. Nadia needs one hour per brief and Owen two, for the same acceptable result. Their genuinely available alternatives are worth \$400 and \$40 per hour respectively. With the stated zero handoff cost, the brief forgoes \$400 for Nadia or **2 × 40 = 80** for Owen. Owen has the lower opportunity cost for that task. Nadia’s speed affects task time; it does not alone settle the allocation.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The trap, in one sentence">
"I'm better/faster at it" is an **absolute-advantage** claim. The decision about
who should do the task depends on **comparative** advantage — on opportunity cost.
The moment you justify keeping a task with "but I'm good at it," stop: you're
answering the wrong question.
</Callout>

If you take one thing from this whole course, take this. Nearly every real-world
misuse of the model — the founder who won't delegate, the surgeon doing their own
scheduling, the country that insists on making everything itself — is someone
mistaking absolute advantage for comparative advantage.

## Transaction costs can eat the gains

Specializing and then trading is not free. Every handoff carries overhead:
coordinating schedules, negotiating terms, communicating requirements, managing
the other party, shipping the goods, checking the work. Economists bundle all of
this into **transaction costs** — the friction of *doing business with someone
else* instead of just doing it yourself.

The model promises a surplus from trade. Transaction costs quietly subtract from
that surplus. And here is the uncomfortable part: if the friction costs more than
the surplus, the trade is a *loss*, even though comparative advantage said "go."

Worked example. Maya and Sam's reorganization created a +10-shirt surplus. Now
suppose arranging that trade — finding each other, agreeing on a price, hauling
shirts and loaves back and forth — burns the equivalent of 12 shirts of effort.
The +10 surplus is now a net **−2**. They would be richer making their own bread
and sewing their own shirts, badly. The gains were real; the friction was bigger.

The everyday version: a manager who spends 30 minutes explaining a 10-minute task,
then 20 minutes reviewing it, has not delegated — they have *added* work. The
delegation created no surplus once you count the coordination.

The rule that fixes this is blunt and worth memorizing:

> **For a strict net gain, the surplus must exceed the additional cost of creating it.** If it falls short, the proposed trade loses to the stated alternative; equality is a tie.

Comparative advantage tells you *which direction* the gains lie. Transaction costs
tell you whether they're worth reaching for.

## Transition and switching costs

Even when specialization pays off in the long run, *getting there* costs money,
time, and pain — and those costs land now, not later. Moving to a new division of
labor means **switching costs**: retraining people, the output you lose while
everyone is still learning, and other future costs that can still be avoided. **Sunk investment** already spent and unrecoverable is not a new switching cost: do not charge that past expenditure again to the proposed change.

A calculation may report an aggregate gain over a chosen horizon. That total does not show which participants benefit, when costs arise or whether they can absorb them.

In a change involving trade between countries or regions, higher aggregate output depends on the particular production and exchange plan. Even if it rises, a factory closure can impose losses on the affected workers and community. An aggregate gain alone does not establish that every participant is better off.

Include prospective retraining, lost output and other transition costs in the comparison. Potential gains do not justify treating those costs as zero; nor should unrecoverable past spending be charged again.

## When resources can't move (immobility)

If a forecast counts gains from a new use of released time, check that the use is actually available. In the office example, the counted alternative is Nadia’s legal work. If it is unavailable, that forecast cannot count its revenue. This is not a rule that any idle hour eliminates every possible gain: the next workshop includes a higher-output plan with one hour left idle.

**Immobility** means a resource cannot make the move assumed by the plan. A worker can take a proposed new job only if that job exists, is reachable and any required training is feasible. Count the consequences of that actual alternative, rather than crediting an unavailable one.

> Name the benefit attributed to the released time and check it against the stated objective. More output, valued rest and learning are different possible benefits; none is established merely by freeing an hour.

Before delegating, finish the sentence: “This plan improves the objective because ______.” Then compare the complete outcomes, including what each participant gives up.

## Other cracked assumptions (brief)

A few more places the map smooths over rough terrain:

- **It assumes you can measure opportunity costs.** Real opportunity costs are
  often fuzzy, uncertain, or unknowable until after the fact. Garbage inputs,
  garbage verdict.
- **The simple output examples use a specific objective.** More quantity is not automatically better if quality, learning, resilience or self-sufficiency matter. Include those criteria when defining the decision.
- **It assumes prices and values are stable.** If a good's value swings, today's
  comparative advantage can be tomorrow's mistake.
- **Concentration can create fragility.** Exposure to a single activity can matter if conditions change. Comparative advantage does not establish that complete specialisation always maximises output; requirements and other objectives can favour a mixed plan.

## Using the model wisely

None of this means comparative advantage is wrong. It remains the **default lens**
for any "who should do what?" decision — between people, teams, or countries.
Start there, always. Then run three checks before you commit:

1. **Am I confusing absolute for comparative?** If your reason is "I'm better/
   faster at it," you haven't actually used the model yet. Ask about opportunity
   cost instead.
2. **Does the surplus beat the costs?** Subtract transaction costs *and* transition
   costs from the predicted gain. If what's left is negative, do it yourself.
3. **Is the benefit attributed to released resources feasible?** Check the proposed new use and its contribution to the objective. Do not count output from an unavailable alternative.

<Callout variant="success" title="The wise user's checklist">
Comparative advantage is the right starting question — almost always. Just don't
stop there. Confirm you're reasoning about **opportunity cost**, not raw skill;
confirm the **surplus survives the friction** of trading and transitioning; and
confirm that any **benefit attributed to released resources is feasible and valued by the objective**. Passing these checks supports the plan under the stated assumptions; uncertain inputs and individual allocations still need checking.
</Callout>
