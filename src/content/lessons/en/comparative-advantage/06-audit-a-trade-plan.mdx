**Comparative advantage** uses relative opportunity costs to identify a useful production direction. It does not pack the van, reserve the handoff time or check what each person needs to keep. A promising trade can fail before anyone argues about its price.

This workshop turns the course's limits into a procedure: define the objective, calculate the best no-trade alternative, fit whole production batches into each time budget and audit the final allocation. You will distinguish an efficient production plan from an exchange both participants will accept.

## Define success before assigning production

Ada and Bruno prepare materials for two community events. The following is a **constructed planning model**, not measured productivity or a recommendation for an actual operation.

Each has **8 available hours before the same deadline**, after breaks. Materials are unlimited, quality is identical, and no rework or output loss occurs. A production batch takes **one uninterrupted hour** and yields the quantities below. Batches cannot be split or carried past the deadline. Posters and packs can be exchanged only as whole units; a pack is a standardised bundle of programmes, not a single page.

| Producer | One poster batch yields | One pack batch yields | Own minimum at delivery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ada | 4 posters | 2 packs | 12 posters and 10 packs |
| Bruno | 1 poster | 3 packs | 3 posters and 15 packs |

These are **consumption requirements**, after exchange. Ada may obtain packs from Bruno; she does not have to produce every pack she keeps.

For this exercise, each person first requires the stated number of packs and posters. Once those requirements are met, each additional usable poster is valuable; packs beyond the minimum add no further value. The planning objective is to **maximise combined posters while meeting both people's requirements**. We will also compare each person's posters with their best no-trade alternative. A larger combined total alone does not establish that both gain.

Two alternatives are allowed:

- **No exchange:** each retains their own output and has all eight hours for production.
- **One barter cycle:** Ada sends some posters to Bruno and Bruno sends some packs to Ada. Each spends **one hour** coordinating, checking and handing over the exchange, leaving at most seven whole production batches each. There is no money, borrowing, third party or other compensation. Waiting and handoff time are included in that hour.

Idle time is allowed. We will account for it rather than inventing a fractional batch to fill it.

## Calculate the no-trade alternative in the same units

Without exchange, Ada needs **10 ÷ 2 = 5 pack batches**, leaving **8 − 5 = 3 poster batches**. She produces **12 posters and 10 packs**. No other no-trade allocation gives her more posters while meeting her pack requirement.

Bruno needs **15 ÷ 3 = 5 pack batches**, leaving three poster batches. He produces **3 posters and 15 packs**.

| Best no-trade plan | Poster hours | Pack hours | Exchange hours | Final bundle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ada | 3 | 5 | 0 | 12 posters, 10 packs |
| Bruno | 3 | 5 | 0 | 3 posters, 15 packs |
| Combined output |  |  |  | **15 posters, 25 packs** |

Here the best no-trade bundles exactly match the stated minima. That is a feature of these numbers, not a general equivalence between “minimum acceptable” and “best alternative.” In another case, the outside option could be better than a contract's minimum.

## Use the cost ratios to find a direction

Ada can make **4 posters instead of 2 packs** in one batch hour. The batch-average trade-off is **2/4 = 1/2 pack per poster**. Bruno can make **1 poster instead of 3 packs**, a ratio of **3 packs per poster**. Thus Ada has comparative advantage in posters and Bruno in packs.

The reciprocal ratios are **2 posters per pack for Ada** and **1/3 poster per pack for Bruno**. These are ratios of the production alternatives. Because batches are indivisible, they do not mean Ada can actually switch a quarter of an hour to produce one extra poster.

In the **divisible, zero-exchange-cost version** of these rates, the usual marginal price benchmark would be:

**1/2 < packs received per poster sent < 3**

[David Ricardo's original foreign-trade example](https://www.marxists.info/reference/subject/economics/ricardo/tax/ch07.htm) explains why relative production costs can support an exchange even when a producer is more efficient in absolute terms. Our additional batch, requirement and handoff assumptions need their own audit; the classical comparison does not remove them.

### Why complete specialisation fails here

After reserving one hour each for exchange, complete specialisation would make Ada produce **7 × 4 = 28 posters** and Bruno **7 × 3 = 21 packs**. The events need **10 + 15 = 25 packs**. Four packs are missing. No price or transfer can create them.

Ada must therefore keep some pack production, despite Bruno's comparative advantage in packs. A candidate plan is:

| Audited partial plan | Poster batches | Pack batches | Exchange hours | Production before exchange |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ada | 5 | 2 | 1 | 20 posters, 4 packs |
| Bruno | 0 | 7 | 1 | 0 posters, 21 packs |
| Combined |  |  |  | **20 posters, 25 packs** |

## Transfer goods and audit the actual exchange ratio

Let **q** be the whole posters Ada sends and **r** the whole packs Bruno sends. The price is **r/q packs per poster**, not q/r in the same units.

For the partial plan, the final bundles are:

- Ada: **20 − q posters**, **4 + r packs**.
- Bruno: **q posters**, **21 − r packs**.

Ada needs at least 10 packs, so **r ≥ 6**. Bruno must retain at least 15, so **r ≤ 6**. Therefore **r = 6**. Ada must retain at least 12 posters, so **q ≤ 8**; Bruno needs at least three, so **q ≥ 3**.

The whole-unit exchanges preserving both outside options are exactly:

| Posters q | Packs r | Price in packs per poster | Ada's posters | Bruno's posters | Poster gain versus no trade |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 6 | 2 | 17 | 3 | Ada +5, Bruno unchanged |
| 4 | 6 | 3/2 | 16 | 4 | Ada +4, Bruno +1 |
| 5 | 6 | 6/5 | 15 | 5 | Ada +3, Bruno +2 |
| 6 | 6 | 1 | 14 | 6 | Ada +2, Bruno +3 |
| 7 | 6 | 6/7 | 13 | 7 | Ada +1, Bruno +4 |
| 8 | 6 | 3/4 | 12 | 8 | Ada unchanged, Bruno +5 |

Both retain their exact pack requirements in every row. If **both must gain strictly in posters**, only q equal to **4, 5, 6 or 7** qualify. The four prices are **3/2, 6/5, 1 and 6/7 packs per poster**, a discrete set. Writing a continuous interval between the lowest and highest would incorrectly admit prices no whole-unit exchange in this plan implements.

Our proposed exchange is **5 posters for 6 packs**: its price is **6/5 = 1.2 packs per poster**. Ada keeps **15 posters and 10 packs**, a gain of three posters; Bruno keeps **5 posters and 15 packs**, a gain of two. The five extra posters were shared. Total goods are conserved through the transfer: **15 + 5 = 20 posters**, **10 + 15 = 25 packs**.

A price within the relaxed cost window is therefore not sufficient. At **2 packs per poster**, for example, q is three and Bruno is merely indifferent under our defined preferences. At other prices, there may be no allowed whole-unit transfer or a person's requirement may fail.

## Prove the plan is best within the declared menu

A feasible example is not automatically the optimum. We can check every permitted production choice without guessing a price first.

Let **a** and **b** be Ada's and Bruno's poster-batch counts. Each has at most seven production batches. Even if every remaining batch makes packs, their maximum combined packs are:

**2(7 − a) + 3(7 − b) = 35 − 2a − 3b**

To reach 25 packs, **2a + 3b ≤ 10**. Their total posters are **4a + b**. The integer possibilities can be bounded by Bruno's poster batches:

| Bruno poster batches b | Largest possible Ada poster batches a | Largest combined posters at that b |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5 | 20 |
| 1 | 3 | 13 |
| 2 | 2 | 10 |
| 3 | 0 | 3 |
| 4 or more | None | Cannot cover the pack total |

The upper bound is **20**, attained by a = 5 and b = 0, with Ada's two and Bruno's seven pack batches. Since we have also exhibited an acceptable exchange, the bound is attainable. This proves optimality **for the declared objective, rates, costs and permitted trades**, not for every possible event-planning objective.

For completeness, checking all nonnegative whole batch counts, including idle time, leaves just three production patterns with an exchange preserving both minimum bundles:

| Ada poster/pack batches | Bruno poster/pack batches | Idle production capacity | Permitted exchanges (q, r) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 / 2 | 0 / 7 | Ada one hour | (3,6), (4,6) |
| 4 / 3 | 0 / 7 | None | q is 3 or 4; r is 4, 5 or 6 |
| 5 / 2 | 0 / 7 | None | q is 3 through 8; r is 6 |

These are **14 allowed exchanges** across three production patterns. Four make both strictly better off in posters; all four use the twenty-poster plan. We did not assume every available hour had to be filled before checking feasibility.


The gain criterion matters. A **Pareto improvement** makes nobody worse off and at least one participant better off, judged by their preferences; requiring both to gain strictly is stronger. Our count of four requires **more posters for each**, with the pack minima preserved. It does not count every possible improvement under other preferences.

For example, use the sixteen-poster pattern: Ada produces **16 posters and 6 packs**, Bruno **0 posters and 21 packs**. Send **3 posters for 5 packs**. Ada keeps **13 posters and 11 packs**, Bruno **3 posters and 16 packs**. Both have at least their original quantities of each good and more of some good. If extra packs also had value, both could gain strictly. Under our declared preferences, Bruno is indifferent: he still has three posters, and packs above fifteen add no value. This is already a Pareto improvement under our stated preferences: Ada gains and Bruno is no worse off. It is not a strict gain for both. Maximising posters and judging gains under a different preference are distinct questions.

## Change the coordination cost and keep the outside option

Suppose a barter now takes **2 hours per person** rather than one. No-trade production still has eight hours each; only the exchange branch loses the additional time. [Coase's discussion of organising exchange](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1468-0335.1937.tb00002.x) makes coordination and contracting costs explicit. Our particular hours are stipulated, not estimates from his paper.

Repeating the old production plan would require **5 + 2 + 2 = 9 hours for Ada** and **7 + 2 = 9 for Bruno**. Both budgets are exceeded.

Nor can a different six-batch exchange plan rescue both minimum bundles:

**Maximum packs = 2(6 − a) + 3(6 − b) = 30 − 2a − 3b**

Requiring at least 25 gives **2a + 3b ≤ 5**. If b is zero, a is at most two, giving at most eight posters. If b is one, a is at most one, giving at most five. Larger b cannot improve that maximum. The exchange branch therefore supplies at most **8 posters** while covering the pack total, below the required **15**. No permitted exchange preserves both minima. **The no-trade plan remains feasible.**

The batch constraint also makes sensitivity uneven. At **half an hour** of coordination, each still has only seven complete one-hour production slots, so the twenty-poster optimum remains. At **more than one hour**, fewer than seven full slots fit and this mutually acceptable exchange menu fails. A small time change near that boundary matters more than the same change far from it. This follows from the stipulated batches, not a universal rule about coordination.

### What to measure before transferring the model

Check completed-batch yields at the required quality, available hours after breaks, the full handoff burden, which goods can be divided and each participant's actual requirements and alternative. Observe whether additional posters really have the value assumed here. A batch that can be split, a delayed delivery that is acceptable or a valued extra pack changes the feasible menu or objective. Recalculate rather than treating our twenty-poster result as a rule.

## Transfer to signs and fixtures

Cora and Devon each have **6 hours**. A full one-hour batch gives Cora **2 signs or 1 fixture**, and Devon **1 sign or 3 fixtures**. Each needs **3 fixtures**. Extra fixtures have no value in this exercise; additional signs do. Without trading, each maximises signs after meeting that fixture requirement. A barter costs **one hour each** and uses whole goods and batches, as before.

A proposed complete-specialisation plan makes Cora produce ten signs and Devon fifteen fixtures. It sends three signs for four fixtures. Before reading the solution, calculate the best no-trade bundles and decide whether both gain. Then try a different production plan.
