## The master decision: optimise or satisfice?

Before you pick an option, pick a **mode.** The most valuable habit from this whole course is
to ask, of the decision itself: *does this deserve the full search, or should I set a bar and
stop?* Three factors help frame that judgement; they are not a necessary-and-sufficient test.

- **Stakes.** How much does getting it right actually matter? A wrong sock is trivial; a wrong
  house or spouse is not. High stakes tilt toward optimising.
- **Repeatability.** Is this a one-off, or a decision you make again and again? A repeated
  decision (a pricing rule, a hiring process) rewards investment in getting the *procedure*
  right — optimise the process once, then satisfice each instance. A one-off can also repay careful comparison when its benefit warrants the cost.
- **Tractability.** *Can* you even optimise? Is the option set small, listable, and cheaply
  scored (Lesson 1)? If enumeration exceeds the budget or the alternatives cannot be listed, that exhaustive method is unavailable. A structured exact method or a better-specified problem may still be possible.

Use these factors to judge whether a more thorough comparison is likely to justify its cost. A small, fully specified problem may warrant exact optimisation even when it is a one-off. A high-stakes but poorly understood task may need better evidence or expert review rather than an arbitrary acceptance bar. Repeated use can make improving a procedure valuable, but repetition is not required for optimisation to be worthwhile.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The triage question, in one line">
Compare the benefit of a better decision procedure with its costs and feasibility. Stakes, repetition and tractability inform that comparison; they do not determine the answer by a universal formula.
</Callout>

## Setting the aspiration level

Once you choose an aspiration-based procedure, set the standard as part of a complete protocol. Four practical levers:

1. **Anchor it to reality, not fantasy.** Set the bar from what's actually available (comps,
   salary bands, a quick scan of the field), not from an imagined ideal. An aspiration
   calibrated to a dream you'll never meet is just disguised maximising.
2. **Review soft preferences against evidence.** Information about available options or search costs may justify a different aspiration. An adaptive standard still needs evaluation; neither raising nor lowering it is automatically better.
3. **Keep hard requirements, limits and fallback explicit.** A deadline does not make an unsafe or inaccessible option feasible. Decide what happens if nothing qualifies: take a reserved alternative, stop without a purchase or follow another declared feasible action.
4. **Write the protocol down before starting.** Include eligibility, aspiration, order, costs, recall rights and a stopping limit. If the aspiration may change, state its update rule. Do not rewrite the rule after seeing the outcome and call that prospective validation.

## Where it transfers

The model fits any decision with a large-or-unlistable option set, real search costs, and
tolerable "good enough" outcomes — which is most of them.

- **Hiring.** You can't interview everyone. Set the bar ("clearly stronger than a solid
  mid-level, ships independently, no red flags"), interview until someone clears it, and hire.
  Optimise the *process* (repeated), satisfice each *req* (one-off within the process).
- **Buying a laptop / house / car.** Define the objective, mandatory requirements and search costs, then compare feasible procedures (Lesson 3). A threshold can save inspections without guaranteeing a better net outcome or greater satisfaction. Reconsider when relevant new evidence changes the comparison, and include any reopening costs.
- **Choosing where to eat while travelling.** Scan a few blocks to calibrate, then commit to the
  first place that clears the bar. Walking the strip twice means eating late and cranky, having
  "optimised" yourself out of dinner.
- **Product & design.** Ship the version that clears the quality bar and hits the date; don't
  gold-plate toward a perfection users can't perceive and the schedule can't afford. "Good
  enough to ship" is an aspiration level.
- **Organisational decisions.** Simon built bounded rationality partly to describe *firms*:
  organisations satisfice (hit targets, clear thresholds) far more than they maximise, because
  optimising across a whole enterprise is wildly intractable. Good management is largely
  well-set aspiration levels.

## Where the model lies

Bounded rationality is powerful precisely because it's forgiving — which is also how it can
seduce you into bad thinking. Here are its honest limits, named plainly.

| The model can be taken to mean… | But actually… | What to do instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Satisficing means I don't have to think hard" | A chosen aspiration can perform poorly; resource limits alone do not prove it optimal | Choose and evaluate the bar deliberately; following an inadequate rule does not make it good |
| "Optimising is always wrong / a waste" | A feasible optimisation can be worth its cost; stakes, repetition and tractability inform the judgement | Triage first; reserve real optimisation for the few decisions that earn it |
| "Good enough is always fine" | 'Good enough' can **entrench mediocrity** and hide a problem that deserved the full search | Watch for decisions where the bar is quietly too low for the stakes |
| "Simple heuristics always beat complex models" | They win only when **ecologically matched**; misfire when the environment shifts | Ask whether the rule fits *this* terrain before trusting it |
| "Set the bar and never move it" | A soft aspiration may be revised under a stated rule; mandatory requirements remain binding | Review the evidence, feasible actions and fallback rather than assuming a universal decline |

Three deserve a closer look.

### Satisficing is not an excuse

Resource constraints do not prove that a particular aspiration is good. A low standard remains a satisficing rule even if it performs badly. Specify the objective and requirements, then ask how the procedure performs in the environment it actually faces. A chosen aspiration becomes an optimal reservation threshold only when a justified model and calculation establish that equivalence.

### Some things deserve optimising

Bounded rationality does not prohibit exact optimisation. A finite set with known comparable payoffs may be cheap enough to solve completely. A repeated decision can justify substantial work on the procedure; a one-off decision can too if the expected benefit warrants it. Conversely, high stakes do not make an underspecified problem tractable. These are judgements about evidence, cost and feasibility, not an automatic sorting formula.

### Heuristics are adaptations, not magic

Lesson 4's punchline — simple rules can beat complex ones — is true *ecologically*, not
universally. A fast-and-frugal rule is an adaptation to an environment, and adaptations get
**stranded when the environment changes.** The recognition heuristic that nails city sizes
betrays you on stock picks; 1/N that beats optimisation in noisy markets loses when data is
abundant and stable. Never carry a heuristic into new terrain without asking whether it still
fits.

## Sort the decisions

The most valuable skill from this course isn't setting a bar — it's the **triage** that comes
before it: sorting decisions into satisfice-worthy and optimise-worthy by stakes,
repeatability, and tractability.
