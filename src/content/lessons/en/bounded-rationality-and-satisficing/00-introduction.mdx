Picture the decision-maker economics textbooks quietly assume you are. Faced with a choice,
this creature knows **every** option that exists, assigns each a precise payoff, and lifts
the single best one into their basket — instantly, at no cost, every time. Call them *Homo
economicus*, the perfect optimiser. Now picture what you actually did the last time you
chose a restaurant, a laptop, or a job: you looked at a handful of options, found one that
seemed fine, and **stopped.** You did not survey the space. You did not rank everything. You
took something **good enough** and got on with your life.

The gap between those pictures is the subject of this course. Real decision-makers have **bounded** time, information, attention and computing power. For an open-ended or very large problem, exhaustive optimisation may exceed those resources or require information the chooser does not possess. This does not rule out exact optimisation for a small, well-specified task.

One response is **satisficing**, Herbert Simon's term combining *satisfy* and *suffice*: set an **aspiration level**, a standard for good enough, and accept the first option that meets it. **Bounded rationality** studies decision-making under such limits. It motivates attention to feasible procedures; it does not prove that any chosen aspiration maximises expected net benefit. [Simon's original account](https://cooperative-individualism.org/simon-herbert_a-behavioral-model-of-rational-choice-1955-feb.pdf)

<Callout variant="tip" title="The one-sentence version">
Limited resources make the procedure of choosing matter: a satisficing rule stops at a declared standard, and its performance depends on the standard, costs, available information and environment.
</Callout>

## See it in one picture

The lab uses independent simulated quality draws from one to one hundred. It subtracts inspection costs on the same point scale. A **satisficer** takes the first option meeting its aspiration; if none qualifies, it must take the last. A **full-search maximiser** pays to inspect every option and then selects the best. That retrospective choice is an explicit model condition.

Move the aspiration and inspection-cost controls. A high aspiration can require many inspections; a low one can accept low quality early. The curves show seeded simulation averages, not a guarantee for each trace or a universal threshold. The “quality gap” measures the difference from the best option in the generated field, not psychological regret.

## What you'll walk away with

By the end you'll be able to look at a decision and ask the two questions that matter:
*should I optimise this, or satisfice it?* and *what's my aspiration level — my bar for good
enough?* Here's the map:

1. **Why optimisation fails** — combinatorial explosion, unlistable options, and the cost of
   computation itself, so you can identify when exhaustive comparison exceeds the available resources.
2. **Satisficing & the aspiration level** — define a chosen standard and distinguish it from an optimal reservation threshold derived under a model.
3. **Maximisers vs satisficers** — the psychology, the *paradox of choice*, and why chasing
   the best can leave you worse off.
4. **Fast-and-frugal heuristics** — Gigerenzer's *ecological rationality*: simple rules that
   often match or beat complex optimisation because they don't overfit.
5. **Transfer — setting aspiration levels** — how to pick and adjust the bar, and which
   decisions to satisfice and which to optimise — plus where the model lies.
6. **[Audit a search protocol](/bounded-rationality-and-satisficing/06-audit-a-search-protocol)** — replay costs and information, enforce a fallback and derive a small reservation-threshold example.

<Callout variant="info" title="Where we're headed">
Keep the comparison visible: accepted benefit minus every inspection cost, under declared rules about information, recall and fallback. Ask what the result shows in this model before carrying the rule elsewhere.
</Callout>

## How to use this course

Every lesson opens with a quick guess, teaches the idea through a concrete story and worked
numbers, and checks that it stuck. Don't skip the guesses — committing to an answer *before*
you know is one of the most reliable ways to actually remember. You'll drive the satisficing
lab from several angles and meet sorting and matching exercises along the way.

This is an **expert-tier** course. It draws on **optimal stopping** for model-based stop-versus-continue comparisons, **the value of information** for the benefit and cost of another inspection, and **circle of competence** for limits on what you know. The six teaching lessons culminate in a graded final exam. It is one-way: submitting an answer locks it, and the score appears only at the end.

<Callout variant="warning" title="One habit to build as you go">
When you want to check one more option, write down the requirements, aspiration, search cost, limit and fallback. Ask whether continuing fits the declared objective and available information. A threshold makes the rule reproducible; it does not by itself prove the rule optimal.

</Callout>
