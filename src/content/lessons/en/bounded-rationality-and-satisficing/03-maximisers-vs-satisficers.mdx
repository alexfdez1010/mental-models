Two people choose the same presentation template. One inspected three alternatives; the other inspected six. The purchase record tells you what they selected. It does not tell you which person feels happier, what either intended, or whether the extra search paid for itself.

Keep three records: **the procedure followed, the outcome after costs, and the person's reported experience**. They answer different questions. This distinction lets us use the psychology of maximisers and satisficers without turning a research association into a personality verdict or a universal stopping rule.

## Distinguish a goal, a procedure and a measure

In a **satisficing procedure**, the chooser sets an aspiration and accepts the first eligible option meeting it. A **full-search procedure** inspects every option in a defined set and selects the best by its declared criterion. These are instructions you can replay.

In psychological research, a **maximising tendency** usually means a measured tendency to seek the best. A **self-report scale** combines answers to questions into a score. That score is not a recording of every action. Different scales can combine goals, alternative search and difficulty deciding differently.

Cheek and Schwartz's **2016** review separates the goal of choosing the best from the strategy of searching alternatives. Their proposed model treats regret and decision difficulty separately from those components. This is a proposal for clarifying measurement, not an agreed law that all scales or people fit. [Original review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/on-the-meaning-and-measurement-of-maximization/F4790B9B64558405453CA105B0C4EDA1)

| Record | Example entry | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Find the highest benefit in a stated set | Which procedure was actually followed |
| Procedure | Stop at the first eligible option meeting the aspiration | A stable personality or guaranteed result |
| Measured tendency | Responses to a named questionnaire | Random assignment to a search procedure |
| Experience | Reported satisfaction after choosing | The selected benefit or search cost |

A high aspiration does not mean exhaustive search. Someone can require step-free access, suitable equipment and strong quality, then stop at the first qualifying venue. Conversely, inspecting every option in a small spreadsheet does not establish a lifetime of agonising over choices. Use these distinctions when describing a search: record the action before labelling the person.

Consider a constructed audit of **six presentation templates**. All meet mandatory format and accessibility requirements. Benefits use **planning credits**, a cardinal exercise scale on which inspection costs can also be valued. This is not a happiness scale or a star rating.

The order is fixed, but each benefit is learned only after paying to inspect that template. Inspected templates remain available until the deadline, with free recall. Compare rules fixed before searching:

- **T:** take the first eligible benefit of at least **85 credits**. If none qualifies after six inspections, take the last template, the declared fallback.
- **F:** inspect all **six**, then return to the highest-benefit template.

The complete table is an auditor's later record. It does not give either chooser future values at an earlier stopping point. Both rules start with the same information and have the same inspection costs, eligibility constraints and recall rights; they use those rights differently.

| 2 | 84 | Reject: below aspiration |

Let **c** be each inspection's cost in planning credits. Final net benefit is **selected benefit − c × inspections**. T selects 85 after three inspections; F selects 90 after six. Count rejected and accepted inspections alike.

| Cost per inspection | T: benefit less search cost | F: benefit less search cost | Better of these two rules on this record |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 0 | 85 − 0 = 85 | 90 − 0 = 90 | F |
| 1 | 85 − 3 = 82 | 90 − 6 = 84 | F |
| 2 | 85 − 6 = 79 | 90 − 12 = 78 | T |

At cost **1**, full search wins despite its positive inspection cost. At cost **2**, the extra three inspections cost **6**, exceeding F's **5-credit** benefit advantage. Compare the *increment*, rather than asking whether search has any cost at all.

The boundary follows from **F − T = (90 − 6c) − (85 − 3c) = 5 − 3c**. They tie at **c = 5/3 credits per inspection**. Above it T wins this comparison; below it F wins.

This compares **two prespecified rules on one sequence**. It proves neither rule optimal among all feasible policies, nor which has higher expected benefit on unknown future sequences. A probability model or prospective evaluation would address that different question.

## Read a simulation without inventing feelings

The lab is a **different model**, not a replay of the six template values. It generates independent uniform integer qualities from one to one hundred. A threshold takes the first meeting its aspiration; if none qualifies, it must take the last. Full search inspects the whole field and recalls its best option. Inspection costs and quality use the same point scale.

The curve and table show **seeded simulation averages** over many fields. The strip shows one complete field for audit, including values the threshold chooser did not inspect. Changing aspiration changes that rule's stopping point, not those values. Dealing another example changes the strip, not the average curves.

Here **quality gap = highest quality in the complete field − selected quality**. In the template record, the analogous **benefit gap** for T is **90 − 85 = 5** and F's is **0**. At cost two, however, T has greater net benefit. A zero quality gap need not mean the best net outcome.

**Psychological regret** is a person's experience of wishing the decision had been different. The lab measures none of it. A five-point gap can coexist with satisfaction, disappointment or neither; these need separate observations. The same applies to expectations, social comparison and the burden of imagining alternatives sometimes proposed to explain research findings.

Use the simulator to investigate its rules. Investigate experience using appropriate observations; adding imagined unhappiness to the curve would create data we do not have.

## Ask what a psychological study identifies

In **Iyengar, Wells and Schwartz's 2006 job-search study**, students completed a maximisation questionnaire and were followed during their search. In that cohort, higher maximisation scores were associated with higher starting salaries and lower job satisfaction, both self-reported. They were **not randomly assigned** to threshold or full-search rules. Participation and follow-up were incomplete. Salary is one outcome, not total job value or happiness. [Primary study](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/874/874.pdf)

An **association** is a pattern of variables moving together. An **intervention** deliberately changes something, such as assigning a search procedure. Adjustment for measured differences can be informative, but does not turn questionnaire groups into a randomised intervention. The study does not promise a pay rise or improved satisfaction from changing your stopping rule.

Cheek and Schwartz's measurement review adds another check: a score containing questions about difficulty deciding is not equivalent to one focused on wanting the best. Inspect what the measure includes before interpreting its relationship with distress. [Measurement review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/on-the-meaning-and-measurement-of-maximization/F4790B9B64558405453CA105B0C4EDA1)

For example, a template-search team could record the rule followed, selected benefit, inspections and their cost, then ask about satisfaction separately. To estimate the effect of assigning rules, it could randomly assign consenting participants to feasible protocols on comparable tasks, checking compliance, missing outcomes and uncertainty. A finite observed difference would still be an estimate of that specific intervention's effect, not an exact universal effect.

## Treat choice overload as a contextual question

**Choice overload** is the possibility that a larger option set worsens a specified outcome, such as decision completion or satisfaction. Fewer purchases, lower satisfaction and longer decision time are different observations.

A **meta-analysis** combines multiple studies. Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd's **2010** review found a mean effect near zero with substantial variation; this does not prove overload never occurs. Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman's **2015** analysis found that task difficulty, option-set complexity, uncertain preferences and decision goals help explain variation. Their data and methods differ; neither supplies a universal menu-size limit. [2010 primary review](https://scheibehenne.de/ScheibehenneGreifenederTodd2010.pdf), [2015 primary review](https://chernev.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ChoiceOverload_JCP_2015.pdf)

Imagine testing a searchable template catalogue against a small shortlist. A shortlist might cut comparison time while excluding the only accessible template one participant needs. A larger catalogue might work well when a useful filter makes that template easy to find. These are possibilities to test, not results of a study we ran.

Keep eligibility, task and information explicit. Record completed selections, suitable-option coverage, time and reported satisfaction separately. A fall in purchases alone cannot tell you whether people were paralysed by fear, disliked their choice, or deliberately postponed it.

Use this evidence when designing a choice environment: test a filter, clearer attributes or a shortlist against the objective. Do not remove relevant options merely to hit a supposedly scientific magic number.

## Transfer the comparison and specify when to review

Audit a different completed template sequence: **72, 80, 83, 86, 87 credits**. All five meet the hard requirements. The chooser knows the order and protocol, not future values. Inspected templates remain available for free recall until the deadline.

T accepts the first benefit of at least **80**, with the last template as fallback if none qualifies. F inspects all five and returns to the highest benefit. Initially every inspection costs **2 credits**. The objective is final net benefit; no satisfaction scores are supplied.

Before opening the solution, identify each rule's selected benefit, inspection count, net benefit and benefit gap. Then change only cost to **3**. Does the preferred rule stay the same?

<details>
<summary>Check the transfer ledger and its limits</summary>

| Cost per inspection | T selected benefit / inspections | T net benefit | F selected benefit / inspections | F net benefit |
|---:|---|---:|---|---:|
| 2 | 80 / 2 | 80 − 4 = 76 | 87 / 5 | 87 − 10 = 77 |
| 3 | 80 / 2 | 80 − 6 = 74 | 87 / 5 | 87 − 15 = 72 |

F's extra benefit is **7**, and it performs **three** extra inspections. Thus **F − T = 7 − 3c**, tying at **c = 7/3**. F wins at cost two; T wins at cost three. Benefit gaps remain **7 for T** and **0 for F**. None of these numbers establishes who feels happier.

Suppose verified later evidence makes the selected 80-credit template ineligible because it fails the required accessibility test. This changes a hard constraint. Rebuild the inputs rather than enforcing “never reconsider”. On the corrected record, T would reject it and accept **83 after three inspections**, giving **77 at cost two**; F still selects **87 after five**, also giving **77**. The defective option's inspection still counts. For this counterfactual replay, assume each inspection reveals eligibility as well as benefit at the unchanged cost. This is not an account of the extra costs of reopening an already completed search. Nor is the corrected record what the original chooser knew before discovering the defect.

</details>

For your protocol, state the objective, requirements, cost, aspiration, horizon and fallback before searching. Declare review triggers: a failed requirement, material new information, changed availability or a documented cost change. Reopening has costs too; count them when they occur. Repeating a search without relevant new information differs from correcting a newly discovered requirement failure.

The ledger tests the procedure against its numerical objective. If satisfaction matters, measure it separately and decide how it enters the objective. You cannot infer it from how many tabs someone opened.
