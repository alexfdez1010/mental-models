A radio producer stops looking after finding an acceptable studio. Later, someone points to a better studio farther down the list. Was stopping a mistake? Start with what the producer knew, what another inspection cost and which offers remained available. A complete audit ledger can contain information the chooser never had.

You will replay a predeclared satisficing rule, compare several rules on that record and solve a different, small model in which an optimal stopping threshold can actually be derived. Following a rule and proving the rule optimal are separate jobs.

## Specify the objective before opening an option

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A studio has the highest advertised quality but fails a mandatory access requirement. How should a search protocol treat it?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Reject it as ineligible, even if its quality exceeds the aspiration.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Accept it if the quality gain offsets the failed requirement.' },
    { text: 'Lower the requirement after paying to inspect the studio.' }
  ]}
  explanation="A hard requirement defines which options can be chosen. A quality aspiration operates within that eligible set; it does not buy permission to violate the requirements."
/>

Consider a **constructed one-session decision**. A community radio station needs a studio with step-free access, the required multitrack equipment and the agreed recording slot. All three are **hard eligibility requirements**.

The station uses **planning credits**, invented cardinal benefit units for this exercise. Studio values already include the station's assessment of session benefits and all booking/production expenses, **before inspection costs**. Every inspection consumes **4 credits** of opportunity cost measured on that same scale. We are not subtracting minutes from star ratings.

The audited outcome is:

$$
N=V-4n
$$

Here $V$ is the benefit of the studio actually selected, $n$ is the number of paid inspections and $N$ is final net planning benefit. Every paid inspection counts, including an ineligible, rejected or accepted studio.

| Protocol term | Fixed before search |
|---|---|
| Aspiration | At least 75 credits, among eligible studios |
| Search order | A, B, C, D, E |
| Information | Pay before learning that studio's eligibility and value |
| Recall | A rejected offer expires; it cannot be recovered later |
| Limit | At most 16 inspection credits: four inspections before the deadline |
| Fallback | A reserved eligible studio worth 62 credits, known without inspection |
| Fallback availability | Remains available through the deadline; no additional inspection charge |
| Stopping | Accept the first eligible studio meeting the aspiration; otherwise use fallback at the limit |

The aspiration is a **chosen acceptance standard**, not a value derived from a probability model of future studios. No such model is supplied here. The rule also needs a stopping limit and a fallback: “keep looking until something works” is not a complete plan when time or money runs out.

Simon distinguished the alternatives considered by a chooser from the alternatives objectively available, and examined sequential search for satisfactory outcomes. His account does not establish that every chosen aspiration maximises expected net benefit. [Simon's original model](https://cooperative-individualism.org/simon-herbert_a-behavioral-model-of-rational-choice-1955-feb.pdf)

The first three paid inspections reveal:

| Inspection | Revealed studio value | Eligible? | Decision under the chosen aspiration | Cumulative inspection cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 82 | No: access requirement fails | Reject | 4 |
| B | 70 | Yes | Reject: below 75 | 8 |
| C | 78 | Yes | Accept and stop | 12 |

The station selects **C after three inspections**. Final net benefit is **78 − 12 = 66 credits**. A's high score did not repair its failed requirement. Rejecting A did not refund its inspection. The accepted C also cost a look.

## Compare replays without turning hindsight into foresight

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="An auditor later discovers that a higher aspiration would have produced more net benefit on this record. What follows?"
  options={[
    { text: 'That rule did better in this replay; prospective superiority is not established.', correct: true },
    { text: 'The original chooser knew the later offer and irrationally ignored it.' },
    { text: 'The higher aspiration is now proved optimal for every future sequence.' }
  ]}
  explanation="A realised replay comparison is useful evidence about that path. It is not a probability model of future paths or permission to use unobserved values in an earlier decision."
/>

<details>
<summary>Open the complete record for an offline audit</summary>

This table is an **auditor's constructed ledger**. It was not fully visible to the chooser following the original rule.

| Arrival order | Studio | Value | Eligible? | Inspectable before the limit? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A | 82 | No | Yes |
| 2 | B | 70 | Yes | Yes |
| 3 | C | 78 | Yes | Yes |
| 4 | D | 93 | Yes | Yes, if search has continued |
| 5 | E | 99 | Yes | No: the four-inspection deadline has passed |

</details>

Replay a second rule with the **same eligibility, order, costs, deadline, fallback and no recall**, but aspiration **90**. It rejects A, B and C, inspects D and accepts it. Inspection cost is **4 × 4 = 16**; final net benefit is **93 − 16 = 77 credits**. That beats the original rule's **66** on this record.

Both replays use only their own observed prefixes when deciding. The comparison does not show that the original chooser could know D's value at C, or that aspiration 90 has the highest expected benefit before search. That requires a model of possible sequences or a suitable prospective evaluation. A threshold selected after seeing this ledger has been fitted to this path.

Nor can we claim the station should have taken E. E's value is high in the complete record, but it cannot be inspected within the declared time limit. A comparison must respect the same feasible actions and information. An omniscient chooser with different deadlines is a different benchmark.

Order matters too. In a **different stipulated order with D first**, the original aspiration accepts D after one inspection, giving **93 − 4 = 89 credits**. We have not assumed random arrival or independent draws. These are explicit records illustrating how a policy's result depends on the environment it meets.

## Plan for no qualifying option

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="The inspection limit is reached and no eligible studio meets the aspiration. What should this predeclared protocol do?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Use the feasible reserved fallback and retain all inspection costs.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Inspect the next studio because an unmet aspiration cancels the limit.' },
    { text: 'Recover an earlier rejected offer despite the no-recall condition.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The fallback is part of the rule, not a failure to follow it. A limit, recall condition or hard requirement cannot be silently removed when the search disappoints."
/>

A third replay uses aspiration **95**. A is ineligible; B, C and D fall below the aspiration. After the fourth inspection, no acceptable offer has appeared. The station takes the reserved fallback:

**Fallback value 62 − inspection cost 16 = final net benefit 46 credits.**

It does not pay to inspect E, revisit C or treat A as accessible. The fallback's value is known for free, but search already consumed resources. The same fallback taken without any inspection would have yielded **62**; those are different histories.

This is why “lower the bar as time runs out” needs care. A predeclared revision of a **soft quality aspiration** may be useful, but access, equipment and the deadline are hard constraints in this model. Lowering a score cannot make an ineligible studio feasible. You can also stop and take the fallback; abandoning a requirement is not the only response to an unsuccessful search.

For a decision about **one more inspection**, costs already incurred are common to stopping and continuing. Compare the additional benefit with the additional cost from now. Keep past costs in the full ledger, without charging them again as a new marginal expense. Whether continuing is justified still needs beliefs or bounds about the unseen offer; “we already spent so much” does not supply them.

### When to use the audit

Use it when a search has a real limit and someone must explain why it stopped. Record order, revealed information, costs and fallback before seeing the outcome. This works for a planned threshold, an adaptive rule or a calculated policy, provided the update rule and available information are explicit.

## Derive a reservation threshold only after stating a model

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="What would make an aspiration an optimal reservation threshold rather than merely a chosen standard?"
  options={[
    { text: 'A derivation using the objective, feasible actions, costs and distribution assumptions.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Naming it a reservation value after an acceptable option appears.' },
    { text: 'Showing that it stopped one recorded search before the deadline.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Threshold-shaped rules can look alike while having different justifications. Optimality refers to a specified objective and model, not to the shape or name of a rule."
/>

Now change the conditions. A risk-neutral chooser holds an eligible offer worth **x credits**. It remains available with **free recall**. There is exactly **one inspection left**, costing **8 credits**. Its eligible offer is worth **50 or 90 credits**, each with probability **one-half**, known in advance. There are no delays or other costs.

Stopping gives **x** from now. Inspecting lets the chooser retain the better of the held offer and the newly revealed one:

$$
I(x)=\tfrac12\max(x,50)+\tfrac12\max(x,90)-8
$$

At **x = 70**, inspecting gives **½ × 70 + ½ × 90 − 8 = 35 + 45 − 8 = 72**. Stopping gives **70**, so inspection has **2 credits** of expected advantage. If the new offer is only 50, the chooser keeps 70 but has paid 8, finishing at **62** from this decision point. The expected advantage is not a guarantee of a better realised result.

    { label: 'High draw: half of the new offer', value: 45 }
  ]}
  rebuiltLabel="Selected contributions before inspection cost"
  instructions="Add the probability-weighted retained values, then deduct the inspection cost in the calculation."
  revealAllLabel="Add both outcomes"
  resetLabel="Start over"
  valuePrefix=""
  valueSuffix=" credits"
  verdictTemplate="Expected retained value is {total} before inspection cost. Subtract the stated cost before comparing inspection with stopping."
  caption="Recall is what keeps the held offer in the low-draw contribution. Without recall, that contribution would be different."
/>

For **50 ≤ x ≤ 90**, the expected advantage of inspection simplifies to:

$$
I(x)-x=\tfrac12(90-x)-8
$$

Set it to zero: **90 − x = 16**, hence **x = 74**. Below **74**, inspect; above **74**, stop; at **74**, both give equal expected benefit. At **78**, inspecting yields **76 < 78**, so stop. For held values below 50, inspection's expected net is 62 and exceeds the held offer; above 90, inspection merely returns the held offer minus its cost. These bounds complete the comparison.

Here **74 is an optimal reservation threshold for this stated one-inspection problem**. It was calculated from the available actions and known distribution, rather than chosen because it sounds demanding enough. It does not replace the original studio aspiration of 75: that search had no recall, several steps, hard eligibility and no supplied distribution for unseen studios.

Weitzman's search model derives reservation values from distributions, search costs and a recall/fallback structure. Those conditions matter; the word “threshold” alone does not establish optimality. [Original MIT working paper](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/aadfb4ca-8ef9-4aaf-ab8d-1422cd035c20/content)

## Transfer by changing recall and the distribution

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="If inspecting a new offer makes the held offer disappear, which payoff must be removed from the inspection calculation?"
  options={[
    { text: 'The ability to keep the held offer after an unfavourable new draw.', correct: true },
    { text: 'The inspection cost, because losing recall makes information free.' },
    { text: 'The value of stopping now, before the held offer disappears.' }
  ]}
  explanation="No recall changes what continuation permits. Stopping now still retains the held offer, while inspecting can force acceptance of an inferior new one."
/>

A **new constructed search** concerns a venue for a short listening session. The chooser holds an eligible offer worth **55 credits**. One final inspection costs **5**; the new eligible venue is worth **20 or 80**, each with probability **one-half**. Credits again put benefits and inspection costs on one declared scale. The objective is expected net benefit from now, and the chooser is risk-neutral.

This time, **inspecting forfeits the current offer** and the chooser must take the revealed new venue. Stopping now keeps the current offer. There are no other alternatives, delays or costs.

<MCQ client:visible
  question="Under the new no-recall conditions, what should the chooser do?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Stop with the held offer; inspection has lower expected net benefit.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Inspect, keeping the current offer automatically if the new one is worse.' },
    { text: 'Inspect because the largest possible new value exceeds the held value.' },
    { text: 'Stop only after paying to inspect and then returning to the held offer.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Use the actions this case actually allows. The worked solution compares stopping with the compulsory new-offer gamble, including its cost."
/>

<details>
<summary>Compare the no-recall result with a recall counterfactual</summary>

Without recall, inspection's expected net is **½ × 20 + ½ × 80 − 5 = 10 + 40 − 5 = 45 credits**, below stopping's **55**. Stop. Its indifference held value is **45** under these conditions.

If free recall were instead guaranteed, inspection would have expected net **½ × 55 + ½ × 80 − 5 = 27.5 + 40 − 5 = 62.5**, above **55**. In that different model, inspect. For held values between 20 and 80, its indifference equation is **½ × (80 − x) − 5 = 0**, giving **x = 70**.

Same costs and distribution, different feasible continuation: the thresholds are **45 without recall** and **70 with recall**. Neither comes from copying the studio aspiration or the earlier reservation threshold. If the recall version inspects and draws 20, it retains 55 but pays 5, finishing at **50**: expected optimality still does not guarantee an improved realised result.

</details>
