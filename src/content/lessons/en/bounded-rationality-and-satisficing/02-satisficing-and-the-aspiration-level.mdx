Herbert Simon's alternative to an exhaustive search has a deliberately awkward name: **satisficing**, from *satisfy* and *suffice*. Decide what **good enough** means, search until an option meets that standard, then **stop and take it**. This lesson makes the procedure concrete and distinguishes a chosen acceptance standard from an optimal stopping threshold. They can have the same form without the same justification.

## The two moving parts

The core procedure has two components. A practical search also needs a limit and a feasible alternative if nothing meets the standard.

1. **The aspiration level.** A concrete threshold that defines "good enough" — a bar an
   option must clear to be acceptable. For a flat: "two bedrooms, under £1,500, within 30
   minutes of work." For a hire: "clearly stronger than a solid mid-level engineer." The
   aspiration is your **standard**, set (ideally) before you start.
2. **The stopping rule.** Search options one at a time and **accept the first one that clears
   the aspiration.** The moment you find good enough, you're done — you don't keep looking to
   see if something even better is out there.

This procedure does not require an exhaustive comparison. Notice what it *doesn't* require: you never need the full option
set or a score for every alternative, and you need not compute the maximum over the entire field. You need only be able to
judge, one option at a time, *does this clear my bar?* — a question you can actually answer
with bounded information.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Satisficing in one line">
**Set a bar for 'good enough,' search until something clears it, then stop.** Two knobs — the
**aspiration level** (how high the bar sits) and the **stopping rule** (take the first
clearer). No full menu, no scoring everything, no maximum. Just: is this good enough? If yes,
done.
</Callout>

## A worked example: hiring a contractor

You need a contractor to remodel a kitchen. The optimiser's fantasy — interview every
contractor in the city, score each on price, quality, and timeline, and hire the global best
— is both impossible (you can't list them all) and absurdly costly (each meeting eats an
afternoon). So you satisfice.

**Set the aspiration.** From two friends' recent jobs and a bit of research, you decide "good
enough" means: quoted under £22,000, at least 4 stars across 20+ reviews, and available to
start within a month. That's your bar.

**Search and stop.** You call contractors one at a time:

- **Contractor 1:** £25,000, 4.6 stars, available soon. *Over budget* — fails the bar. Skip.
- **Contractor 2:** £20,000, 3.9 stars, available soon. *Rating too low* — fails. Skip.
- **Contractor 3:** £21,000, 4.5 stars across 20+ reviews, starts in three weeks. *Clears every part of the bar.*
  **Hire them. Stop.**

You did not find the single best contractor in the city — there may well be a £19,500,
4.9-star gem three phone calls away. But you found one who is **genuinely good enough** after
three calls instead of thirty, and you got your weekends back. Whether stopping was wise
depends on the bar, inspection costs, alternatives and available information. The example reproduces the chosen rule; it does not prove that this contractor maximises expected net benefit.

## A chosen aspiration is not automatically an optimal reservation value

An **aspiration level** is the acceptance standard a chooser uses. An **optimal reservation threshold** is derived by comparing stopping and continuing under a specified objective, distribution, cost, horizon and set of available actions. A chosen aspiration can coincide with such a threshold when those assumptions and the derivation justify it. Merely accepting the first option above a bar does not establish optimality.

| Question | Chosen aspiration | Optimal reservation threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Where does it come from? | A selected standard, perhaps informed by experience | A stop-versus-continue calculation under a model |
| What does following it show? | The option meets the declared standard | The chosen action has maximal expected value among the model's permitted actions |
| Must it fall as time runs out? | No universal update rule | Its time pattern depends on the model |
| Can it ignore mandatory requirements? | No | No: feasibility is part of the model |

For a small **constructed calculation**, hold an eligible offer worth 70 benefit points. It remains available for free. One final inspection costs 8 points and reveals an eligible offer worth either 50 or 90, equally likely. All benefits and costs use the same cardinal scale; the objective is expected net benefit from now. Inspecting gives **½ × 70 + ½ × 90 − 8 = 72**, compared with **70** for stopping. Inspection is better in expectation, though a low draw leaves **70 − 8 = 62**, worse than stopping on that realised path. The workshop will derive this model's threshold and change its recall assumption.

Simon also described **adaptive aspirations**: experience can change what a person considers satisfactory. That is an account of a search procedure, not a theorem that every adjustment improves expected value. [Simon's original model](https://cooperative-individualism.org/simon-herbert_a-behavioral-model-of-rational-choice-1955-feb.pdf)

<Callout variant="info" title="Review the standard without erasing the constraints">
A deadline may justify revising a soft preference or taking a fallback. It does not make a mandatory access requirement optional. Specify how aspirations may change and what happens if no feasible option qualifies; do not assume the right threshold must always descend.
</Callout>

## Setting the bar: the two failure modes

Two common risks come from a poorly chosen aspiration. Whether a threshold is too low or high depends on the objective and environment, not on its number alone.

- **Too low for the task:** an early option may meet the standard while offering little benefit compared with the cost of another inspection.
- **Too high for the task:** no inspected option may qualify before the deadline, leaving the chooser to use the stated fallback. Without a limit or fallback, the protocol is incomplete.

The lab illustrates these trade-offs in a particular model: independent simulated quality draws from one to one hundred, a fixed field size and a cost per inspection subtracted on that same point scale. The threshold procedure takes the first qualifying option; if none qualifies, it must take the last. The full-search comparator pays to inspect everything and then selects the best. Its ability to choose retrospectively is a model condition, not a right that every real search has.

The plotted averages come from seeded simulation. The threshold with the highest simulated mean net value is the best **simulated** threshold among those tested, not a universal optimal reservation value. The “quality gap” is best-field quality minus accepted quality; it does not measure psychological regret.

## Evaluate the procedure under its constraints

Limited resources can make a feasible satisficing procedure useful. They do **not** prove that every such procedure is optimal subject to those limits. A low standard remains a satisficing rule even if it performs badly; calling it good enough is not evidence that it serves the objective.

To evaluate a rule, state the requirements, order, inspection cost, deadline, recall rights and fallback. Then distinguish what a replay establishes from what a probability model or prospective evaluation would establish. The [search-audit workshop](/bounded-rationality-and-satisficing/06-audit-a-search-protocol) works through those comparisons without giving the chooser information disclosed only later.
