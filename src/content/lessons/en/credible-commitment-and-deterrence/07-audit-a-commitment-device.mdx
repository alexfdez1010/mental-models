A deposit can be returned in full and still be too expensive to arrange. A threat can be credible and still invite entry. And a contract can deter entry while leaving its buyer worse off than doing nothing.

Auditing a **commitment device** requires three different calculations. This workshop gives each one a decision point, a ledger and an outside option.

## Write down the game before judging the device

Our constructed game has an incumbent **I** and an entrant **E**. Each maximizes its own stated payoff. All payoffs are **changes measured in fictional credits**, abbreviated **cr**; utility is linear in these credits. For each player, an extra credit has the same value across these outcomes. The numbers are assumptions of this exercise, not measurements of a market.

Without a device, E first chooses **Stay** outside or **Enter**. After entry, I chooses **Fight** or **Accommodate**. The base payoffs are:

<tr><td>Enter, Fight</td><td>−1</td><td>−2</td></tr>
<tr><td>Enter, Accommodate</td><td>2</td><td>2</td></tr></tbody>
</table>
</div>

Pairs later in the lesson always read **(I, E)**. Work backwards. Once entry has happened, I prefers Accommodate, because 2 exceeds −1. E anticipates a payoff of 2 from entry rather than 0 from staying out. Thus NoDevice leads to Enter, Accommodate: **I receives 2 cr**.

Now add an initial decision for I: buy **one specified contract**, or select **NoDevice**. E observes that choice before moving. Everyone knows the rules and payoffs; enforcement is certain, and there is no renegotiation. These assumptions let us solve a small game. They do not establish that an actual rival shares our model or that an actual contract will be enforced.

A **subgame-perfect equilibrium** specifies optimal play in every continuation game, including branches that the initial choices never reach. The response after entry matters even if the prediction is that entry will not happen. We use backward induction to check those continuations, then return to the initial choice.

### When to use this distinction

Ask these three questions when somebody calls a device “credible”. The word can answer the response question while leaving deterrence and purchase value unresolved. An unavailable or unaffordable device also cannot become an initial option just because its continuation looks attractive.

## Separate the returned deposit from the fee

The offered contract has a deposit **B** and a fee **c**. An independent, nonstrategic enforcer collects c once at posting and holds B. It returns B if E stays out or if I fights after entry. If I accommodates, it forfeits B to an outside party. E receives none of the deposit.

**Accommodate remains legal.** A finite penalty changes its payoff; it does not delete the action. There is no interest, borrowing, renegotiation or extra contractual charge in this model.

For the main contract, **B=4 and c=1**. Posting requires 5 cr upfront, but that does not make every terminal payoff five credits lower. On Stay, the deposit returns and I receives **5−1=4 cr**. On Fight, the deposit also returns: **−1−1=−2 cr**. On Accommodate, it does not return: **2−4−1=−3 cr**.

After the fee has been paid, it is common to both replies. It is then a **sunk cost**: changing the reply cannot recover it. The deposit is different. Its future refund depends on the reply, so forfeiting it is still an avoidable consequence at that node. Calling both amounts “already committed” would erase the distinction that drives the game.

### When the ledger is useful

Trace each payment to a terminal branch before comparing it with a payoff. A deposit that is merely held is not a fee that has been consumed. Conversely, a later refund does not mean the deposit required no funding upfront.

## Solve the main contract from the end

With B=4 and c=1, the continuation has three steps:

1. **I after Enter:** Fight gives −2; Accommodate gives −3. Fight is the strictly better reply, even though both payoffs are negative.
2. **E after observing the posted contract:** Enter leads to Fight and −2 for E; Stay gives 0. E stays out.
3. **I before purchasing:** the contract gives 4; NoDevice gives 2. If this contract can be funded, I buys it in this specified menu.

The fee has already been charged in every contract branch below. The figure lists legal choices and their payoffs. It does not remove Accommodate or choose a best reply for you.

**Credibility** here means that carrying out the response is optimal when its decision point arrives. **Deterrence** means that the anticipated response makes the target prefer not to enter. **Ex ante value** means value *before* the initial choice and payment, compared with a feasible alternative. The main case passes all three tests; no single test implies the others.

### Why a negative response can still be credible

The relevant comparison is −2 versus −3, not −2 versus an imagined option worth zero. Stay was E’s earlier choice; I cannot choose it after entry. Adding a new option for I would change the game and require another solution.

## Change the fee without changing the response

Hold the deposit at four credits and raise the fee to four. Fight now gives −5 and Accommodate −6. The ranking remains the same: the common fee cancels in that comparison. E still anticipates Fight and stays out. Yet I would get only **5−4=1 cr** from buying, below the **2 cr** from NoDevice.

Each row below offers its own binary choice: **that row’s contract or NoDevice**. It is not a simultaneous catalogue from which I can pick any deposit and fee.

For these base payoffs, subtracting the reply values gives:

$$(-1-c)-(2-B-c)=B-3.$$

Fight is strictly better when B exceeds 3; Accommodate is strictly better below 3; both are optimal at 3. The fee affects the level of both continuation payoffs, and it matters again at the initial decision.

At **B=4, c=3**, the responses are strict, but the **initial** choice is tied: the contract and NoDevice each give I 2. This is a tie between buying and declining, not a tie between Fight and Accommodate. The entrant gets different payoffs along those two selected paths; equality for I is not equality for everyone.

### When to compare the initial options

A device already installed and a device still available for purchase are different decision problems. Do not recommend paying its setup fee using only a calculation that begins after the fee has been paid.

## Retain the response tie instead of inventing a unique path

Now fix **B=3, c=1**. After entry, Fight and Accommodate both give I **−2 cr**. Either pure reply, or a mixture of them, is optimal at that node.

Let **r** be the probability that I chooses Fight **conditional on Enter and this posted contract**. This is a strategy mixture we stipulate for analysis, not confidence that the payoff table is true. The entrant’s conditional entry payoff is:

$$r(-2)+(1-r)2=2-4r.$$

Stay pays 0. Thus E enters if r is below one half, stays out if r is above one half, and is indifferent at exactly one half. At that tie, E may enter with any probability **s** between zero and one.

Where does **4−6s** come from? Under the fixed contract, I gets 4 on Stay and −2 after Enter, whichever tied reply it uses. Hence its value before entry is:

$$(1-s)4+s(-2)=4-6s.$$

This describes the continuation **after the contract is fixed**. It does not yet say I voluntarily buys it. At the initial node, compare 4−6s with NoDevice’s 2: buy if s is below one third, decline if s is above one third, and either initial choice is optimal at s=1/3.

For the complete optional-installation game, the cases combine as follows. If r is below one half, E would enter with s=1, so I declines the contract. If r is above one half, E would stay out with s=0, so I buys it. At r=1/2, the initial decision follows the one-third threshold just derived; when s=1/3, any mixture of initial purchase and refusal is optimal for I. These conditions describe a family of equilibria under the stated rules, not a procedure that selects one of them.

The two **pure fixed-contract continuations** are Stay/Fight and Enter/Accommodate. Their full-game counterparts are, respectively, **buy/Stay** and **NoDevice/Enter/Accommodate**. Buying and then accommodating for −2 is not a voluntarily selected pure equilibrium of the complete game: declining would give I 2 instead. Off-path replies still have to be optimal.

### When a tie matters

Keep all optimal replies when the question asks what the game allows. A simulator can display one continuation using an explicit tie policy. That selected path does not prove uniqueness, and it cannot replace a comparison at an earlier decision point.

### What the change isolates

We changed a stated payoff, not a psychological label such as “irrational” or “fearless”. E maximizes its own payoff in both cases. Its outside-option comparison explains the changed response without claiming that a real entrant’s preferences can be read from a single action.

## Check whether the device can be posted

Give I a **free posting budget L**: credits available for the deposit and fee **after base-game obligations have separate reserved funds**. Those reserves cannot fund posting. Under this model’s budget rule there is no borrowing, so the offered contract is feasible only if **L is at least B+c**. L is not total wealth and does not redefine the base-game terminal payoffs.

For B=4, c=1, **L=4 is insufficient**; **L=5 is sufficient**. Once posted and followed by Stay, the net payoff remains 4 because the deposit returns. The funding requirement of 5 and the terminal net payoff of 4 answer different questions.

### When to reach for this audit

Use it when a promise has been called credible merely because it is expensive, or when a returned deposit has disappeared from the funding calculation. Draw the legal actions, calculate future replies, work back through the target’s outside option, and finally compare feasible initial choices. Keep the accounting and the order of moves visible.

### Source and model limits

Backward induction and optimal play in continuation games follow the concepts in David McAdams’s MIT lectures [4](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-040-game-theory-for-managers-spring-2004/f5f9770bf7533b650be71ef021154857_lec4.pdf) and [5](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-040-game-theory-for-managers-spring-2004/ea6b6f05311a1e508e5865dbac9b3043_lec5.pdf). Ben Polak’s [Yale lecture on sequential games](https://oyc.yale.edu/economics/econ-159/lecture-13) illustrates how collateral changes endpoint payoffs. Our players, credits, contract and numbers are constructed here; they are not source data or a recommendation for an actual negotiation. The results hold for the listed actions, information, enforcement, preferences and offered menu.
