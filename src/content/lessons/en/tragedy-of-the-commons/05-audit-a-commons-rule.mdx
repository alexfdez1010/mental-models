A community bans additional cows and collects fines. Its fund ends the period richer. Has the community gained? You need two checks before congratulating the treasurer: whether anyone changes their decision, and what happened to the users' balances.

This workshop builds a complete, deliberately small rule audit. The people, credits and probabilities below are **invented**. A credit is a common accounting unit for production and resource losses over one stated period. These are stipulated payoffs, not measurements of a real pasture.

## Set the boundary before adding the balances

Three users, A, B and C, already share a pasture. Existing cows and income stay fixed. For the **next period**, each chooses either no additional cow or exactly one. Write that choice as $x_i=0$ or $x_i=1$, where $i$ names a user. A **profile** lists all three choices in order A, B, C: for example, $100$ means only A adds. Zero does not mean closing the pasture.

| Input | Main case | Meaning within this period |
|---|---:|---|
| Users | 3 | Fixed A, B and C; no newcomers |
| Production benefit per addition | 6 credits | Goes to its owner, before the shared loss |
| Loss per addition to **each** user | 3 credits | Includes the owner; total loss is 9 |
| Available additions per user | 0 or 1 | No larger herd choice is available |

The loss represents a valued reduction in future forage, already expressed on this period's credit scale. We will not calculate grass growth, a collapse date or perpetual sustainability. Think of a shared account: entering one transaction can benefit its author while reducing several people's balances. The credit ledger describes that allocation; it does not describe the biology.

Each user values **only their own incremental credits**, linearly: twice the credits has twice the value. With uncertain fines, they compare expected credits, the probability-weighted average. This is the risk-neutral, self-regarding preference assumption; concern for others, fairness or reputation is outside it. The collective measure will add the three users and, when introduced, a passive fund. That accounting boundary does not make the fund's balance part of any user's personal objective.

### When to use this boundary

Use it to compare these one-period credit outcomes. A real decision would also need a defensible horizon, resource valuation, affected outsiders and distributional criteria. A positive sum would not by itself establish fairness, legality or ecological safety.

## Hold the other users fixed

<MCQ client:visible
  question={"If both other users add, can adding still be your best response even when your final balance is negative?"}
  options={[{"text":"Yes, if adding leaves you better off than not adding in that same situation.","correct":true},{"text":"No, because a negative balance makes every alternative equally unattractive."},{"text":"Only if the other users remove their additions when you decide."},{"text":"Only if the combined community balance also rises when you add."}]}
  explanation={"A best response compares your available actions while holding the others’ actions fixed. A final loss and a privately advantageous change can occur together."}
  pretest
/>
Let $n=x_A+x_B+x_C$ be the number of additions. Without a rule, user $i$ receives:

$$u_i=6x_i-3n.$$

For A, let $m=x_B+x_C$ count the **other** additions. No addition gives $-3m$. Adding gives $6-3(m+1)$. Subtract the first from the second:

$$[6-3(m+1)]-[-3m]=6-3=3.$$

| Other additions | A does not add | A adds | Change from adding |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | +3 | +3 |
| 1 | −3 | 0 | +3 |
| 2 | −6 | −3 | +3 |

All amounts are incremental credits. In the last row A chooses between losing 6 and losing 3, not between losing 3 and an unavailable zero. A **best response** is an action with the highest personal payoff given the other actions. Here adding is strictly better in every row. All three users face that same comparison.

The collective change from one addition is different: production adds 6, while three losses remove $3\times3=9$. Net: $6-9=-3$ credits. The part imposed on **other** users is $2\times3=6$, the external cost. The owner's own loss of 3 is already in their private comparison; counting it again as an external cost would double-count it.

A **pure equilibrium** is a profile where no user can improve their personal payoff by changing their own action alone. “Pure” means choosing an action, rather than randomizing. Here only $111$ qualifies: each gets $6-9=-3$, for a combined $-9$. At $000$ everyone gets zero, the highest combined value among the eight profiles, but any one user can gain 3 by adding. Collectively better and individually stable are different tests. This is a prediction **under the stated preferences**, not an observation about how all communities behave.

### When this comparison applies

Use the table when actions, benefits and losses really have the stated form. Bounded additions are essential: this finite model predicts a choice among eight profiles. It cannot derive unlimited herd growth from a choice set that permits only one extra cow each.

## Price the probability of a fine

<MCQ client:visible
  question={"A collected fine applies only when an addition is detected. Which quantity belongs in a risk-neutral user’s comparison?"}
  options={[{"text":"The fine weighted by the known detection probability.","correct":true},{"text":"The full fine regardless of whether detection occurs."},{"text":"The fund’s receipts divided equally among the users."},{"text":"The administrative bill added to every individual fine."}]}
  explanation={"An expected fine weights the possible payment by its probability. Whether users receive rebates or pay administration personally is a separate rule that must be specified."}
  pretest
/>
The community now prohibits additions. Each added cow has a known detection probability $q$, between zero and one. Detection leads to a **collected fine of 5 credits**, paid by the user into a passive fund. No addition means no fine. There are no false positives or collection failures; all balances can cover payments. The fund pays a real administration cost of **2 credits once per period**, including when nobody adds. It has sufficient initial resources.

No fine is returned to a user, and nobody anticipates a later personal benefit from the fund within this horizon. The actors still maximize only their own credits. Probability is an **exogenous input**: it does not change with the number of violations. We have not estimated it from a small sample.

At $q=1/2$, an addition incurs a payment of 5 with probability one half and zero otherwise. Its expected fine is:

$$\tfrac12\times5+\tfrac12\times0=2.5.$$

The realized payment is 5 or zero, never a compulsory half-fine of 2.5. Expected credits are the decision criterion under the linear preference assumption. We do not need detections to be independent across users to add their expected balances; each stated marginal detection probability suffices for this linear calculation.

The private change from adding is now:

$$\Delta u_i=6-3-5q=3-5q.$$

| Detection probability | Expected fine | Private change from adding | Model prediction |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 100% | 5 | −2 | Only no additions |
| 50% | 2.5 | +0.5 | Only all add |
| 60% | 3 | 0 | All eight profiles qualify |

Amounts are credits. Keep the selected hypothetical choices below at $111$ and move detection to 100%. Their accounts change, but the checkboxes do not: a scenario you inspect is not the model's behavior prediction.

### A fine is not automatically a Pigouvian price

The external loss here is 6 credits per addition. The stated fine is 5 **conditional on detection**, and its expectation is $5q$. It is not a tax set equal to the marginal external damage. It can nevertheless reverse this particular binary private comparison when large enough. That local result does not establish an efficient price for unrestricted real-world extraction.

## Follow credits through the fund

<MCQ client:visible
  question={"The fund has positive fine receipts. What additional information decides whether the rule improves the combined credit total?"}
  options={[{"text":"The actions induced and the real costs, including administration.","correct":true},{"text":"The size of the receipts, counting each as a new social benefit."},{"text":"The fine’s stated maximum, excluding its actual collection chance."},{"text":"The fund’s final balance, excluding the users who made payments."}]}
  explanation={"The fund is inside the boundary. Receipts offset user payments; changed production/resource losses and real administration remain."}
  pretest
/>
For any profile, expected user balances and the expected fund change are:

$$u_i=6x_i-3n-5qx_i,\qquad f=5qn-2.$$

Sum all three users. Their production is $6n$, resource losses are $9n$, and fine payments are $5qn$. Then add the fund:

$$\sum_i u_i+f=6n-9n-5qn+5qn-2=-3n-2.$$

The two fine terms cancel because the same credits leave users and enter the included fund. The administration bill consumes real resources and remains. This is an accounting identity at a **fixed profile**. Changing detection can still change the predicted profile and therefore the combined outcome.

At 50% detection, each strictly prefers adding, so $n=3$. Each user gets $6-9-2.5=-5.5$. The fund receives $3\times2.5=7.5$ and spends 2, leaving $+5.5$. Together: $3(-5.5)+5.5=-11$. A delighted treasurer could be looking at a worse collective result.

| Regime | Predicted profile | Each user | Fund | Combined |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| No rule, no administration | 111 | −3 | 0 | −9 |
| Rule, detection 0% | 111 | −3 | −2 | −11 |
| Rule, detection 50% | 111 | −5.5 | +5.5 | −11 |
| Rule, detection 100% | 000 | 0 | −2 | −2 |

At full detection nobody adds; no fines are collected. The regime still costs 2. Relative to the no-rule prediction, the combined value improves by $-2-(-9)=7$ credits. At zero or half detection this rule instead makes the combined value 2 credits worse. **Zero detection does not switch the administration cost off.** These comparisons use the same people, horizon and metric.

### When the cancellation is useful

Use it to check a ledger for double-counting, not to conclude that fines never matter. A fund outside the chosen boundary, a costly collection process, a personal rebate or different preferences requires a different audit. The distribution of balances is also visible: equal totals need not mean equally acceptable outcomes.

## Keep the tie exact

<MCQ client:visible
  question={"At the exact point where adding and not adding have equal expected personal value, what can this model predict?"}
  options={[{"text":"Every binary profile can be stable; an extra behavioral assumption is needed.","correct":true},{"text":"No additions must occur; indifferent users always follow the written rule."},{"text":"All users must add; indifferent users always take the available benefit."},{"text":"Only a mixed profile is stable; identical incentives require different choices."}]}
  explanation={"With zero unilateral gain, neither action strictly improves personal value. Every user is best-responding at every binary profile in this additive model."}
  pretest
/>
Strict deterrence means that **not adding is strictly better**:

$$3-5q<0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q>\tfrac35.$$

At exactly $q=3/5=60\%$, the private difference is zero for every configuration of the other users. All eight pure profiles are equilibria. Their combined values range from −2 at $000$ to −11 at $111$. Equal private alternatives do not make all collective outcomes equal, and they do not tell us which one people will choose.

| Detection | Expected fine | Private change | Sign-based prediction |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 59% | 2.95 | +0.05 | All add |
| 60% | 3 | 0 | No unique profile |
| 61% | 3.05 | −0.05 | None add |

Credits are shown to the cent here. Move the rule control through these three percentages. Its sign test uses exact hundredths for the stated integer inputs, not a tolerance that erases a small incentive. A tiny modeled difference is still sensitive to real uncertainty about the inputs; numerical precision does not make the assumptions certain.

### When to use the threshold

Use it to test sensitivity under this fixed fine and preference model. It does not tell you the cheapest monitoring technology, whether enforcement is legitimate, or how actual users respond near indifference. Those questions need additional costs and evidence.

<details>
<summary>Show the complete transfer solution</summary>

The private difference is $4-2-3q=2-3q$. At half detection the expected fine is $3/2$, leaving a gain of $1/2$. All add. Each gets $4-6-1.5=-3.5$; the fund gets $4.5-1=3.5$; combined $3(-3.5)+3.5=-7$.

At three-quarters detection the expected fine is $9/4$, leaving $2-9/4=-1/4$. None add, all user balances are zero and the fund loses 1 to administration. Combined: −1. The no-rule comparison for these **new** coefficients is all-add with combined $(4-6)\times3=-6$, so the two rules change that total by −1 and +5 respectively.

The exact tie solves $2-3q=0$: $q=2/3$, not $0.67$. At 66% the difference is $2-1.98=+0.02$; at 67% it is $2-2.01=-0.01$. The integer-percentage control cannot select two thirds exactly. Its neighboring settings remain strictly on opposite sides of the tie.

</details>

## Decide what would change the audit

<MCQ client:visible
  question={"Suppose the fund promises to return fine receipts to the users. What must you do before reusing the private threshold?"}
  options={[{"text":"Specify the rebate rule and include any action-dependent personal return.","correct":true},{"text":"Keep the threshold because transfers cancel in the combined account."},{"text":"Replace the collected fine with the whole fund’s administrative expenditure."},{"text":"Ignore the promise because only immediate physical losses affect utility."}]}
  explanation={"Collective cancellation does not imply unchanged private incentives. A personal return can depend on your action, others’ actions or both."}
  pretest
/>
The rule passes different tests that must remain separate:

1. **Accounting:** do payments, receipts and real costs sum correctly at each profile?
2. **Incentives:** given the stated personal objective, is changing one's action attractive?
3. **Behavior and outcomes:** do actual users face those choices and respond as the model predicts, and are the valued resource effects defensible?

A practical audit records the permitted additions, who can act, the accounting period, benefit and loss estimates, who pays or receives, the collection protocol, and administration resources. Detection records must distinguish **violations detected** from **violations that actually occurred**: the former alone do not supply the detection probability. A controlled inspection exercise with known violations could inform that input; three observed fines cannot establish it by themselves.

False positives, inability to pay, detection that falls as violations crowd the monitor, rebates, concern for neighbors and later interactions change the model. For example, if administration rises from 2 to 12 while full detection still induces no additions, the combined value becomes −12, below the no-rule −9. Compliance alone would no longer justify this regime under our chosen total-credit measure. That is a new cost assumption, not a contradiction in the ledger.

Ostrom's [Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/ostrom_lecture.pdf), especially sections 2B, 3, 4C–E and 8, distinguishes resource attributes from governing rights and argues for institutions suited to their setting. It motivates checking the actual rules. **The coefficients and thresholds in this workshop are our constructed example**, not her empirical estimates. A finite credit comparison cannot certify a real governance arrangement.
