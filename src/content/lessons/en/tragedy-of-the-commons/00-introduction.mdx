A shared resource can be damaged by choices that pay each user privately. The person taking one more unit keeps its benefit while other users bear part of its cost. Even people who understand the damage can face a reason to take more.

That is the incentive problem behind the **tragedy of the commons**. It is a model to investigate, not a forecast that everything shared must collapse. A **common-pool resource** has subtractable use: what one person takes reduces what others can use. Excluding users is difficult or costly. Those resource attributes do not mean access must remain open to everyone; a community can hold rights and enforce rules over a common-pool resource.

## The central comparison

<Callout variant="tip" title="Who gains and who pays?">
Compare the private change from one more unit with its total effect on the people affected. A gap can reward taking that makes the group worse off. Then ask whether the actual rules and preferences create that gap.
</Callout>

Changing rights, limits or payments can change behavior. Whether a particular change helps depends on the actions available, enforcement, real costs and what people value. A written ban alone does not guarantee restraint.

## Why structure matters

Blaming greed does not identify which action pays or which rule could change it. The incentives course taught you to inspect the reward attached to an action. Here some consequences fall on other people: an **externality** is a cost or benefit imposed on someone outside the decision that generates it. A user can bear part of a resource loss and still impose a further part on others.

A model must state the preferences as well as the payments. Maximizing personal credits, caring about neighbors and protecting a reputation are different objectives. A prediction made under one objective need not hold under another.
