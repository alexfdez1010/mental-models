A commons rule can assign access, limit withdrawals, require information or change payments. Those are different interventions, with different implementation costs. This lesson compares the toolkit; the following workshop will audit one rule numerically.

A common-pool resource need not be open to everyone or lack owners. Resource attributes and the rights governing access are separate questions. Shared use can be governed well or badly; neither automatic ruin nor automatic cooperation follows from the label.

The practical job is to identify what a tool changes and where it can fail. A cap on paper, a valuable ownership share and a collected fine are not interchangeable mechanisms.

## What institutional tools change

The common task is to change the conditions under which taking occurs: permitted actions, information, payoffs or the capacity to coordinate.

When the private gain from a marginal withdrawal exceeds its private cost while its total effect is negative, the user and the collective comparison disagree. Possible interventions include:

- **Re-internalize the externality** — make more of the external damage enter the private comparison; rights or charges can do this when they cover the relevant effects.
- **Limit extraction** — specify quantities or timing, then check that the limit is implemented and appropriate for the resource.
- **Support coordination and restraint** — change consequences, information or agreements so that users may find restraint worthwhile; check the resulting personal comparison.

The previous fishery model illustrates a stipulated extraction cap. It does not itself model beliefs, fines or users choosing best responses. Its toggle is not evidence that every managed resource is sustainable.

</Callout>

## Institutional alternatives

Hardin’s [1968 article](https://faculty.lsu.edu/kharms/files/hardin_1968.pdf) discusses private property, regulated access and charges. Those alternatives change institutions; they do not establish one universally effective ownership form.

## Property rights — give the resource an owner

**Analogy.** If you expect to keep a car, a repair that preserves its future value can benefit you. The incentive depends on capturing that future value; owning a title alone does not guarantee maintenance.

**Definition.** Property rights specify who may access, withdraw, manage, exclude or transfer rights. They can be held by individuals, firms or groups. Secure future rights may make future resource value matter privately; they need not internalize every spillover or settle the extraction limit.

**Application.** An individual transferable quota (ITQ), or catch share, specifies an entitlement to a share of authorized catch. It can give its holder a stake in future authorized harvests. The total cap, credibility of the rights and effects on other users still matter; a share is not ownership of every fish in the ecosystem.

**Where it can fail.** Rights may not cover the effects crossing a boundary, may be hard to enforce or may allocate benefits unfairly. Dividing surface land does not necessarily separate groundwater effects. A right focused on one use can also omit values held by other users. Specify what the entitlement covers before assuming an externality is internalized.

## Regulation and quotas — a central cap on the take

**Analogy.** Imagine a park whose ranger service sets the rules — stay on the path, no fires in August, permits for the backcountry — and enforces them for everyone.

**Definition.** Regulation sets permitted actions, quantities or timing and specifies implementation. A quota can limit withdrawals; whether it is observed and ecologically appropriate depends on compliance, measurement and resource dynamics.

**Examples of instruments.** Fishing quotas and closed seasons, hunting licenses with bag limits, emissions caps and water-withdrawal rights can specify permitted use. The permitted amount still needs a resource model, and a written limit does not establish actual compliance or regeneration.

**Where it can fail.** Monitoring and enforcement can be costly, and users may have incentives to conceal withdrawals or avoid checks. A quota on paper does not establish compliance: some users may follow it voluntarily, while others may not. Check the actual choices, monitoring and consequences rather than infer evasion from a stock decline alone. Regulatory capture and poor local fit are further possibilities to investigate. A rule designed around average conditions can fit some places poorly; it does not follow that it fits nobody.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The enforcement tax">
Specify which withdrawals need to be observed, by whom, with what accuracy and at what cost. The difficulty can vary across uses and places. A cap and a monitoring budget alone do not establish compliance; missed withdrawals and false accusations can matter too.
</Callout>

## Pigouvian taxes — make the price tell the truth

**Analogy.** Imagine a noisy workshop that benefits its owner while reducing the neighbors’ ability to work. A charge changes the owner’s private calculation. Whether its amount matches the marginal loss imposed on others requires another calculation.

**Definition.** A Pigouvian charge is calibrated to marginal external damage at the relevant efficient allocation under the benchmark model. It adds a missing external cost to the private comparison. A charge can change behavior without being exactly calibrated; efficiency also depends on the model’s other assumptions.

**Application.** Carbon charges and congestion charges are pricing instruments. Their names alone do not establish that their rates equal the relevant external damage. A refundable deposit changes the incentive to return an item, while a permit system limits authorized quantity. Auditing each instrument requires its actual payment and enforcement rules.

**Where it can fail.** Calibrating a charge requires evidence about the relevant marginal harm and how actions respond. Its name does not supply those inputs. Distribution also matters: record who pays, who receives the revenue, whether rebates are promised and what collection consumes. Those details can change private incentives and who benefits, even when payments cancel inside a chosen collective boundary.

## Social norms and user governance

**Analogy.** Residents can agree quiet hours and choose someone to hear complaints. These working rules can coexist with responsibilities handled by larger organizations. The example illustrates user participation, rather than proving that every neighbor will cooperate.

**Definition.** In self-governance, resource users participate in making, monitoring or enforcing rules. This can coexist with public recognition and institutions at other levels; it does not mean the absence of property rights or of a state.

**Evidence and scope.** Ostrom’s [Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/ostrom_lecture.pdf), sections 4C–E and 8, separates resource attributes from rights and discusses institutions operating at several scales. Its findings motivate checking the specific arrangement rather than assuming a resource must be ungoverned.

The lecture summarizes institutional features associated with long-surviving arrangements. They guide investigation rather than guarantee success:

1. **Clearly defined boundaries** — everyone knows who has rights and what the resource is.
2. **Rules matched to local conditions** — the rule fits *this* pasture, *this* river, not a national average.
3. **Collective choice** — the people who live under the rules help *make* the rules.
4. **Monitoring** — usage is watched, often by the users themselves or accountable monitors.
5. **Graduated sanctions** — sanctions start low and become stronger for repeated violations.
6. **Cheap conflict resolution** — fast, low-cost ways to settle disputes locally.
7. **Recognized right to organize** — outside authorities recognize users' right to devise their own rules.
8. **Nested enterprises** — for large systems, layers of local rules sit inside regional and broader ones.

Monitoring and sanctions can change the consequences of violations. Communication, trust and repeated interaction can also matter. Their presence does not automatically turn a dilemma into cooperation: costs, information and preferences determine what changes. The next workshop derives a one-period response without assuming repetition.

**Where it can fail.** Rules may fit poorly, lack legitimacy, be costly to enforce or leave important spillovers outside the group. Larger systems can require coordination between levels; local participation and broader institutions can complement each other. Large scale alone does not establish that user governance is impossible.

## Choosing the tool — there is no panacea

So which fix do you use? Ostrom's deepest lesson is the most useful and the least satisfying: **there is no panacea.** The right answer depends on the resource. So you *read the commons* first — its **scale**, how **excludable** it is (can you keep non-payers out?), how **measurable** the take is, and the **group size** — and then you match the remedy. A **blend** is also a candidate to evaluate.

| Tool | Potential fit | Possible failure | Example instrument |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Property rights / ITQs** | Entitlements can be specified and enforced; future resource value matters to holders | Rights omit shared effects, enforcement is weak or allocation is unfair | Withdrawal and management rights; catch shares |
| **Regulation / quotas** | A central body can monitor and enforce; conditions are uniform enough for one rule | Monitoring is costly or gameable; regulators are captured or wrong; conditions vary locally | Fishing seasons, hunting licenses, emissions caps |
| **Pigouvian charge / pricing** | Relevant marginal harm and behavior can be assessed; the charge can be collected | Calibration, implementation or distribution is poor | Charges on emissions or congested trips |
| **User governance** | Users can help design, monitor and revise credible rules | Coordination, legitimacy or coverage of spillovers is inadequate | Local rules nested within broader institutions |

Universal ownership prescriptions skip the conditions that make a tool work. Compare the rights that could be enforced, the costs of implementation and whether the arrangement covers the relevant spillovers. A proposed coastal-fishery arrangement might pair catch shares with a closed spawning season and user monitoring. That is a candidate combination to evaluate, not evidence that its parts necessarily reinforce each other.

## Recap

1. **Institutional tools change actions, information, payoffs or coordination.** An extraction cap, a price and a user agreement need different implementation checks.
2. **Property rights** define entitlements that can attach future value to restraint. Check which effects they cover, how credible they are and who receives them.
3. **Regulation and quotas** specify permitted extraction. Check the limit, implementation, resource dynamics and actual compliance; monitoring costs and opportunities for evasion can matter.
4. **Pricing instruments** add private consequences. Calling a charge Pigouvian does not prove exact calibration; check marginal external damage, implementation and the other assumptions.
5. **User governance** can operate alongside public recognition and nested institutions. Monitoring, sanctions and repeated interaction are mechanisms to inspect, not guarantees of cooperation. Diagnose the case and compare actual consequences, including costs.
