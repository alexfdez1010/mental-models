A community event needs three deliveries before setup starts. Each delivery has a one-in-four chance of arriving late. A coordinator adds the risks and gets an expected three-quarters of a late delivery. Can one spare handling slot cover the problem?

The average does not answer that question. Delays might be scattered across batches or arrive together when a shared depot is disrupted. This workshop keeps the individual delay probabilities fixed while changing that joint pattern. The existing lessons introduced expected value and spread; here you will build the distribution needed for a particular operating decision.

## Define the count before interpreting the average

This is a **hypothetical, bounded planning case**, not delivery-performance data. A **batch** is the three named deliveries A, B and C due before one fixed setup deadline. A delivery is either late or on time relative to that deadline, with no partial lateness counted. “Together” refers to the same deadline window; exact arrival times are not modelled. Let **N** be the number late in the batch: zero, one, two or three.

The **marginal probability** concerns one delivery considered on its own. Each has a late probability of **1/4 = 25%**. A **joint distribution** describes how the deliveries' outcomes occur together. **Independence** means learning the other delivery outcomes does not change the specified probability; a shared disruption can violate that condition. Events are not mutually exclusive merely because they concern different deliveries: several can be late in the same batch.

Score each delivery **1 if late and 0 otherwise**. Its expected contribution is **(1/4 × 1) + (3/4 × 0) = 1/4**. Adding the three contributions gives:

**Expected late count = 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 3/4 delivery per batch**

This addition **does not require independence**. Linearity of expectation, explained in [Stanford's probability reader](https://chrispiech.github.io/probabilityForComputerScientists/en/part2/expectation/), applies to dependent counts too. Our count is bounded, so all these expectations are finite.

The average **0.75** is not a promise that at most one delivery will be late, and it is not a **75% chance of any delay**. A probability of an event lies between zero and one; a count expectation has count units and could exceed one in a larger problem. Their numerical values are not interchangeable.

## Build two complete probability catalogues

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="Two plans have the same expected late count. Must their chances of a simultaneous overload also be the same?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; the same mean can come from different weights on the possible group counts.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; a shared mean identifies every probability in the count distribution.' },
    { text: 'Yes; any shared disruption must leave all group-event probabilities unchanged.' },
    { text: 'No; different delivery names alone establish independent outcomes.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The mean is one weighted summary of the distribution. It does not specify how often the total is zero, one, two or three. Names do not establish independence."
/>

We compare two deliberately simple models for the **same one-batch horizon**. Each retains the 25% individual delay probability. Other service features and costs are held fixed for the comparison.

**Model I: independent local delays.** Give each delivery four equally likely local condition labels: one produces lateness and three produce on-time arrival. The labels for the three deliveries are selected independently. There are **4 × 4 × 4 = 64 equally likely combinations**. This finite construction makes the independence assumption explicit. The corresponding late count is binomial; [NIST's glossary](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/glossary.htm) identifies independent trials as a binomial-model assumption.

Count combinations by the number late:

| Late count N | Counting the combinations in Model I | Catalogue entries |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Three on-time labels for each delivery: 3 × 3 × 3 | 27 |
| 1 | Choose the late delivery, then choose an on-time label for each of the other two: 3 × 1 × 3 × 3 | 27 |
| 2 | Choose the on-time delivery and one of its three on-time labels: 3 × 3 | 9 |
| 3 | The one late label for all three: 1 × 1 × 1 | 1 |
| Total | 27 + 27 + 9 + 1 | 64 |

**Model S: one shared disruption.** A depot disruption makes **all three** deliveries late with probability **1/4**. Without it, all three arrive on time; there are no additional local delays in this model. Represent that distribution on a catalogue of 64 equally likely condition labels: **16** select disruption and **48** select no disruption. Different labels can produce the same delivery outcome.

| Late count N | Model I probability | Model S probability |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 27/64 | 48/64 |
| 1 | 27/64 | 0 |
| 2 | 9/64 | 0 |
| 3 | 1/64 | 16/64 |

These are **probability catalogues**, not realized samples. They do not promise that the next 64 actual batches will produce those frequencies, and the number 64 does not mean a batch contains 64 deliveries. Every batch still contains three.

Check the common mean from the complete distributions:

- Model I: **(0 × 27 + 1 × 27 + 2 × 9 + 3 × 1) ÷ 64 = 48/64 = 0.75**.
- Model S: **(0 × 48 + 3 × 16) ÷ 64 = 48/64 = 0.75**.

Both also have the same delivery-level rate. Across the weighted catalogue there are **48 late items out of 64 × 3 = 192 delivery positions**, or **25%**. The arrangement of those late items across batches differs.

### Ask which group event matters

**Any delay** means N is at least one. In Model I it has probability **(27 + 9 + 1)/64 = 37/64 ≈ 57.81%**. In Model S it has probability **16/64 = 25%**.

**Two or more delays** means N is two or three. Its probability is **(9 + 1)/64 = 10/64 = 15.625%** in Model I and **16/64 = 25%** in Model S. For **all three** late, the probabilities are **1/64 = 1.5625%** and **16/64 = 25%**.

The shared model has **fewer affected batches but more late items in each affected batch**. That is a statement about these models, not a claim that dependence increases every tail probability or that independent routes are always preferable. Other joint distributions can share the same marginal probabilities.

## State the operational objective before ranking the plans

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="One coordinator wants batches with no disruption; another wants to avoid simultaneous overload. Must they prefer the same model?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; define each event or cost function before comparing its model probabilities.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; the same mean makes every operational objective equivalent.' },
    { text: 'Yes; lower probability of any delay always means lower overload probability.' },
    { text: 'No; use whichever isolated outcome looks most reassuring and ignore its weight.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The two objectives concern different features of the distribution. A ranking needs the stated outcome, constraint or cost, rather than a universal preference for a dependence pattern."

For **Objective A**, require at least **60% of batches to have every delivery on time**. Model I gives **27/64 ≈ 42.19%**, so it does not meet that modelled target. Model S gives **48/64 = 75%**, so it does.

For **Objective B**, there is one handling slot for a late item. A slot covers one late-delivery exception in this batch, rather than specifying a concurrent unloading bay. Require at least **80% of batches to stay within that one-slot capacity**, meaning N is zero or one. Model I gives **(27 + 27)/64 = 54/64 = 84.375%**, so it meets that target. Model S gives **48/64 = 75%**, so it does not.

| Objective, under the stipulated model | Model I | Model S |
|---|---|---|
| Every delivery on time in at least 60% of batches | Does not meet target | Meets target |
| No more than one late item in at least 80% of batches | Meets target | Does not meet target |

If both constraints are required, neither plan meets both in this comparison. A choice between them does not solve that problem. A mitigation, more capacity or a changed delivery process would require new assumptions, costs and evidence.

### Compute a cost using the outcome it actually charges

Suppose handling uses **2 staff-minutes per late item**, with no batch setup cost. Both models then require an expected **2 × 0.75 = 1.5 staff-minutes per batch**. The linear cost depends only on the expected count.

Now change the policy: opening an incident record takes **8 staff-minutes once per affected batch**, plus the same **2 per late item**. This is a different, explicitly defined cost function:

**Work for a batch = 8 × indicator of any delay + 2 × N**

The indicator is one if any delivery is late and zero otherwise. It is charged once, even if all three are late. Thus:

| Model | Expected incident-record work | Expected per-item work | Expected total work per batch |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 8 × 37/64 = 4.625 | 2 × 0.75 = 1.5 | 6.125 staff-minutes |
| S | 8 × 16/64 = 2 | 2 × 0.75 = 1.5 | 3.5 staff-minutes |

This policy favours Model S on expected work, whereas the one-slot service constraint favoured Model I. The conclusion changes because the objective changes. The example does not establish which delivery plan would be best once actual fees, delay lengths, accessibility or other requirements are included.

Probability of overload and expected overload **size** are different again. With one slot, two late items leave one beyond capacity; three leave two. Expected overflow is **(9 × 1 + 1 × 2)/64 = 11/64 ≈ 0.172 items per batch** in Model I, and **16 × 2/64 = 0.5** in Model S. The overload probability was **10/64** versus **16/64**. Use the event probability for an overload-frequency target and the expected overflow count for a linear per-overflow-item workload; do not substitute one for the other.

## Keep the joint pattern in the evidence

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="A dashboard reports only the overall fraction of late deliveries. What extra record would help distinguish the two models?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Batch identifiers and the number late within each common deadline window.', correct: true },
    { text: 'A larger pooled item count with every batch relationship removed.' },
    { text: 'Only the average late count, rounded until it becomes a whole delivery.' },
    { text: 'Only whether the last batch happened to have no late deliveries.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Both models have the same pooled delivery-level late rate. Group identifiers retain the clustering that changes the chances of simultaneous incidents. One observed batch does not identify the full joint distribution."
/>

For real planning, collect records that preserve the **same batch definition and deadline**, the individual delivery outcomes and relevant shared resources or disruptions. Compare the observed distribution of zero, one, two and three late items, not just the pooled late percentage. Records of shared depot use, common dispatch times and interruption timing can help investigate a mechanism. Co-occurrence alone does not prove which shared cause produced it.

The two catalogues are deliberately simplified alternatives. Real delays could combine a shared disruption with local problems, have unequal individual probabilities or change with the season. The result is then a different joint distribution. Renaming routes does not establish independence; neither does getting the same average from two dashboards.

A range of **zero to three** is also insufficient: both examples have those minimum and maximum counts, but assign different mass within them. A range does not supply the probability of each count, and it does not promise that every interior value has positive probability. In Model S, one or two late deliveries never occur.

### Separate dependence within a batch from dependence across batches

Everything calculated here is for a one-batch distribution. Adding its expected counts needs no claim that successive batches are independent. However, a disruption lasting several sessions can also link outcomes across batches. Estimating frequencies and uncertainty from real records must respect that clustering and whether the process stayed comparable over time. A catalogue is a model, not a guarantee that a long sequence of observations will reproduce its weights.

### Transfer to a staffing decision

Two helpers each have a **50%** chance of being unavailable for one event window. In Model A their statuses are independent, giving four equally likely cases: both available, only the first available, only the second available, neither available. In Model B they attend the same outside meeting: both are available with probability one half and neither is available with probability one half. Both models have an expected **one unavailable helper**.
