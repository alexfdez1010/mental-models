An archive detector flags a folder. Does it carry the hidden mark the detector is looking for? “The detector is accurate” sounds reassuring. “The mark is rare” sounds cautionary. Neither sentence tells you how many marked and unmarked folders end up in the alerted group.

We will build that group and count it. The same method will also show when the supplied information cannot answer the question.

## Start with a named population

A **base rate** is the frequency of a condition in a relevant starting population, before incorporating the new signal. The **reference class** is that population: the cases whose frequency you intend to use. “Rare” needs a denominator. Rare among every folder ever made is not automatically the same as rare in this archive.

Here is our constructed model. A hidden mark can be checked by opening a folder; the detector gives an alert without opening it. There are exactly **1,000 folders: 50 marked and 950 unmarked**. First imagine choosing uniformly from the whole archive, meaning every folder has the same chance of selection. The chance of a mark is:

$$P(\text{marked})=\frac{50}{1000}=5\%$$

Now choose uniformly **only from folders with an alert**. That is a different population. To calculate the marked share there, we need to know how many folders of each kind generated alerts.

These are complete, stipulated catalogues, not measured samples or forecasts of how a real detector will behave. Each later protocol describes an alternative catalogue. We never apply two protocols successively or assume their signals are independent.

Psychologists use **base-rate neglect** for judgments that give insufficient weight to relevant starting frequencies. Tversky and Kahneman discussed it in tasks involving personality descriptions and category frequencies. A vivid resemblance does not supply a conditional rate. Equally, knowing the starting frequency alone does not settle what an informative signal implies.

### When to use this starting point

Use a base rate when it describes how the current case was selected, or when there is a defensible reason to transfer it to the current case. Ask which cases were counted, what the condition means and whether the new case belongs to that population. A historical average needs those checks before becoming a forecast.

## Filter both possible origins of an alert

Protocol A gives the following complete table. A **false alarm** is an alert on an unmarked folder; an alert is therefore not the same as a confirmed mark.

| Actual state | Alert | No alert | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Marked | 20 | 30 | 50 |
| Unmarked | 95 | 855 | 950 |
| Total | 115 | 885 | 1,000 |

Work through the selection:

1. Keep the 20 marked folders with alerts.
2. Also keep the 95 unmarked folders with alerts. They satisfy the selection rule too.
3. There are 115 eligible folders, of which 20 are marked.

Thus the probability of a mark **given an alert**, meaning within the alerted group, is:

$$P(\text{marked}\mid\text{alert})=\frac{20}{20+95}=\frac{4}{23}\approx17.39\%$$

This is a **posterior probability**: the probability after incorporating the signal. It is above the initial 5%, while still below one half. The alert changed the estimate; it did not identify the state with certainty.

### Odds and the strength of the signal

**Odds** compare the number in a condition with the number outside it, rather than with the total. Initial marked-to-unmarked odds are 50:950, or 1:19. After filtering they are 20:95, or 4:19.

The **likelihood ratio** for an alert compares how often an alert appears in the two states:

$$\frac{P(\text{alert}\mid\text{marked})}{P(\text{alert}\mid\text{unmarked})}=\frac{20/50}{95/950}=\frac{0.4}{0.1}=4$$

An alert is four times as frequent among marked as among unmarked folders. This ratio multiplies the **odds**: 1:19 becomes 4:19. Convert back to a probability by dividing the first part by both parts together: 4/(4 + 19) = 4/23. Multiplying the initial probability 5% directly by four would give 20%, which is a different and incorrect answer.

The count table is enough to solve this case. Odds are another way to check the same result, not another piece of evidence.

## Stronger evidence can reverse the majority

Keep the initial 50 marked and 950 unmarked folders. Change only the detector’s stipulated behaviour. Protocol B alerts 80% of the marked folders and 2% of the unmarked folders:

| Actual state | Alert | No alert | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Marked | 40 | 10 | 50 |
| Unmarked | 19 | 931 | 950 |
| Total | 59 | 941 | 1,000 |

Among the 59 alerts, 40 are marked: **40/59 ≈ 67.80%**. The marked state is now more likely, despite still being rare in the starting population.

B’s likelihood ratio is (40/50)/(19/950) = 0.8/0.02 = 40. Initial odds 1:19 become 40:19; the probability is 40/(40 + 19). In this particular starting population, for protocols with a positive false-alarm rate, an alert makes marked folders the majority when its likelihood ratio exceeds 19. At exactly 19 the two states would tie. That threshold comes from the initial odds; it is not a universal threshold for every population.

### What the contrast rules out

“A rare condition must remain unlikely after any alert” fails in B. “A positive result tells you the detector’s hit rate” fails in A. The answer depends on the starting population **and** the signal’s conditional rates. There is no fixed rule that case-specific evidence may only make a small adjustment.

## Overall accuracy does not identify the alerted group

Someone reports “99% accurate.” Ask which quantity they mean. Here **overall accuracy** means correct classifications divided by all folders: marked folders with alerts plus unmarked folders without alerts. The **false-positive rate** instead divides unmarked alerts by all unmarked folders. Neither definition is the marked share among alerts.

Consider two more alternative catalogues with the same initial 50 marked and 950 unmarked folders:

| Protocol | Marked with alert | Marked without alert | Unmarked with alert | Unmarked without alert |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| C | 40 | 10 | 0 | 950 |
| D | 50 | 0 | 10 | 940 |

For C, overall accuracy is (40 + 950)/1,000 = 99%. For D, it is (50 + 940)/1,000 = 99% too. Yet their alerted groups differ:

- C: 40 marked alerts and no unmarked alerts, so 40/40 = **100% marked among alerts**.
- D: 50 marked alerts and 10 unmarked alerts, so 50/60 = 5/6 ≈ **83.33% marked among alerts**.

In the chart, change from “correct classifications” to “marked folders among alerts”. **The denominator changes from all folders to alerted folders.** Identical bars for the first question do not require identical bars for the second.

### What information to request

For a new population, seek an applicable initial marked share and both conditional alert rates: among marked folders and among unmarked folders. Alternatively, a complete applicable count table supplies the same information directly. Clarify selection and time period. An unexplained “accuracy” label leaves several different questions bundled together.

## Choose the reference class before calculating

Suppose protocol A’s archive consists of two known groups, R and S. The table partitions the **same A catalogue**, not a new detector:

| Group | Marked with alert | Marked without alert | Unmarked with alert | Unmarked without alert | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| R | 8 | 12 | 8 | 72 | 100 |
| S | 12 | 18 | 87 | 783 | 900 |
| Whole A archive | 20 | 30 | 95 | 855 | 1,000 |

If the sampling rule selects from all A alerts and tells you nothing else, use 20/115. If you **already know the folder belongs to R**, start within R. There are 20 marked folders among its 100 folders. Within R’s alerted group, eight are marked and eight unmarked, giving **8/16 = 50%**.

You can check that this change does not require the detector to behave differently inside R. Its conditional alert rates are 8/20 = 40% among marked folders and 8/80 = 10% among unmarked folders. In S they are 12/30 = 40% and 87/870 = 10%. The starting marked proportions differ. Return to the R scenario in the earlier chart to compare the correct pair of populations.

Membership in R has already been incorporated by using R’s table. Do not count it again as if it were a second independent signal. And these subgroup rates have been defined only for A; B’s whole-archive table does not tell us B’s rates within R.

### When historical cases can help

For a project deadline, a reference class might consist of projects with comparable scope, resources and a consistent definition of completion. Record how cases entered the class, including relevant failures rather than only convenient successes. A new method or changed resources can matter substantially; explain why the old outcomes would still inform the current project, or what evidence supports a different forecast.

There is no automatic “smallest class wins” rule. A narrower class may be more relevant but leave little data; a larger class may mix unlike processes. Historical samples also bring estimation uncertainty, unlike our stipulated complete catalogues. A five-week historical average does not by itself tell you every new job lasts five weeks, nor that every justified adjustment must be small.

## Transfer to a new catalogue

Put the previous detector’s rates aside. In a **new complete catalogue of 200 folders**, 20 are marked and 180 are unmarked. Its new protocol alerts 15 of the marked folders and nine of the unmarked folders. Selection is uniform among this new catalogue’s alerts.

The alerted group contains 15 + 9 = 24 folders. Its marked share is **15/24 = 5/8 = 62.5%**, compared with the initial 20/200 = 10%. The hit rate among marked folders is 15/20 = 75%; that is not the requested conditional probability. The false-positive rate is 9/180 = 5%.

As a separate check, the likelihood ratio is 0.75/0.05 = 15. Initial odds 1:9 become 15:9, or 5:3, which converts to 5/(5 + 3) = 5/8.

To transfer this answer, check both how the next folder is selected and whether the next population and detector have the same applicable rates. Different archive membership, a changed detector or selective recording of alerts could require a new table. Without that information, state the limitation rather than treating 62.5% as a property of every alerted folder everywhere.

</details>

### A usable review rule

Write down the condition, starting population, signal and filtered denominator. Recalculate when new information changes the relevant population or either conditional rate. If those inputs are absent, report what is missing. If they come from estimates rather than a complete stipulated table, report their uncertainty too.

## Sources and next step

[Tversky and Kahneman’s 1974 article](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124), in its discussion of representativeness and prior probabilities, provides the research context for base-rate neglect. [Ayal and Beyth-Marom’s 2014 study](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1930297500005775) examines how computational steps and compatibility between data and questions affect performance in particular Bayesian reasoning tasks. A table is a way to expose the relevant groups, not a guarantee of understanding or a diagnosis of anyone’s reasoning.

All archive counts here are constructed teaching data. Next, [Expected Value](/thinking-in-probabilities/03-expected-value) combines probabilities with outcomes: knowing how likely a result is does not yet say how much that result is worth for a decision.
