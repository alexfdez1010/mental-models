A training centre selects operators after unusually high task scores. They receive advanced practice, then their average falls. Did the practice hurt? A forecast allowing for regression could have predicted a fall even if the practice helped.

To decide, separate three questions: what the model predicts on average, which paths an individual can take, and what comparison would estimate the practice effect. A line through expected values is a forecast, not a rail that every result must follow.

## An expected result is not a required next result

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A model forecasts a less extreme retest after a high score. Which individual outcome would contradict that forecast by itself?"
  options={[
    { text: "A retest above the first score, because no individual may rise." },
    { text: "A retest below the reference mean, because no individual may cross it." },
    { text: "Neither result by itself: an average forecast still permits variation.", correct: true }
  ]}
  explanation="A conditional average does not specify each realised result. To assess the model, examine its predictions and variation across suitable observations rather than treating one path as a deterministic rule."
/>

A **conditional expectation** is the average a model predicts given specified information. Here that information is an initial measurement. The forecast does not tell you the exact next measurement, and it does not identify the cause of a change you later observe.

Consider a toy **task-performance index**, in points, with higher values better. It is an unbounded index in this model, not a percentage or a score restricted to zero through one hundred. The reference population mean is **50**, the given reliability is **0.5**, underlying ability stays fixed and each task adds fresh independent temporary variation. For this model, use:

$$E[\text{next}\mid\text{initial}=x]=50+0.5(x-50).$$

The $E$ denotes the conditional expectation; the vertical bar means “given”. Reliability tells us how much of the initial deviation to retain in this forecast. These are stipulated teaching parameters, not estimates from actual operators.

| Forecast step after an initial index of 90 | Calculation |
|---|---|
| Reference mean | 50 points |
| Initial deviation | 90 − 50 = 40 points |
| Deviation retained in the expectation | 0.5 × 40 = 20 points |
| Expected follow-up | 50 + 20 = 70 points |

The expectation is between 50 and 90. An individual follow-up can nevertheless be below 50, between 50 and 90, or above 90. None is ruled out by this model. Berkeley's regression chapter explicitly distinguishes the average regression effect from any requirement that an individual's second observation be less extreme. [Berkeley on regression errors](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~stark/SticiGui/Text/regressionErrors.htm)

<details>
<summary>Optional derivation and the variation around the forecast</summary>

One precise construction is $X=T+e_0$ and $Y=T+e_1$. The stable ability $T$ has a normal distribution with mean 50 and standard deviation 10. Each temporary deviation $e_0,e_1$ is independently normal with mean zero and standard deviation 10, independent of $T$. A normal distribution is the bell-shaped, unbounded distribution used for this toy construction.

**Variance** measures spread in squared units; standard deviation is its square root. **Covariance** measures shared variation, and **correlation** divides it by the product of the two standard deviations. Each measurement has variance $100+100=200$. Their shared ability contributes covariance 100, giving correlation $100/200=0.5$. For these jointly normal measurements, the conditional mean is the forecast above and the conditional variance is:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=90)=200(1-0.5^2)=150.$$

The conditional standard deviation is $\sqrt{150}\approx12.25$ points, so knowing the first result does not remove variation in the next. The construction and conditional formulas follow the [bivariate-normal theorem in Pishro-Nik's probability text](https://www.probabilitycourse.com/chapter5/5_3_2_bivariate_normal_dist.php). A different distribution or dependence structure needs its own justified forecast.

</details>

### When to use it

Use the distinction when someone turns “expected to be less extreme” into “must improve”, “cannot cross the mean” or “must decline next time”. For a real forecast, establish the relevant reference population, measurement stability and reliability rather than borrowing this example's parameters.

## A real change can be a step or a drift

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="An intervention creates a lasting improvement that stays at the same size. Must its measured benefit keep growing to count as real?"
  options={[
    { text: "Yes: a genuine effect must increase at every subsequent measurement." },
    { text: "No: a persistent level change can be real without a continuing upward trend.", correct: true },
    { text: "Yes: a flat improvement proves that regression has already ended." }
  ]}
  explanation="A lasting change in level differs from a continuing drift. Time-pattern shape alone does not establish causation, and a real effect need not grow each week."
/>

A **level shift** changes the underlying process by an amount that then persists. A **drift** changes it progressively over time. Either can coexist with regression associated with the initial selection.

Keep the initial index of 90 and the same temporary-variation model. Compare these stated hypothetical processes, **conditioning only on that initial observation**:

| Assumed process after selection | First follow-up expectation | Second follow-up expectation | Third follow-up expectation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ability remains unchanged | 70 | 70 | 70 |
| Practice adds a lasting 10 points before the first follow-up | 80 | 80 | 80 |
| Underlying ability decreases by 5 points each period | 65 | 60 | 55 |

For the lasting practice effect, **70 + 10 = 80** at every follow-up. That remains below the selected initial result of 90, even though the stipulated practice effect is beneficial. A decline from the peak can mask a positive change in the underlying process.

For the declining process, **70 − 5 = 65**, then **70 − 10 = 60**, then **70 − 15 = 55**. The reference forecast changes because the underlying process changes; “regression explains it” would not justify ignoring that change.

The unchanged row does **not** predict an automatic sequence of 90 → 70 → 60 → 55. We are using the original information throughout. New observations can update the forecast, but repeatedly shrinking the previous forecast as if it were fresh data invents information.

Nor is regression identifiable as a single visible bounce that must end after one period. A selected cohort can remain below its original peak across many later measurements without any intervention. Persistence is useful to record; it is not by itself a causal test. A dose-response pattern can also be informative, but it needs a design that separates the dose from who receives it and other changing conditions.

### When to use it

Distinguish a forecast under fixed conditions from a process that may change. Check interventions, task difficulty, equipment and measurement rules. Avoid the rule “a real effect keeps growing”: a permanent step, a temporary effect and a gradual drift have different shapes, and their causes still need evidence.

## Independent trials do not owe a reversal

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A fair coin has produced a run of heads. Under independent tossing, what happens to the next toss's chance of tails?"
  options={[
    { text: "It rises because the earlier heads require compensation." },
    { text: "It falls because the coin has demonstrated a preference." },
    { text: "It stays unchanged; the previous run does not alter the mechanism.", correct: true }
  ]}
  explanation="Independence means the prior run does not alter the next toss's distribution. Predicting a less extreme fresh block is different from claiming the opposite outcome is now overdue."
/>

The **gambler's fallacy** is the belief that independent outcomes must compensate for a run. For a fair independent coin, the next toss has probability **0.5 of tails**, even after five heads. It does not rise to repay the streak.

Now define a different question: how many heads should we expect in a **fresh block of five tosses**? The answer is **5 × 0.5 = 2.5**. The first block's five heads was extreme; the expected fresh block is less extreme. That statement does not require a higher tails probability or a stable “coin skill” component. An independent follow-up has its ordinary distribution, regardless of the selected first block.

| Question after five observed heads | Correct expectation or probability |
|---|---|
| Tails on the next toss | 0.5 |
| Heads in the next five tosses | 2.5 |
| Total heads after those five additional tosses | 5 + 2.5 = 7.5 out of 10 |
| Expected cumulative heads fraction then | 7.5 ÷ 10 = 75% |

The expected cumulative fraction after ten tosses is still above one half. In this expectation, fresh observations dilute the starting streak; no realised cancellation is required. A fresh block can be all heads again. Neither an average forecast nor independence forbids that outcome.

### When to use it

Ask exactly what is being predicted: the next event, a new block, a cumulative average or an individual's retest. The information and target determine the forecast. “Regression cannot occur without skill” and “tails is now due” both miss that distinction.

## A comparison estimates an effect without making it exact

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Both an advanced-practice group and a comparable usual-practice group fall from a selected high starting point. Can the new practice still have helped?"
  options={[
    { text: "Yes, if the decline is smaller under new practice; assess the contrast and its uncertainty.", correct: true },
    { text: "No, because a beneficial intervention must beat its own selected initial measurement." },
    { text: "Yes, and any positive contrast automatically equals the exact causal effect." }
  ]}
  explanation="After selection on an extreme baseline, both groups can decline even if the underlying process is unchanged. A between-group contrast can support an effect estimate under the design assumptions, while retaining uncertainty."
/>

Return to the training centre. Before follow-up, similarly selected high scorers are randomly allocated to advanced practice or the usual programme. Use the same tasks and scoring rules, comparable exposure and the same follow-up dates. Retain all allocated operators, check implementation and assume the practice does not affect the comparison group's training. These conditions support the comparison; the totals alone do not verify them.

Here is a separate hypothetical **observed** group record. The earlier table described assumed expectations; this table records realised averages, which need not match an expectation exactly.

| Group | Selected initial average | Follow-up average | Follow-up minus initial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advanced practice | 90 | 78 | 78 − 90 = −12 |
| Usual programme | 90 | 72 | 72 − 90 = −18 |
| Observed difference in changes | | | −12 − (−18) = +6 points |

The advanced-practice average fell by twelve points. It nevertheless fell **six points less** than the comparison average. Under the stated randomized design, that contrast estimates an advantage of advanced practice in this sample and period. It neither proves a harmful twelve-point effect nor identifies a beneficial effect of exactly six points.

The comparison's eighteen-point decline is not a direct measurement of regression alone. Shared conditions, regression associated with selection and random differences can all contribute. Random allocation supports balance **in expectation across possible allocations**, while realised groups can differ. J-PAL explicitly makes that distinction in its [randomization guidance](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/randomization).

To assess precision, retain operator-level outcomes and the allocation design; these averages alone are insufficient. A replicated contrast and well-designed variation in the practice can strengthen the evidence, but persistence or a dose-response pattern is not a substitute for checking the comparison. Self-selection into more practice, for example, can produce a dose pattern without identifying its cause.

### When to use it

Use this comparison after interventions following peaks or slumps. Distinguish the observed before-after change, the contrast estimating an effect and the uncertainty in that estimate. Do not mechanically subtract an assumed amount of regression and label every remainder a proven cause.

## Use a toolkit that can fail its own assumptions

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A large service team has an extreme month because its shared software failed. Does averaging many workers remove that disturbance?"
  options={[
    { text: "Yes: a large headcount always eliminates temporary variation." },
    { text: "No: a shared disturbance can affect everyone's measurement together.", correct: true },
    { text: "Yes: team averages contain no noise after individuals are combined." }
  ]}
  explanation="Averaging can reduce independent temporary variation. A common outage does not cancel merely because many affected workers are averaged."
/>

Use the following checks as questions about the actual process, rather than rules that declare every extreme temporary.

| Check | What to establish | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Inspect selection | Why this period or group was chosen | That all extremeness was noise |
| Check the reference | Which population and historical conditions are relevant | That an old mean survives a changed process |
| Use repeated measurements | Persistence, variation and measurement stability | That a visible pattern identifies its cause |
| Average suitable observations | Less independent measurement variation when assumptions hold | That common shocks or systematic error disappear |
| Build a justified comparison | A defensible alternative without the intervention | An exact finite-sample effect |
| Shrink with a supported model | A forecast balancing the observation and prior information | A universal reliability or a compulsory path |

**Shrinkage** pulls an estimate toward a supported reference rather than treating one noisy observation as the full signal. In the toy normal model, the estimated stable ability after the first index of 90 is 70: the reference plus the retained deviation. That same value predicts an unchanged future task because its fresh noise has mean zero. The interpretation is **Bayesian**: it combines the population distribution of abilities with information from the observed score. It applies to the stated model; other useful shrinkage rules can have different forms.

For an independent transfer, consider a museum's visitor ratings. It changes its welcome script after the lowest-rated weekend of the year. Ratings then rise for a month, but that month also has a different visitor mix. More independent ratings can improve precision; they do not by themselves separate the script, visitor mix and selection on a poor weekend. A practical evaluation keeps the rating question consistent, records comparable periods and visitor groups, and tests the script with a justified allocation and comparison where feasible.

### When to use it

Apply the toolkit when a prediction or evaluation rests on an exceptional measurement. Keep the action proportionate to uncertainty and consequences.
