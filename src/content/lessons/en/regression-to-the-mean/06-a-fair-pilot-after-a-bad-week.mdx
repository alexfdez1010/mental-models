Support replies are late, the dashboard turns red and someone proposes a new triage checklist. The next week looks better. The improvement matters to customers. Whether the checklist caused it is a separate question.

This workshop follows one pilot from its eligibility rule to its review decision. The queues, counts and decision threshold are teaching data. The task is to learn what the comparison can support, rather than to assume the checklist works or that every recovery is noise.

## Inspect the rule that triggered the pilot

The service defines a **late reply** as a first response after the promised deadline. Its **late-reply rate** is the number of late first replies divided by all eligible tickets received in the measurement window. Higher is worse. Follow-up continues until every ticket's deadline has passed; a ticket still unanswered at its deadline counts as late. The definition, ticket types and deadline stay the same throughout this example.

The pilot admits queues with at least a 20% late-reply rate in the latest completed week. Twelve queues qualify. Their combined historical record gives context:

| Completed week | Late replies | Eligible tickets | Late-reply rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three weeks before selection | 156 | 1,200 | 156 ÷ 1,200 = 13% |
| Two weeks before selection | 144 | 1,200 | 144 ÷ 1,200 = 12% |
| One week before selection | 168 | 1,200 | 168 ÷ 1,200 = 14% |
| Selection week | 288 | 1,200 | 288 ÷ 1,200 = 24% |

The selected week is high relative to those earlier observations. That is a reason to examine workload, staffing and measurement, not proof that the next week will return to 13%. A lasting change in demand could keep delays high. Temporary disruption or measurement variation could also have contributed to the spike.

**Regression to the mean** describes a tendency for unusually high or low measurements to be followed by less extreme measurements under an appropriate repeated-measurement process. Barnett and colleagues emphasise both baseline selection and measurement variation as reasons to consider it when interpreting change. It is a candidate explanation, not a finding that every selected queue must improve. [Barnett and colleagues on regression to the mean](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyh299)

### When to use it

Inspect the trigger whenever help, sanctions, publicity or a new process follows an exceptional result. You can address a demonstrated operational problem while keeping the evaluation honest. Waiting for a better comparison does not require ignoring customers whose replies are late.

## Select eligibility first and allocate the pilot second

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Which comparison best addresses this pilot's selection problem?"
  options={[
    { text: "Randomly allocate similarly eligible queues to checklist or usual practice.", correct: true },
    { text: "Give the checklist to the worst queues and compare them with routine queues." },
    { text: "Give every eligible queue the checklist and compare only with its bad week." }
  ]}
  explanation="Both groups should come from the same eligibility process. Random allocation then supports a fair comparison in expectation; routine queues did not enter through the same extreme starting point."
/>

A **comparison group** estimates what would happen under the alternative being considered. Here that alternative is continuing usual triage, with the same existing service and staffing. The pilot changes only the checklist being tested; necessary routine support continues in both groups.

Before revealing any follow-up results, the organiser forms pairs of eligible queues with similar prior workload and delay records. Within each pair, a lottery assigns one queue to the checklist and one to usual practice. There are six queues per group. This is **random allocation within matched pairs**: matching defines comparable pairs; the lottery determines which receives the pilot.

Each queue is handled by a separate team, and the checklist is not shared with comparison teams during the review. Allocating whole queues fits the way the process is delivered. J-PAL distinguishes the unit receiving random allocation from the individual observations collected within it, and explains why interference between groups matters. [J-PAL on randomization and its unit](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/randomization)

In the constructed data, both groups happen to start at a 24% late-reply rate. Random allocation does not guarantee equal starting values or equal future fluctuations in every small pilot. Its protection concerns how the comparison behaves across possible allocations, not a promise that the two realised groups are identical.

### When to use it

Define eligibility and allocation before knowing who improves. If random allocation is unavailable, explain how the comparison was selected and which differences may remain. A longer baseline helps understand variation; it does not turn a non-comparable group into a randomized control.

## Compute the contrast without naming every change regression

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Both selected groups improve after the bad week. What does the usual-practice group's change measure?"
  options={[
    { text: "Observed change under usual practice, which can have several causes.", correct: true },
    { text: "Pure regression, with every other possible influence removed." },
    { text: "Proof that the checklist cannot have any beneficial effect." }
  ]}
  explanation="The comparison group's change can include regression, shared calendar conditions and other variation. It is useful without identifying a pure regression component."
/>

Each selected queue contributes 100 eligible tickets per week in these constructed data, giving 600 per group. After the first follow-up week, the record is:

| Group | Selection week | First follow-up week | Reduction in late-reply rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selected checklist queues | 144 ÷ 600 = 24% | 96 ÷ 600 = 16% | 24% − 16% = 8 percentage points |
| Selected usual-practice queues | 144 ÷ 600 = 24% | 114 ÷ 600 = 19% | 24% − 19% = 5 percentage points |
| Routine queues not selected by the bad-week rule | 72 ÷ 600 = 12% | 66 ÷ 600 = 11% | 12% − 11% = 1 percentage point |

A **percentage point** is the difference between two percentages. Moving from 24% to 16% is an eight-percentage-point reduction. The relative reduction is **8 ÷ 24 ≈ 33.3%**. Keep the units explicit; those are two descriptions of the same observed change, not two effects to add.

Comparing the reductions in the **two selected groups** gives **8 − 5 = 3 percentage points** in favour of the checklist in this week. Because their baselines are equal, the after-only contrast is also **19% − 16% = 3 percentage points**. With the stated allocation and measurement conditions, that contrast is an estimate of the checklist's effect for these eligible queues and this period. It remains uncertain.

Calling all eight points “the effect” ignores the selected comparison. Calling all five comparison points “regression” goes further than the data allow: shared workload conditions, regression and ordinary random variation could all contribute. This table does not separate those components.

Using routine queues instead produces **8 − 1 = 7 percentage points**. That arithmetic is correct, but its causal interpretation is weak: the routine group did not enter through the same bad-week selection. An attractive subtraction cannot repair a mismatched counterfactual.

### When to use it

Use a between-group contrast only after checking who was compared, how they were allocated and which dates were measured. A positive contrast is evidence to assess, rather than an exact causal fact. J-PAL's randomization guidance explicitly distinguishes an estimate's behaviour in expectation from the error that can remain in a particular sample. [J-PAL's explanation of randomization error](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/randomization)

## Keep the review window fixed

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="The first week looks promising, but the plan specified a longer review. What should determine the main summary?"
  options={[
    { text: "The complete review window chosen before seeing the results.", correct: true },
    { text: "Whichever single week gives the largest apparent benefit." },
    { text: "An indefinitely extended window ending when a favourable result appears." }
  ]}
  explanation="A predefined window avoids choosing the result after seeing which week looks best. New exploratory summaries can be useful, but they should not silently replace the planned one."
/>

Before the pilot, the service agreed to review **three complete follow-up weeks**, each with the same ticket eligibility rules. It also chose a practical target: at least **three percentage points fewer late replies** relative to usual practice across that window, subject to acceptable workload and a credible uncertainty assessment. This is the service's decision criterion for the case, not a universal standard.

Here are all the planned follow-up observations:

| Follow-up week | Checklist late replies out of 600 | Usual-practice late replies out of 600 | Observed between-group contrast |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | 96 = 16% | 114 = 19% | 3 percentage points |
| Second | 102 = 17% | 108 = 18% | 1 percentage point |
| Third | 90 = 15% | 102 = 17% | 2 percentage points |
| Whole window | 288 ÷ 1,800 = 16% | 324 ÷ 1,800 = 18% | 2 percentage points |

The pooled calculation uses all eligible tickets: **(96 + 102 + 90) ÷ (600 + 600 + 600) = 16%** for the checklist, and **(114 + 108 + 102) ÷ 1,800 = 18%** for usual practice. The observed contrast is **18% − 16% = 2 percentage points**. Equal weekly denominators mean averaging the three weekly rates gives the same result here. With unequal ticket volumes, that shortcut would not generally give the pooled ticket rate.

A report showing only the first week would make the result look as though it met the three-point target. The whole planned window falls below that target. It does not follow that the true effect is zero, nor that a real effect must grow each week. Effects, workload and estimates can vary over time.

### When to use it

Fix the window when short-term results fluctuate and people can choose when to celebrate. Repeated observations improve the record; they do not automatically eliminate selection bias, shared disruptions or dependence between observations.

## Decide what the pilot supports next

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="The complete window shows a positive contrast below the chosen practical target. What is a defensible next step?"
  options={[
    { text: "Review precision, implementation and costs before deciding on further testing.", correct: true },
    { text: "Roll out immediately because any observed positive number proves value." },
    { text: "Declare the checklist ineffective because the observed target was missed." }
  ]}
  explanation="A result can be positive, below a practical threshold and uncertain at the same time. The next decision should use those distinctions rather than convert them into automatic success or failure."
/>

The team records: **“Across the planned window, checklist queues had two percentage points fewer late replies than selected usual-practice queues. This observed contrast is below our three-point practical target. We have not established the precision of the effect estimate from this aggregate table.”** That sentence preserves the improvement without overstating attribution.

There are **twelve allocation units: queues**, even though the table contains thousands of tickets. Tickets handled by one team may share staffing and workload conditions. An uncertainty analysis must respect the queue-level allocation and pairing, rather than treat every ticket as an independently randomized trial. The aggregate counts shown here are insufficient to calculate that analysis; keep queue-level records.

Before deciding on a larger test, the service checks whether teams actually used the checklist, whether comparison teams copied it, whether ticket mix or staffing changed differently, and whether any tickets disappeared from measurement. These checks can change what the contrast means. A complete-case table can look reassuring if the slowest unresolved tickets were quietly excluded.

The decision is to **defer a full rollout on this evidence**, check implementation and queue-level uncertainty, then decide whether a revised, prospectively planned test is worth its cost. Routine support continues. If a larger test is chosen, its outcomes, allocation and review date are specified before results arrive. The team does not extend the current pilot repeatedly until a favourable week appears.

### When to use it

Use this review when a pilot starts after an alarm. Distinguish operational improvement, an estimated causal contrast and a rollout decision. A plausible regression concern does not prove that the intervention did nothing; a surviving contrast does not prove its exact size, durability or value in other queues.
