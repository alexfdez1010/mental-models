A manager changes a procedure after an unusually bad month. The following month looks better. The improvement is worth recording; attributing it to the procedure needs more evidence. Some of the earlier extreme may have been temporary variation, and conditions may also have changed between months.

The **regression fallacy** is treating a change associated with regression to the mean as evidence for a particular cause without adequately checking alternatives. “It improved after we acted” does not separate the action from what would have happened without it. The opposite claim, “the action did nothing because regression exists”, is unsupported too.

## What an extreme does to the comparison

  explanation="The observed change is real. Selection on a noisy extreme creates a possible regression effect, while camera effects and other changes may also matter. This record alone cannot divide the fall among them."

An observed result can combine a persistent component with temporary variation. Selecting the worst result can select both a genuinely difficult case and an unusually unfavourable temporary fluctuation. If underlying conditions remain sufficiently stable and repeat measurements are imperfectly correlated, the next result can be expected to be less extreme under an appropriate model. A **conditional expectation** is the average predicted for cases sharing the information used to select them; it is not a promise about each case.

As [Berkeley's regression explanation stresses](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~stark/SticiGui/Text/regressionErrors.htm), the regression effect does not require every individual to move closer to average. A site can have another bad year. A selected group's observed average can also worsen in a finite follow-up. The size and even the suitability of a regression prediction depend on the measurement process and distribution, rather than the word “extreme” alone.

There are two distinct errors to avoid:

1. **Ignore the selection.** Compare a deliberately selected extreme with its next measurement as though the first were a typical baseline.
2. **Promote a tendency into a guarantee.** Declare that improvement must occur, that all units will improve, or that every observed improvement is regression.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Check what stayed stable">
A new machine fault, a changed customer mix or a different counting rule can alter the underlying process. An old average is not a force that pulls every changed system back. Investigate those conditions before using a stable-process regression model.
</Callout>

## A cover story without a guaranteed curse

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="A magazine features a player after an exceptional scoring run. The player then scores less often. What needs checking before blaming the cover?"
  options={[

    { text: 'Whether the later decline proves the photoshoot reduced their skill.' },
    { text: 'Whether exceptional players are required to decline in the next round.' },
    { text: 'Whether appearing first in time is sufficient evidence of a causal effect.' }
  ]}
  explanation="A peak-based selection rule can generate a less extreme follow-up without a cover effect. The cover could have consequences too; the sequence alone does not identify them."
/>

Imagine a sports magazine selecting players after unusually successful runs. Ability matters, but temporary influences such as favourable opportunities can contribute to the peak. A later decline would be compatible with regression, rather than requiring a curse. It could also involve injury, changed opposition or other causes. This scenario explains a possible selection mechanism; it is not a historical evaluation of a magazine's effects.

To isolate temporary variation, give four players the same fixed underlying ability of 50 points. Each round independently adds −20, −10, +10 or +20 points, with each value equally likely. Draws are independent between players and rounds. No cover, coaching change or injury occurs.

| Player | Fixed ability | First-round score | One possible second-round score |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 50 | 60 | 70 |
| B | 50 | 60 | 70 |
| C | 50 | 40 | 30 |
| D | 50 | 30 | 40 |

Select A and B because they have the highest first-round scores. Their initial average is (60 + 60) ÷ 2 = **60 points**. The temporary contribution has mean (−20 − 10 + 10 + 20) ÷ 4 = **0 points**, so each selected player's expected second-round score is 50 + 0 = **50 points**. The selected group's expected average is therefore also 50.

### Read the conditional result

The displayed follow-up is possible under exactly that model: both selected players draw +20. Their observed average is (70 + 70) ÷ 2 = **70 points**, even further above 50 than before. The expected reduction was ten points; this particular outcome instead increases by ten. An expectation averages possible follow-ups rather than prescribing this one.

This deliberately simple case isolates variation; real players can differ persistently in ability. Their performance and its temporary variation need measurements and a justified model. A selected group's further rise does not, by itself, establish that ability improved or that an intervention caused it.

## A comparison group changes the question

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="Why measure a comparable group during the same period as an intervention group?"
  options={[
    { text: 'To estimate change that might also occur without the intervention.', correct: true },
    { text: 'To guarantee that both groups experience identical random fluctuations.' },
    { text: 'To label every change in the comparison group as regression alone.' },
    { text: 'To remove the need to check measurement rules and other changes.' }
  ]}
  explanation="A comparison group helps construct the missing no-intervention comparison. It does not make separate groups identical, identify every source of change or repair inconsistent measurements."
/>

A **comparison group**, or control group, is measured without receiving the intervention being evaluated. The aim is to learn about the **counterfactual**: what would have happened to the intervention group without that intervention. You cannot observe both versions of the same group's history, so the design must justify using another group's outcomes as evidence.

If all eligible units meet the same selection rule, randomly allocating them to intervention and comparison groups helps balance their characteristics **in expectation across possible allocations**. It does not guarantee identical baseline totals, the same regression in this particular trial or equal later events. [NIST's randomized-design guide](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pri/section3/pri331.htm) treats observed responses as effects plus experimental error, rather than effects alone.

For the comparison to support an intervention-effect estimate, check that outcomes are measured consistently over comparable periods, relevant exposure is accounted for, implementation is understood and one group's intervention does not change the other's outcomes. That last problem is a **spillover**: for example, cameras diverting traffic into comparison sites. If allocation is not random, baseline similarity alone does not establish comparability: earlier trends and unmeasured differences can still matter.

### Work through the camera example

Consider a hypothetical evaluation with ten sites in each group. All were eligible because of unusually high recent collision counts; allocation to cameras or no cameras is random. The totals happen to match before installation. Retain outcomes for every allocated site and measure the same type of collision over equally long periods, with comparable traffic exposure and reporting. Assume no other differential change or spillover between groups. These are design assumptions to check, not findings established by the table.

| Group | Earlier annual collision total | Later annual collision total | Later minus earlier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ten camera sites | 20 | 12 | 12 − 20 = −8 |
| Ten comparison sites | 20 | 14 | 14 − 20 = −6 |
| **Observed difference in changes** | | | **−8 − (−6) = −2** |

The camera group's raw reduction is 8 collisions, or 8 ÷ 20 = **40%** of its earlier count. The comparison group's reduction is 6 collisions, or **30%** of its earlier count. The observed difference in changes is **−2 collisions across a group of ten sites per annual period**, equivalent to −0.2 per site when averaged across the ten sites. Under the stated design assumptions, it is an estimate of an additional reduction associated with allocation to cameras. It is not a known exact causal effect.

The control's reduction of 6 is **not a direct measurement of regression alone**. It can combine regression, broader traffic trends, weather, reporting changes and chance. Random allocation supports comparison of groups; it does not reveal those components separately. Nor does it guarantee that their finite-sample contributions cancel exactly.

### Keep uncertainty in the result

To assess the estimate's precision, you need more than these totals: site-level variation, the allocation design, exposure and suitable uncertainty analysis. Without those, the table does not tell you whether the observed contrast is distinguishable from random variation. Do not relabel a two-collision contrast as an established effect or multiply it into a guaranteed benefit elsewhere.

Regression is a documented evaluation concern in this setting. A [2007 Department for Transport circular](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a819278e5274a2e87dbe588/dft-circular-0107.pdf) discusses it when summarising camera evaluation and reports benefits after adjustment. That historical report is separate from the hypothetical numbers above. Recognising regression does not establish that cameras work, fail or have a particular effect at these sites.

## Transfer the check to a print shop

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="A print shop revises its checklist after the shift with the most rejected sheets. Rejections decline on the next shift. Which question targets the missing comparison?"
  options={[
    { text: 'What would rejection rates have done under the previous checklist?', correct: true },
    { text: 'How can the full decline be credited to the checklist immediately?' },
    { text: 'Why must every later shift now have a lower rejection rate?' },
    { text: 'Which description makes the original shift sound most exceptional?' }
  ]}
  explanation="The relevant counterfactual is the outcome without the revision. An unusually poor trigger shift makes a simple before-and-after comparison ambiguous."
/>

Suppose the later shift also uses a different paper batch and prints simpler jobs. A lower rejection count could reflect the checklist, the paper, the job mix, temporary variation or a combination. If the volume printed changes, compare a consistent **rejection rate**—rejected sheets divided by sheets printed—rather than raw counts alone. Check that the rule for calling a sheet defective has stayed the same.

Before choosing a design, define the outcome, how the poor shift was selected and which changes can be separated. Where practical, randomly allocate comparable production runs to the checklists, use the same acceptance rule and record job type, paper and machine. Ensure that using one checklist does not train operators in a way that contaminates the comparison runs. If random allocation is not feasible, use multiple earlier periods and a justified contemporaneous comparison, then state the remaining assumptions. A longer history can make the baseline more informative, but does not by itself create a causal control.

### When to use this check

Use it when an action follows a peak, a slump or a rule that selects unusually high or low measurements. Ask how the units were selected, what could change without the intervention and how the comparison represents that counterfactual. Keep the observed change, the estimated effect and the uncertainty separate. Evidence that the before-and-after is ambiguous is a reason to improve the evaluation, not to assume the intervention's effect is zero.
