A test station posts an excellent reading. Its next reading might fall, repeat exactly or rise further. “Regression to the mean” describes a pattern in conditional averages, not a rule that decides which of those happens next.

We will build that pattern from a small experiment whose possibilities you can count. No normal curve or hidden statistical software is needed. The arithmetic will also show why fresh random variation can repeat an earlier value.

## Define the persistent part and the temporary part

The **signal** is the persistent part of the measurement in the model. The **noise** is temporary variation around that part. “Persistent” describes what carries across tests under the model's assumptions; it does not mean the component is eternal, desirable or free from systematic measurement bias.

Our experiment has four stations with fixed benchmark levels: **A = 30, B = 50, C = 50 and D = 70 points**. Choose one station at random, with each equally likely. Each test adds either **−10 or +10 points**, each with probability one half. Draw that noise independently of the station and independently on each test. Nothing changes a station's persistent level between tests.

Write the observed index as $X$, the station level as $T$ and the temporary deviation as $e$:

$$X=T+e.$$

These are teaching rules, not measured laboratory data. B and C intentionally have the same level: middle-level stations are twice as common as either extreme level in this little population.

For one possible test of B, the level is 50 and the temporary deviation is +10, so the observed index is **50 + 10 = 60**.

## Selection changes which possibilities remain

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="You keep only high initial readings. What must be checked before interpreting their average as persistent station quality?"
  options={[
    { text: "Which station levels and temporary draws could have produced them.", correct: true },
    { text: "Whether every selected station must have had positive temporary noise." },
    { text: "Whether selecting a reading changes the station's physical level." }
  ]}
  explanation="The selected measurements can reflect both persistent levels and temporary variation. Enumeration shows their mixture without assuming every selected case was lucky."
/>

There are **eight equally likely initial possibilities**: four station choices times two noise draws. The table lists possible runs of the experiment, not eight different stations tested together.

| Station | Persistent level | Initial noise | Initial observed index | Keep if at least 60? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 30 | −10 | 20 | No |
| A | 30 | +10 | 40 | No |
| B | 50 | −10 | 40 | No |
| B | 50 | +10 | 60 | Yes |
| C | 50 | −10 | 40 | No |
| C | 50 | +10 | 60 | Yes |
| D | 70 | −10 | 60 | Yes |
| D | 70 | +10 | 80 | Yes |

The whole population's mean is **(30 + 50 + 50 + 70) ÷ 4 = 50**. Average noise is zero, so the average initial reading across all eight possibilities is also 50.

Now **condition on** a high reading: restrict attention to possibilities consistent with an initial result of at least 60. Four equally likely possibilities remain. Their observed average is **(60 + 60 + 60 + 80) ÷ 4 = 65**. Their persistent-level average is **(50 + 50 + 70 + 70) ÷ 4 = 60**. Their initial-noise average is **(10 + 10 − 10 + 10) ÷ 4 = 5**. Thus:

**Selected observed average 65 = selected persistent average 60 + selected temporary average 5.**

The selected possibilities are enriched in positive noise **on average**. Not every selected case has positive noise: D can enter with a −10 draw. Likewise, selecting a high reading does not prove every chosen station is the population's best one.

### When to use it

Inspect the selection rule when someone ranks the best, targets the worst or acts after a record result. Conditioning means using the possibilities consistent with the information you have. Pishro-Nik's [conditioning chapter](https://www.probabilitycourse.com/chapter5/5_1_3_conditioning_independence.php) gives the general probability rule; this finite table makes it visible by counting.

## Count the retest instead of prescribing a path

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A selected station is tested again with fresh independent noise. Which information carries over?"
  options={[
    { text: "Its fixed station level, while the next noise draw keeps its ordinary probabilities.", correct: true },
    { text: "Its entire first reading, because selection makes temporary variation permanent." },
    { text: "A debt requiring a negative draw whenever the first draw was positive." }
  ]}
  explanation="The level persists under the model. The new noise is independent, so its mean is zero and either sign is still possible."

Each of the four selected initial possibilities has two equally likely retests. Here are **all eight complete possibilities after selection**:

| Selected initial possibility | Initial index | Fresh retest noise | Retest index |
|---|---|---|---|
| B with initial +10 | 60 | −10 | 40 |
| B with initial +10 | 60 | +10 | 60 |
| C with initial +10 | 60 | −10 | 40 |
| C with initial +10 | 60 | +10 | 60 |
| D with initial −10 | 60 | −10 | 60 |
| D with initial −10 | 60 | +10 | 80 |
| D with initial +10 | 80 | −10 | 60 |
| D with initial +10 | 80 | +10 | 80 |

Their average retest is **(40 + 60 + 40 + 60 + 60 + 80 + 60 + 80) ÷ 8 = 60**. Their initial average was 65. The expected average has moved toward the population mean of 50 while retaining a real persistent advantage.

A **conditional expectation** averages the possibilities consistent with the information given, weighting each by its conditional probability. Here the information is the high initial reading rule, and the eight remaining paths are equally likely, so an ordinary average gives 60. A single retest need not equal 60, and a small realised sample need not have exactly that average. [Pishro-Nik on conditional expectation](https://www.probabilitycourse.com/chapter5/5_1_5_conditional_expectation.php)

Read the paths individually. A first reading of 60 can be followed by **40**, crossing below the population mean of 50. It can be followed by **80**, becoming more extreme. An 80 can repeat as **80**. In four of the eight rows, the noise value repeats and the observed score is unchanged. Independence allows repetition; it does not avoid it.

These outcomes do not violate the regression of the conditional average from 65 to 60. Berkeley's regression text makes the same distinction: the regression effect describes averages and does not require an individual's second observation to be less extreme. [Berkeley on the regression effect](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~stark/SticiGui/Text/regressionErrors.htm)

### When to use it

Distinguish “the selected average is expected to be less extreme” from “this unit must decline”. Use the distribution of possible outcomes for individual decisions. A single rise, repeated value or crossing of the mean is not, by itself, proof that the model failed or that a compensating force exists.

## Persistence changes what the first result tells you

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Suppose every station instead has the same fixed level, with independent temporary variation. What would a high first reading reveal about persistent differences between stations?"
  options={[
    { text: "Nothing about differences that do not exist; the high reading reflects the temporary draw.", correct: true },
    { text: "It would prove the chosen station has a permanently higher level than the others." },
    { text: "It would force the next temporary draw to have the opposite sign." }
  ]}
  explanation="When all station levels match, there is no persistent between-station advantage to infer. The next independent draw still follows the same noise rule."
/>

**Persistence** means that part of what distinguishes units carries across measurements. In the main experiment, a high initial reading makes D more prominent among the remaining possibilities. That is why the expected retest remains above 50 rather than collapsing all the way to it.

Compare three deliberately specified limits:

| Model | Information in a high initial reading | Retest implication |
|---|---|---|
| Different fixed levels and fresh noise, as above | A mixture of persistent differences and temporary variation | The high-selection expectation falls from 65 to 60, not to 50 |
| Every station fixed at 50, with fresh −10 or +10 noise | No persistent differences between stations | After a reading of 60, retests are 40 or 60 equally often in the possibilities; expectation 50 |
| Original station levels with no temporary noise | The reading identifies the fixed station level | The reading repeats exactly while that level stays unchanged |

The middle row has **zero persistence in differences between units**, not a zero baseline: all stations still have level 50. A retest of 60 can repeat with probability one half. The last row has no fresh variation to create regression, but its conclusion still depends on levels remaining fixed.

In this independent additive model, **reliability** describes the fraction of observed variation attributable to persistent differences. More persistent information can support a forecast retaining more of an observed deviation. That is a statement about the model and its variation, not an instruction to label every high scorer “mostly skill” by intuition.

<details>
<summary>Optional arithmetic for reliability in this finite example</summary>

**Variance** is the average squared deviation from a mean. The four persistent levels have mean 50, so their variance is **(400 + 0 + 0 + 400) ÷ 4 = 200 squared points**. The temporary deviations have mean zero and variance **(100 + 100) ÷ 2 = 100 squared points**. Independence makes the observed variance **200 + 100 = 300**.

Thus the reliability is $r=200/300=2/3$. For this particular table, enumeration verifies the linear conditional-mean rule $E[Y\mid X=x]=50+(2/3)(x-50)$ at each possible initial reading: 20, 40, 60 and 80. Here $Y$ is the retest and $E[Y\mid X=x]$ is its expected value given an initial reading of $x$.

Averaging the four selected forecasts gives 60. Because this verified rule is linear, using the selected initial average gives the same result: **50 + (2/3) × (65 − 50) = 60**. The 65 is an average of possible readings, not an additional possible reading to condition on.

Do not assume that a reliability ratio alone determines every conditional forecast. Other signal or noise distributions can require a nonlinear conditional average. Count the possibilities here; use a justified model elsewhere. If variation is correlated across tests, the fresh-independent-noise assumption also needs revision.

</details>

### When to use it

Ask what is persistent, which reference population is relevant and how repeat measurements are related. The special cases prevent two opposite mistakes: dismissing every strong result as noise, or assuming every high reading reveals a lasting advantage.

## Check the conditions before transferring the model

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A persistent fault changes the underlying process after the first reading. Should the old fixed-level forecast be applied unchanged?"
  options={[
    { text: "Yes: selecting an extreme guarantees the same return toward the old mean." },
    { text: "No: a changed persistent component changes the forecast's assumptions.", correct: true },
    { text: "Yes: any later observation must be noise because the first one was selected." }
  ]}
  explanation="The fixed-level calculation depends on fixed levels. A real process change does not disappear because regression is also a possible concern."
/>

The decomposition is useful when its assumptions fit. If a device's calibration changes, a team learns a new procedure or work becomes harder, the persistent component may change. If all devices share a power fluctuation, their temporary errors may move together. Averaging many affected devices does not make that shared disturbance cancel automatically.

For an independent worked transfer, suppose a delivery route has a persistent travel time of **30 minutes**, plus a fresh independent temporary deviation of **−5 or +5 minutes**, equally likely. A trip lasting 35 minutes is selected for review. With the route unchanged, the next possibilities are **25 or 35 minutes**, so the expectation is **(25 + 35) ÷ 2 = 30 minutes**. Repeating 35 is allowed.

Now a road closure adds **15 persistent minutes** before the next trip. The new base is **30 + 15 = 45 minutes**. The next possibilities become **40 or 50 minutes**, averaging **45**. Both are longer than the original 35. That does not refute the fixed-route arithmetic; the route no longer satisfies its assumptions.

| Transfer question | Relevant check |
|---|---|
| Why was the trip examined? | It was selected after a high observed duration |
| What repeats in the original model? | The route's base time, not its exact temporary delay |
| What changed before retesting? | A closure changed the persistent route time |
| Does a worse next trip prove the temporary-noise idea false? | No; the old unchanged-route forecast was applied to a changed process |

Regression reasoning also does not establish an intervention effect. If a change is made after an extreme and the result improves, the observation can combine the intervention, selection-related regression and other conditions. Later lessons construct comparisons for that causal question; a signal-and-noise decomposition alone does not answer it.

### When to use it

Use the model to improve a forecast or question an attribution, then check the physical and measurement conditions. Do not treat an extreme as proof of instability, persistence or a particular cause. Preserve the observation while being explicit about what remains uncertain.
