A trainer praises an outstanding performance. The next attempt is worse. After a dreadful attempt, the trainer gives a stern correction. The next attempt improves. Soon the trainer has a personal theory of learning, with shouting in a starring role.

The missing question is what would have happened after those same starting performances under another response. **Feedback selected by performance** carries a built-in comparison problem: praise and correction are being tested at different starting points.

## What the instructors actually establish

Tversky and Kahneman's 1974 paper reports a discussion with flight instructors: praise after excellent landings tended to be followed by worse landings, while criticism after poor landings tended to be followed by improvement. The authors use it to illustrate a mistaken inference about reward and punishment. Their account presents an observation, without a randomized comparison or an estimated feedback effect. [Read the original paper, p 1127](https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/mpsingh/local/Social/f24/wrap/readings/Tversky%2BKahneman-heuristics-biases-1974.pdf)

The statistical issue survives even if the instructors remember the sequence accurately. An unusually high score can partly reflect favourable temporary variation; an unusually low score can partly reflect unfavourable variation. With a sufficiently stable process, a suitable regression model can predict less extreme follow-up averages. Feedback then arrives just before a change it may not have caused.

That is a statement about a conditional average: the average predicted after selecting cases with particular starting information. It does not require every trainee to decline after praise or improve after criticism. A new task, changed skill or persistent disruption can also make an old regression prediction inappropriate.

### When to use this distinction

Reach for it when someone evaluates their response by what happened immediately after the event that triggered it. Preserve the observed change. Question its attribution. The historical account is a warning about comparison design, not a trial demonstrating that praise always helps or punishment always fails.

## Give a powerless feedback rule a trial

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="Could feedback with exactly zero influence look helpful after poor performances and harmful after good ones?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Yes, if the response is selected by the starting score.', correct: true },
    { text: 'No, a visible change proves some feedback influence.' },
    { text: 'Only if the evaluator misremembers the scores.' }
  ]}
  explanation="An accurate record can still support a misleading comparison. The following fully specified experiment produces the pattern without any feedback influence."
/>

Here is an invented proofreading experiment whose entire mechanism we know. Every participant has a fixed level of **60 points**. Each timed task adds either **−20 or +20 points**, with equal probability. Draw each task's variation independently of the previous task. Scores therefore take only the values 40 and 80.

The trainer praises a score of 80 and criticizes a score of 40. **By assumption, neither response changes ability, task conditions or the next random draw.** This is a demonstration of what selection can do, rather than evidence about real trainers.

There are four equally likely two-task paths:

| First score | Trainer's response | Second score | Observed change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | Criticism | 40 | 0 |
| 40 | Criticism | 80 | +40 |
| 80 | Praise | 40 | −40 |
| 80 | Praise | 80 | 0 |

After criticism, the expected second score is **(40 + 80) ÷ 2 = 60**, so the expected change is **60 − 40 = +20**. After praise, the expected second score is also **60**, so the expected change is **60 − 80 = −20**. The feedback has zero effect by construction, yet a review based on average change would applaud the criticism and blame the praise.

Notice the deliberately awkward detail: **nobody actually scores 60**. Half the possible paths repeat the first score. The expected value sits between the possible outcomes; it is not a compulsory destination. A finite group could also happen to repeat its scores throughout.

### When the toy model stops applying

Its conclusion depends on fixed levels, independent task variation and zero feedback influence. Real feedback might change knowledge, effort or conditions. Real task difficulty might persist. The toy example proves that the observed pattern is possible without a feedback effect; it cannot tell us the effect in an actual classroom or workplace.

## Keep the starting band fixed when testing feedback

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="A proofreading trainer wants to test task-specific guidance. Which comparison addresses that question?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Guidance after poor attempts versus praise after excellent attempts.' },
    { text: 'Within each starting-score band, randomly assign guidance or the usual feedback.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Keep changing the response until the next score rises.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The same starting band gives both responses the same selection rule. Random assignment then supports a comparison between the specified feedback policies, subject to implementation and measurement assumptions."
/>

Move from the known toy mechanism to a **separate invented pilot**. Its follow-up scores are teaching data, not draws from the two-value model above. The trainer compares a written, task-specific guidance sheet with the usual feedback, which reports the score and lists detected errors. Both groups continue ordinary training.

Before any sheets are delivered, the trainer sets the rules:

1. Define the bands before the qualifying task: **45 points or below** for the low band and **75 or above** for the high band. Enrol **40 volunteers in each band** using that common task.
2. Within each band, randomly assign **20 people to guidance and 20 to usual feedback**. Assignment happens after the qualifying score, before the new feedback.
3. Deliver the assigned feedback at the same time. Keep task duration, practice opportunity and access to other materials the same. Assume participants do not exchange the new sheet.
4. One week later, give everyone the same new proofreading task, using the same 0–100 rubric. A marker who does not know assignments scores it. Include everyone assigned; assume complete follow-up.
5. Specify the comparison and a practical target before seeing results: an average advantage of **at least 4 score points** would justify the sheet's preparation time, provided the evidence is sufficiently precise.

The starting bands are **strata**: groups defined before assignment so that the random comparison can be made within each. [J-PAL's randomization guide](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/randomization) describes this design and explains why randomization balances other influences in expectation, rather than making every realized group identical.

Here are the observed means. The equal starting means are a feature of these invented data, not a guarantee of random assignment.

| Starting band | Assigned feedback | People | Qualifying mean | Follow-up mean | Change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | Usual | 20 | 40 | 52 | +12 |
| Low | Guidance | 20 | 40 | 57 | +17 |
| High | Usual | 20 | 80 | 68 | −12 |
| High | Guidance | 20 | 80 | 73 | −7 |

### What is being tested

This pilot tests **this guidance sheet versus this usual-feedback policy, after these qualifying tasks, over one week**. It does not test shouting against kindness, all rewards against all punishments, or effects on long-term retention. A pleasant tone and a useful explanation are different features; changing several features together estimates the package rather than isolating each ingredient.

For the low band, the guidance group's gain is **57 − 40 = 17 points**. The usual-feedback group gains **52 − 40 = 12 points**. The difference in observed changes is **17 − 12 = +5 points** in favour of guidance. Since the starting means are equal here, subtracting follow-up means gives the same answer: **57 − 52 = +5**.

With equal-sized bands and arms, the pooled guidance mean is **(57 + 73) ÷ 2 = 65**, and the pooled usual-feedback mean is **(52 + 68) ÷ 2 = 60**. Their difference is again **+5 points**. The simple average is valid here because each row contains the same number of people. With unequal sizes, compare both feedback policies using the same starting-band weights, chosen to represent the intended population, and report the band-specific results too.

### When to use these calculations

Use a same-trigger comparison when a response follows an extreme. Calculate both raw changes and the between-group contrast so the story cannot quietly switch between them. Here both bands favour guidance by the same observed amount; real bands can differ, and a pooled result can conceal that difference.

## Decide what the evidence is strong enough to support

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="The observed guidance advantage exceeds the practical target. The table contains only group sizes and means. Can the trainer declare the target securely achieved?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Yes, crossing the target removes sampling uncertainty.' },
    { text: 'No, precision and implementation still need assessment.', correct: true },
    { text: 'No, regression makes every trial useless.' }
  ]}
  explanation="A point estimate can exceed a useful-effect threshold while remaining imprecise. Group means alone do not supply the outcome variation needed for an uncertainty assessment."
/>

The **observed five-point contrast** is a useful estimate under the stated random-assignment design. It is not the exact effect handed down on a stone tablet. A different random allocation or a different week's tasks could produce another contrast. Means and group sizes alone do not tell us how variable individual scores were, so this table does not tell us how precisely the pilot has estimated the advantage.

The practical target was four points. Five exceeds it numerically by **one point**, but that margin cannot establish whether the true average advantage clears the target. Retain individual scores and assignments, assess uncertainty using an analysis that respects the two starting bands, and check whether delivery and follow-up actually matched the plan. Report missing outcomes or shared sheets rather than silently dropping inconvenient cases.

In this low-cost invented pilot, the trainer keeps usual training in place while planning a larger comparison. The review rules will be fixed before collecting new results, and adoption of the sheet as the default will wait for that review. That decision reflects uncertain evidence and preparation time. A different cost or consequence could justify a different next step; the mental model does not set those priorities for you.

### Limits that matter for feedback

Learning can change the persistent signal. Instructions can change what a participant knows. Motivation, task mix and measurement can change too. Do not explain away a genuine mechanism because regression is possible. Equally, repeated improvement or an impressive personal testimonial does not remove the need for a fair comparison. Evaluate the particular response, outcome and time window you care about.

## Catch the same pattern in a ritual

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="A translation club starts a lucky playlist only after a member's worst round so far. Scores often rise next round. What should the club test?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Whether eligible post-poor-round members do better with the playlist than under a comparable alternative.', correct: true },
    { text: 'Whether any member ever improves after hearing it.' },
    { text: 'Whether scores always rise after every poor round.' }
  ]}
  explanation="The playlist follows selected poor results. A fair comparison must preserve that trigger while changing the response, rather than crediting every subsequent rise to the playlist."
/>

This is an independent invented case. The playlist could affect concentration; calling it lucky does not prove it powerless. The club's existing record lacks comparable rounds without it. New prompts might also be easier, members might practise, and judging might change.

The club can define eligibility after the same poor-round rule, randomly assign eligible members to the playlist or ordinary quiet practice, fix the next-round date and prompt difficulty, and use the same blinded judging rubric. It should account for people hearing another member's audio or carrying the playlist into a later round. If those conditions cannot be controlled, the conclusion must retain that limitation.

The first decision is therefore to improve the comparison, rather than label the playlist effective or useless. **Superstition in this lesson is the causal belief built from the selectively timed aftermath**, not a mathematical proof that every unfamiliar ritual has zero effect.

### When to reach for the model

Check the trigger whenever a response seems unusually good at rescuing the worst cases or unusually bad at preserving the best. Ask what changed in the process, what stayed comparable and how an alternative response was assigned. Regression is a reason to investigate attribution, not a reason to stop investigating.
