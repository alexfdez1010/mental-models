Two players say they choose Heads half the time. It sounds like the matching-pennies equilibrium from lesson three. Before accepting the claim, ask how their choices are generated. Two private coins and one shared coin can produce identical individual frequencies and completely different results.

This workshop audits the rules rather than a short run of lucky outcomes. You will compute what each player expects, what each could gain by changing their own rule, and which assumptions make that comparison valid.

Our constructed game has two players, **Row** and **Column**. Each chooses **Heads** or **Tails** without seeing the other's realised action. Row likes a match; Column likes a mismatch. There is one decision, with this fixed table of **utility points**:

**Utility** is the player's numerical valuation of an outcome. Here we stipulate that each player chooses the rule with the highest **expected utility**: the sum of each possible utility multiplied by its probability. The scale matters for that averaging. Merely relabelling outcomes from best to worst does not tell us how the player values uncertain combinations.

A **pure strategy** chooses one action with certainty. A **mixed strategy** specifies probabilities over the actions, including a probability of zero or one. A **profile** is the pair of players' strategies. For now, their random draws are private and **independent**: learning the realised result of one draw would not change the distribution of the other. The probability rules are known; the realised actions are hidden until both commit.

We are analysing a stipulated mathematical game, not measuring someone's risk preferences or advising anyone to bet. A randomising rule is also different from alternating on a visible schedule: if the rival can infer your next action, the information in this model has changed.

### When this model applies

Use the audit when the actions, utilities and information are specified well enough to calculate a comparison. If either player values uncertainty differently from the stated utility scale, observes the rival's action, or changes behaviour after a recommendation, write down that new problem first.

## Turn two probabilities into four outcomes

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="Row chooses Heads one quarter of the time and Column chooses it three quarters of the time, independently. Which pair should be most common?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Heads for both players' },
    { text: 'Heads for Row and Tails for Column' },
    { text: 'Tails for Row and Heads for Column', correct: true },
    { text: 'Tails for both players' },
  ]}
  explanation="Row usually chooses Tails and Column usually chooses Heads. Independence lets us multiply those two probabilities. Their combination has probability nine sixteenths; the other three combinations must also be included in the average."
/>

Let $p$ be Row's probability of Heads and $q$ Column's probability of Heads. The leftover probabilities are $1-p$ and $1-q$. A **marginal probability** concerns one player's action; a **joint probability** concerns a pair of actions. Under the stated independence assumption:

| Action pair, Row first | Joint probability | At $p=1/4$, $q=3/4$ | Row utility | Contribution to Row's mean |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heads, Heads | $pq$ | $3/16$ | $1$ | $3/16$ |
| Heads, Tails | $p(1-q)$ | $1/16$ | $-1$ | $-1/16$ |
| Tails, Heads | $(1-p)q$ | $9/16$ | $-1$ | $-9/16$ |
| Tails, Tails | $(1-p)(1-q)$ | $3/16$ | $1$ | $3/16$ |

For example, the Tails–Heads probability is $(3/4)(3/4)=9/16$. The four probabilities sum to $(3+1+9+3)/16=1$, as a complete distribution must. Adding the two cells where Row chooses Heads recovers $4/16=1/4$; adding the two where Column chooses Heads recovers $12/16=3/4$.

Multiply each probability by the cell's utility, then add **all four contributions**:

$$
U_R=\frac{3-1-9+3}{16}=-\frac14,
\qquad U_C=-U_R=\frac14.
$$

The subscripts identify Row and Column. Their means are opposite because the utilities are opposite in every cell. They do not each receive the same average just because both randomise.

An expected utility is a probability-weighted mean, not a promised score for this decision. Row can realise only $1$ or $-1$ here, never $-1/4$. Over repeated independent copies of this fixed experiment the sample mean can fluctuate. A handful of realised wins does not certify a strategy profile.

## Hold their rule fixed and test your alternatives

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="Column already has positive expected utility in the biased profile. Does that mean Column has no profitable deviation?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Yes, a positive mean is the equilibrium condition' },
    { text: 'No, another own rule may give an even higher mean', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes, the player currently ahead cannot improve' },
    { text: 'No, the players must change both rules together' },
  ]}
  explanation="The Nash test compares current utility with the best available unilateral alternative. Being ahead is insufficient: Column currently earns one quarter but could earn one half while Row keeps the same rule."
/>

A **best response to a mixture** is a strategy with the highest expected utility against that fixed mixture. Hold Column's Heads probability at $q=3/4$ and compare Row's pure actions. Then hold Row's at $p=1/4$ and do the same for Column, using **Column's own utility**:

| Player changing alone | Always Heads | Always Tails | Current mean | Best pure mean | Gain from changing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Row, with $q=3/4$ fixed | $(3/4)(1)+(1/4)(-1)=1/2$ | $(3/4)(-1)+(1/4)(1)=-1/2$ | $-1/4$ | $1/2$ | $1/2-(-1/4)=3/4$ |
| Column, with $p=1/4$ fixed | $(1/4)(-1)+(3/4)(1)=1/2$ | $(1/4)(1)+(3/4)(-1)=-1/2$ | $1/4$ | $1/2$ | $1/2-1/4=1/4$ |

The **unilateral deviation gain** is best-response utility minus current expected utility, in utility points for that player. It measures an available improvement against a held-fixed rival rule. It is not a feeling of regret after seeing the outcome, and it is not an accumulated learning score.

Why check only the two pure alternatives? Any new mixture is an average of their two values. If a candidate gives Heads probability $s$, Row's value against this fixed $q$ is:

$$s\left(\frac12\right)+(1-s)\left(-\frac12\right)=s-\frac12\leq\frac12 \quad (0\leq s\leq1).$$

The average cannot exceed its larger ingredient. The same argument works for Column. Pure deviations therefore suffice to test whether **any** mixed deviation improves utility, not just the six control options. If both pure values tie, every mixture of those two actions ties against that rival rule.

### When the comparison is useful

Audit both sides of a proposed arrangement. A zero gain for Row alone is not enough. Try Row at half and Column at three quarters: Column cannot improve its mean of zero against Row's half mixture, but Row can improve from zero to one half. One satisfied player does not make a mutually stable profile.

## Find the two mixtures that pass

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="To make Row indifferent between Heads and Tails, whose probability must you solve for?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Row’s, because a player’s own probability fixes their pure values' },
    { text: 'Both at once, without comparing any pure values' },
    { text: 'Column’s, because Row’s pure values depend on Column’s rule', correct: true },
    { text: 'Neither, because randomisation automatically makes actions tie' },
  ]}
  explanation="Fixing a pure Row action leaves only Column’s uncertainty to average over. Thus Column’s Heads probability determines whether Row prefers Heads or Tails. The other equation similarly solves Row’s probability to make Column indifferent."
/>

**Indifference** means equal expected utilities, not a demand that someone feel undecided. With both actions receiving positive probability at equilibrium, neither can be strictly worse than the other: otherwise moving its probability to the better action would help.

| Player's pure alternatives | Expected utility | Condition for a tie |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Row Heads / Tails | $2q-1$ / $1-2q$ | $2q-1=1-2q$, so $q=1/2$ |
| Column Heads / Tails | $1-2p$ / $2p-1$ | $1-2p=2p-1$, so $p=1/2$ |

For instance, Row's Heads value is $q(1)+(1-q)(-1)=2q-1$. At the two half mixtures all four cells have probability $1/4$. Row's mean is $(1-1-1+1)/4=0$; Column's is also zero. Every pure-response value is zero, so both deviation gains are zero. No other mixture can exceed those tied endpoints. This verifies the mixed Nash equilibrium for the stated game.

Set both controls to half and check the two zero gains. This result requires **both** rules; randomising at arbitrary probabilities is not sufficient. The equal split comes from these particular payoffs and independence, not from the word mixed.

## Check whether the draws really are independent

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="A device sends the same fair Heads-or-Tails instruction to both players. They follow it. What happens to Row’s mean?"
  options={[
    { text: 'It is zero because both marginal probabilities are halves' },
    { text: 'It is minus one because sharing creates mismatches' },
    { text: 'It is one half because only one coin was drawn' },
    { text: 'It is plus one because their actions always match', correct: true },
  ]}
  explanation="Following the same instruction produces only Heads–Heads and Tails–Tails. Both give Row plus one. Each individual still chooses Heads half the time, but the pair distribution is no longer the independent product."
/>

Enumerate four equally likely pairs of fair seed bits. A zero bit means Heads and a one means Tails. **Independent** uses one bit per player. **Shared-same** uses the first bit for both and ignores the second. **Shared-opposite** gives Column the opposite of the first bit and also ignores the second.

| Seed pair | Independent actions | Shared-same actions | Shared-opposite actions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $00$ | Heads, Heads | Heads, Heads | Heads, Tails |
| $01$ | Heads, Tails | Heads, Heads | Heads, Tails |
| $10$ | Tails, Heads | Tails, Tails | Tails, Heads |
| $11$ | Tails, Tails | Tails, Tails | Tails, Heads |

These are the complete possible seeds, not four observations from a sampled game. Count what each mechanism produces:

| Mechanism | HH | HT | TH | TT | Row mean | Column mean |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Independent | $1/4$ | $1/4$ | $1/4$ | $1/4$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| Shared-same | $1/2$ | $0$ | $0$ | $1/2$ | $1$ | $-1$ |
| Shared-opposite | $0$ | $1/2$ | $1/2$ | $0$ | $-1$ | $1$ |

Each row still gives each player a Heads marginal of $1/2$. The marginal summaries have thrown away which actions occur together. The product formula belongs to the independent row only.

Does a shared rule make a stable agreement? State the information before answering. Suppose both know the shared-same rule, each receives their recommended action, then each can privately decide whether to follow. To test a unilateral deviation, hold Row’s rule fixed: Row follows its recommendation. Column knows Row has been told the same action. Flipping its own recommendation instead of following then makes a mismatch every time: Column's utility rises from $-1$ to $1$, a gain of $2$. Even committing to always Heads **before** receiving a recommendation earns Column zero instead of minus one, a gain of one.

Thus this shared proposal is not self-enforcing under either of those deviation opportunities. A shared draw is not automatically an equilibrium. Information and allowed responses determine the right comparison; the two independent controls above do not model these recommendation-dependent rules.

Here is the **new constructed game**, with the same timing, independent private draws and expected-utility objective. Every listed entry is Row's utility; Column receives its negative:

| Row / Column | Heads | Tails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heads | $2$ | $-1$ |
| Tails | $-1$ | $1$ |

Before opening the solution, calculate both pure-response pairs, find $p$ and $q$ that tie them, and verify both current means and gains. The island starts at the old half profile so you can inspect why it fails.

Row's pure Heads utility is $2q-(1-q)=3q-1$; its Tails utility is $-q+(1-q)=1-2q$. Tie them: $3q-1=1-2q$, so $5q=2$ and $q=2/5$.

Column's pure Heads utility is $-2p+(1-p)=1-3p$; its Tails utility is $p-(1-p)=2p-1$. Tie them: $1-3p=2p-1$, so $p=2/5$ too.

| Action pair | Joint probability at $p=q=2/5$ | Row contribution | Column contribution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heads, Heads | $4/25$ | $8/25$ | $-8/25$ |
| Heads, Tails | $6/25$ | $-6/25$ | $6/25$ |
| Tails, Heads | $6/25$ | $-6/25$ | $6/25$ |
| Tails, Tails | $9/25$ | $9/25$ | $-9/25$ |

Row's mean is $(8-6-6+9)/25=1/5$; Column's is $-1/5$. Substituting two fifths into both Row pure formulas gives $1/5$; both Column pure formulas give $-1/5$. Both gains are exactly zero. This verifies the equilibrium, even though Column's expected utility is negative.

At the old half profile, Row's current mean is $(2-1-1+1)/4=1/4$. Its pure Heads value is $1/2$ and pure Tails value zero, so its gain is $1/4$. Column's current mean is $-1/4$; pure Heads gives $-1/2$ and pure Tails zero, so its gain is also $1/4$. The equal mixtures fail both tests.

</details>

## State what the audit establishes

<MCQ client:visible className="not-prose" pretest
  question="You verify that neither player can improve unilaterally at a profile. What follows about repeated human play?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Nothing about a learning path follows without further assumptions', correct: true },
    { text: 'Every sequence of best responses must reach that profile' },
    { text: 'A short observed run must have the equilibrium mean' },
    { text: 'The players must already know the correct utility table' },
  ]}
  explanation="The audit is a conditional statement about a specified profile, information and utilities. It does not supply a learning rule, evidence about players’ knowledge, or a guarantee about a short realised sequence."
/>

Nash's finite-game existence result and a numerical equilibrium check answer different questions from “how will these players learn?”. Even in our first game, a rule that updates each pure action to a best response to the **previous** pair can cycle:

| Previous pair | Next pair under simultaneous pure best-response updates |
| --- | --- |
| Heads, Heads | Heads, Tails |
| Heads, Tails | Tails, Tails |
| Tails, Tails | Tails, Heads |
| Tails, Heads | Heads, Heads |

At every step the previous loser changes and the previous winner stays. This particular deterministic update returns to its starting point. It is a constructed counterexample to automatic convergence, not a theorem that all learning fails. The independent half profile still exists and passes the unilateral test.

Use the audit as a checklist for a proposed rule, not a substitute for observing the actual game. You need a defensible utility scale, available actions, timing and randomisation law. If the opponent sees your action before choosing, if a common device couples the actions, or if future rewards matter, the comparison must represent those changes. A successful deviation against a fixed rule does not predict what happens after the rival changes its rule too.

### Sources and the boundary of the examples

The distribution and equilibrium definitions follow [Nash's original equilibrium note](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.36.1.48). The expected-utility formula and the sufficiency of checking pure deviations are set out in [MIT's lecture on normal-form games and Nash equilibrium](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/nfgs_nash.html). The distinction between independent strategies and a recommendation device is explained in [MIT's section on correlated play](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/correlated.html). All numerical tables, seed mechanisms and the transfer case here are constructed and derived from their declared rules.

The final exam now includes these calculations alongside the earlier games. Keep the four steps in order: specify the rules, build the joint law, compute both means, and check both unilateral gains.
