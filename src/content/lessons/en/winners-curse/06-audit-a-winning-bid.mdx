A bid can buy you a disappointing lot. It can also buy you a profitable one. “We won” tells you which offers beat the competition; it has not yet done the accounting.

This workshop turns the **winner's curse** into a decision you can check row by row. You will rebuild what winning means for each candidate bid, calculate the payment separately, and compare the result with taking no lot. **The auction, catalogue, weights, credit values and costs are invented teaching assumptions.** They are not observations from a market or a recommended bidding rule.

## Specify the information before choosing the bid

Imagine a sealed, indivisible lot of surplus parts. Its **common gross value**, V, is the same credit value to either bidder before payment or handling. “Common” describes that shared underlying value; it does not mean the bidders know it. The value is learned after allocation. Credits are a linear comparison scale: we prefer the larger expected net credit total. They are not currency or measured business returns.

There is one rival. R denotes that rival's sealed bid. **Our information I** is the following complete conditional catalogue: given everything we know before bidding, exactly these three states are equally likely. Each has probability one third. We cannot see the actual state or rival bid before submitting. The table displays hypothetical possibilities for analysis, not a live peek inside the envelope.

| State given I | Common gross value V | Rival bid R | Probability weight |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Low | 80 | 70 | 1 |
| Middle | 120 | 110 | 1 |
| High | 160 | 150 | 1 |

Before filtering, our conditional mean given I is:

$$E[V\mid I]=\frac{80+120+160}{3}=120.$$

A **conditional mean** is an average restricted to a specified condition. That first mean already uses our information I. Later we will add the condition that a particular bid wins; keep I attached rather than comparing a knowledgeable bidder with a different, uninformed one.

Our allowed menu is **0, 75, 95, 120 and 155 credits**. Zero means **abstention**: no allocation to us, no payment and zero profit. It does not mean nobody else gets the lot. For a positive bid b, the highest bid wins and **we lose ties**: we receive the lot only if b is strictly greater than R. Losers pay nothing. There is no reserve, entry fee, budget constraint, private value, resale friction or other unlisted outcome.

### When this catalogue is useful

Use a finite catalogue to expose assumptions you might otherwise hide inside “our best estimate.” The joint rows say how value and rival bid vary *together*. Knowing their separate averages would not recover that relationship. The weights here are stipulated probabilities, not counts collected from three auctions. A real application would need a defensible model and evidence for it.

## Rebuild the winning set for each candidate

Think of a bid as a transparent filter laid over the rows. A row passes if our positive bid beats its R. Changing the filter does not change V in a row, but it can change which rows survive.

For **b = 120**, the low state passes because 120 > 70; the middle passes because 120 > 110; the high state fails because 120 < 150. Each passing state retains equal weight. Write $W_b$ for the event that bid b receives the lot. Then:

$$P(W_{120}\mid I)=\frac{2}{3},\qquad E[V\mid I,W_{120}]=\frac{80+120}{2}=100.$$

The extra winning condition revises the mean from 120 to 100. It does not change the value of either selected lot or prove that payment exceeds value in every selected state.

Compare two changes carefully:

| Bid | Winning states | Gross value mean if we win |
|---:|---|---:|
| 75 | Low | 80 |
| 95 | Low | 80 |
| 120 | Low and middle | 100 |
| 155 | All three | 120 |

Moving from 75 to 95 keeps the selected information the same. Moving from 95 to 120 adds the middle state, so the conditional mean changes too. At 155 all states pass, and winning adds no information beyond I in this catalogue. There is no single fixed “winner correction” to subtract regardless of the bid.

### Watch the strict inequality

The displayed positive menu bids do not tie R. If a new candidate of 110 were allowed, it would lose the middle-state tie and still win only the low state. A bid of 70 would win nothing. These are checks of the rule, not additional candidates used to declare the menu winner.

## Separate the winning average from the whole opportunity

Start with **first price**: when we win, we pay our own bid b. With no additional cost, profit is V − b on a winning row and zero otherwise. At b = 120, the complete account is:

| State | Awarded to us? | Gross value | Payment | Our profit |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Low | Yes | 80 | 120 | −40 |
| Middle | Yes | 120 | 120 | 0 |
| High | No | 160 | 0 | 0 |

The **conditional profit given a win** averages only winning rows: (−40 + 0)/2 = **−20**. The **expected profit per opportunity** includes every row: (−40 + 0 + 0)/3 = **−40/3**, about −13.33 credits. The two denominators answer different questions. A losing row contributes zero profit; it does not vanish from the opportunity.

Because losing rows have zero profit, for a positive chance of winning:

$$E[\Pi_b\mid I]=P(W_b\mid I)\,E[\Pi_b\mid I,W_b].$$

Here $\Pi_b$ denotes our net profit from bid b. Multiplying −20 by 2/3 recovers −40/3. If no state wins, the conditional means are **undefined**, because there are no winning weights to average. Display a dash for them, not zero. The overall expected profit is still zero, obtained directly from all the zero-profit rows.

Now audit the **entire allowed menu**, not just whether one win would look acceptable:

| Bid | Probability of an award | Gross value mean if awarded | Profit mean if awarded | Expected profit per opportunity |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0, abstain | 0 | — | — | 0 |
| 75 | 1/3 | 80 | 5 | 5/3 |
| 95 | 1/3 | 80 | −15 | −5 |
| 120 | 2/3 | 100 | −20 | −40/3 |
| 155 | 1 | 120 | −35 | −35 |

At 75, the only award is worth 80 and costs 75: a profit of **5**, even though winning revised the value below its prior mean. At 95 the *same* award costs 20 more, turning that profit into −15; its selection information did not change. At 155 the profits are −75, −35 and 5, with average −35. Winning every state does not make this the best candidate.

**The highest expected profit in this menu is 5/3, at 75.** It wins less often than 120 or 155 and beats abstention's zero under the stipulated expected-credit criterion. This is “best among these candidates,” not a continuous optimum, an equilibrium strategy or a promised profit in a richer model.

### When to use both averages

Use conditional profit to understand what receiving the lot would mean. Use expected profit per opportunity, including the outside option, to compare candidate policies before knowing the state. Choosing a bid changes the event you condition on; do not select a conditional mean once and keep it fixed while changing the bid.

## Change the payment rule while holding the rival fixed

A filter and a cashier do different jobs. In this **second-price counterfactual**, we hold the same joint catalogue, rival bids, information, menu and tie rule fixed. A positive bid still wins exactly when b > R. But if we win we pay **R**, the sole rival's bid, rather than b.

This is a controlled accounting comparison. In a real format change, the rival might change strategy. We are not claiming both bidders best respond under both formats or proving revenue equivalence.

The value-payment difference is now 80 − 70 = 10 in the low state, 120 − 110 = 10 in the middle and 160 − 150 = 10 in the high. Therefore:

| Bid | Winning states | Payment mean if awarded | Profit mean if awarded | Expected profit per opportunity |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0, abstain | None | — | — | 0 |
| 75 | Low | 70 | 10 | 10/3 |
| 95 | Low | 70 | 10 | 10/3 |
| 120 | Low and middle | 90 | 10 | 20/3 |
| 155 | All three | 110 | 10 | 10 |

For the same candidate, the winning set and gross conditional mean did not change. Payment did. Bids 75 and 95 now have identical outcomes because both select the same low row and pay its R. Candidate 155 has the highest expected profit **within this menu and fixed-rival model**.

### Keep the private-value result in its own setting

In the standard single-lot **known private-value** second-price setting, bidding your value is a weakly dominant action: it does at least as well as any other bid against every fixed rival bid. [MIT's auction lecture](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-271-industrial-organization-i-fall-2022/mit14_271_f22_lec22slides.pdf) separates that argument from common-value reasoning. Here our value is uncertain and linked to R. The private-value argument does not make bidding the earlier mean dominant or insure us against losses. The next change provides an explicit counterexample to that insurance claim.

## Charge handling only on awarded rows

Now add a known **common handling burden of 20 credits**, paid once per lot only if we receive it. Both bidders would face that same burden if awarded; the gross common value remains V. The catalogue, information and rival bids are unchanged. Abstention and losses still cost zero.

For a winning first-price row, profit becomes V − b − 20; for a winning second-price row, V − R − 20. A losing row remains zero. If a candidate wins with probability p, its expected profit falls by **20p**, not automatically by 20.

Try the lowest positive first-price candidate: its sole award yields 80 − 75 − 20 = **−15**. Across the whole catalogue, that is −15/3 = **−5**. For second price, the same award yields 80 − 70 − 20 = **−10**, with expected profit −10/3. The higher conditional gross value and the format label cannot pay the handling bill for us.

Before opening the full solution, recompute the other candidates from the earlier tables and compare them with abstention.

### When zero wins can be the right choice

Never winning is not enough evidence of excessive shading. You may be refusing negative-value opportunities. It can also mean missing profitable ones; investigate the foregone alternatives rather than prescribing a target win rate.

For example, at second-price 75 with the handling burden, the prior net value after handling is 120 − 20 = 100. But conditional on its only winning state the net value before payment is 80 − 20 = 60, below the payment of 70. Ignoring selection would hide this loss. This is an explicit common-value second-price loss under the given rules, not a claim that every second-price winner loses.

## Transfer the method to unequal weights

A different invented catalogue tests whether you learned a denominator or merely memorized “divide by three.” There are now two possible states, with weights three and one. These are assumed probabilities of three quarters and one quarter, not four observed sales. Gross value and rival bid are still common and fixed jointly given the new information.

| State | Gross value | Rival bid | Weight |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Lower | 50 | 40 | 3 |
| Higher | 110 | 100 | 1 |

Use **first price**, no extra cost, lost ties and menu **0, 45, 105**. The prior mean is (3 × 50 + 1 × 110)/4 = **65**. Bid 45 wins only the lower state: conditional gross value is (3 × 50)/3 = 50 and conditional profit is 5. Overall expected profit is (3 × 5 + 1 × 0)/4 = **15/4**, or 3.75 credits.

Now compare 105 with abstention and 45. Keep the larger weight attached to the lower state even when both states win.

## State what the calculation can and cannot decide

**An auditable decision memo** for the initial model reads: “Given I, the three joint states have equal probability. We lose ties and pay our own bid only if awarded. Of the allowed candidates, 75 has the largest expected net profit, 5/3 credits, while abstention gives zero. The selected gross value is 80 rather than the earlier mean 120, yet payment is only 75. This is not an equilibrium or a guarantee. We would recompute if weights, costs, rival behavior, information or available bids changed.”

That memo also names the limits:

- **Menu limit.** A best candidate need not be best among all real-valued bids. If the menu were expanded to include 71, it would win the low state and yield (80 − 71)/3 = 3, above 5/3. We did not include it in the original choice set.
- **Strategic limit.** The rival bid is a state-dependent input held fixed, not a strategy we derived. An **equilibrium** requires mutually consistent responses by the players; accounting for our alternatives alone does not establish one.
- **Information limit.** The catalogue needs the relationship between value and rival behavior. Learning about a different signal, selection process or dependence can change the winning conditional value. It need not always move downward.
- **Objective limit.** Expected credits are the stated criterion. A binding budget, loss constraint, risk aversion, entry fee or alternative use of resources would require another account.

The [Royal Swedish Academy's explanation](https://www.kva.se/en/news/ekonomipriset-2020-2/) places common-value bidding in its information setting: bidders consider both what they know and what they believe others know. It does not supply a universal discount percentage. The calculations here follow our invented catalogue, not an empirical law that every winner overpays.

## Reconstruct the audit from memory

First specify what is known. Then, for each candidate, filter the winning states, apply the payment and cost rules, and average over the right cases. Compare complete opportunity values with the outside option before choosing. If the winning probability is zero, keep its conditional means undefined and compute its overall value directly.
