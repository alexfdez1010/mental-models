The winner’s curse asks you to check selection before deciding what to pay. Its limits matter just as much: winning does not prove a loss, a format is not insurance, and a low win rate does not diagnose a bad policy. Keep those distinctions before turning to the complete catalogue workshop.

## Limit one — distinguish common and private value

The relevant distinction is whether rivals’ information can change the value you should assign to the object. In a pure **known private-value** case, your value is stipulated independently of their information. A different taste or lower rival bid is not a shared truth that makes your own value wrong.

For example, if a painting is worth a known personal amount to you solely for enjoyment, another collector’s lower bid does not establish that you overvalued it. You can still pay more than your own value, misjudge future enjoyment or face other costs. Those possibilities are not ruled out by the label “private value”; they are different from inferring shared value from others’ information. Real purchases can mix private and common components.

## Limit two — separate selection from a bidding mistake

The **information effect** is that winning can change the relevant distribution of value. A bidder can anticipate that effect before choosing. The **money-losing mistake** is failing to account for relevant selection, payment and costs when comparing available actions. Calling both things “the curse” must not turn every conditional revision into an unavoidable loss.

An **equilibrium** describes mutually consistent strategies under specified information and preferences, not a badge attached to experienced bidders. A policy that correctly compares expected net profit with a zero outside option need not choose a negative-expectation bid, but a realized loss can still occur in a model with uncertain outcomes. Neither experience nor subtracting a generic offset proves that the policy was correct. [Thaler’s abstract](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.2.1.191) explicitly treats solving for bids as difficult and actual bidder behavior as an empirical question.

Correct accounting can change which bid is preferred. It does not remove all uncertainty, prove that a professional is at equilibrium or guarantee that an individual award profits.

## Limit three — the payment rule is not protection by itself

First price pays our own bid when we receive the lot; second price pays the highest rival bid. Changing that rule changes payment. It does not automatically remove common-value selection or additional costs. The familiar truth-telling result for known private values does not establish a dominant bid equal to an earlier common-value estimate.

An ascending process may reveal useful information, depending on what actions mean under the model. It can also change strategies and participation. Specify what is learned and how it changes the conditional catalogue; do not prescribe a universally lighter discount from the format name alone. The next workshop holds rival behavior fixed to isolate payment, and labels that restriction.

## Limit four — compare missed opportunities with abstention

**Over-shading** means choosing too low a bid relative to a better feasible policy under the stated objective. To diagnose it, you need information about the opportunities and alternatives, not merely a long run of losses or abstentions. You could be missing positive-value awards; you could instead be rejecting opportunities that all have negative expected net profit.

If a feasible bid beats abstention after selection, payment and costs, declining it can forgo value. If every feasible positive bid is worse than the outside option, abstaining can be the best action. The objective is not a prescribed number of wins or a conditional break-even margin alone: compare expected value per opportunity and respect any other constraints.

## Limit five — an award does not prove a policy

A selection effect concerns a conditional distribution, not a guaranteed individual path. A profitable award does not prove the bidding policy was optimal, and a disappointing one does not by itself prove it ignored information. Also, a lower conditional gross value is not the same as negative net profit: compare it with payment and costs. In some models or for a bid that wins every state, winning need not lower the earlier conditional mean at all.

## Using it well — the whole model in one posture

Use the limits to build a comparison rather than a universal discount:

1. Specify the value, own information and the relationship with rival behavior.
2. Rebuild the winning event for each candidate, including tie and abstention rules.
3. Calculate payment and applicable costs separately from gross value.
4. Use winning weights for conditional means and all opportunity weights for expected profit.
5. Compare feasible alternatives, then state what a model change would invalidate.
