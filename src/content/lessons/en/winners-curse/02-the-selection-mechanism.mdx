Put four estimates on a desk and take their average. Now put the same estimates on the desk and keep only the largest. The inputs did not change; the operation did. An auction that mechanically selects the largest estimate does not average estimation errors away.

This lesson rebuilds that selection mechanism with a complete finite model. It also separates three questions: how estimates err before selection, what the selected estimate looks like, and what a bidder can infer about value. The **values, error laws and credits below are invented teaching inputs**, not auction observations or bidding recommendations.

## Mean-zero is a weighted claim

An **estimation error** is estimate minus true value. An estimate is **unbiased** in a stated model when its expected error is zero. Expected means probability-weighted average over the model's possible results, not a promise about one result.

A balanced account need not contain equal numbers of positive and negative entries. For a separate, asymmetric error model:

| Error | Probability | Contribution to mean error |
|---:|---:|---:|
| −2 | 1/3 | −2/3 |
| +1 | 2/3 | +2/3 |

The mean error is $(-2)(1/3)+(1)(2/3)=0$. Yet the probability of a positive error is $2/3$, not $1/2$. This estimator is unbiased without being symmetric or equally likely to err in either direction. Large negative errors can balance more frequent small positive errors.

**Symmetry** would impose more: errors and their reflections have the same distribution. Even symmetry alone would not establish independence between different bidders. **Independence** means that learning one error does not change the probabilities for the other, within the stated model. A **marginal distribution** describes one error alone; the joint distribution describes several together. Keep those two descriptions separate.

### When to use the definition

Use “unbiased” only with a named target and averaging model. Before conditioning on an auction result, specify the signals, their dependence, how bids use them and how a winner is selected. Unbiasedness alone does not supply that whole model.

## Enumerate what the maximum selects

<MCQ client:visible
  question={"Two bidders mechanically bid their estimates. The higher bid wins and pays its bid. Which operation determines the winning estimate?"}
  options={[{"text":"The maximum of the pair, retaining the joint probabilities.","correct":true},{"text":"The average of the pair, canceling errors within each auction."},{"text":"The earlier mean estimate, ignoring both realized errors."},{"text":"The true value, replacing each estimate before selection."}]}
  explanation={"The winner is chosen by rank under this imposed rule. To average its error, first compute the maximum in every possible pair."}
  pretest
  className={"not-prose"}
/>
Fix the common value at **100 credits for the analyst**. Write it as $V=100$. Each of two mechanical bidders has an estimate $S_i=100+\varepsilon_i$. Each error is either −10 or +10 with probability one half, independently of the other bidder. Thus each individual estimate has mean 100.

The bidders follow the stipulated rule **bid the raw estimate**. First price means the winner pays that bid. One lot is always awarded, losers pay nothing and there are no other costs. In a tie, an independent fair coin chooses the winner's identity; the tied estimate and payment are the same either way. We are analyzing this mechanism, not proving that mechanical bids are rational or optimal. No distribution of possible values V or bidder posterior has been supplied.

Four ordered error pairs are equally likely because $(1/2)(1/2)=1/4$ for each:

| Errors A, B | Probability | Highest estimate and payment | Winner profit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| −10, −10 | 1/4 | 90 | +10 |
| −10, +10 | 1/4 | 110 | −10 |
| +10, −10 | 1/4 | 110 | −10 |
| +10, +10 | 1/4 | 110 | −10 |

Let $M=\max(S_A,S_B)$, the highest estimate. Its error is −10 once and +10 three times. Therefore:

$$E[M]=\frac{90+110+110+110}{4}=105,$$

$$E[M-100]=5,\qquad E[100-M]=-5.$$

The winner's average payment exceeds value by 5; the average winner profit is −5. But the first row has a **positive profit of 10**. “Loss on average” is not “loss in every case.” Ties are part of the sample space, not rows to discard.

For contrast, the mean of the two estimates in the four rows is 90, 100, 100 and 110. Its expectation is $(90+100+100+110)/4=100$. Selecting the maximum and averaging the estimates answer different questions.

## Keep selected error separate from value

<MCQ client:visible
  question={"In the model that fixes the common value at one hundred, does learning that a particular bidder won change that fixed value?"}
  options={[{"text":"No; it can change the distribution of selected errors, not the fixed reference.","correct":true},{"text":"Yes; it lowers the reference by the mean winning error in every auction."},{"text":"Yes; it makes the reference equal the realized highest estimate."},{"text":"No; therefore selection cannot affect any payment or profit."}]}
  explanation={"A constant remains the same constant after conditioning on an event with positive probability. Estimates, payments and errors can still have different conditional distributions."}
  pretest
  className={"not-prose"}
/>
The selected estimate has mean 105. The object in this particular model still has value 100. These are different quantities: a random estimate and a fixed value. Since V is fixed here, $E[V]=100$ and $E[V\mid W]=100$ for a winning event W with positive probability. The upward selected error is **not** a proof that a random value's conditional mean has fallen.

An observed estimate is also different from an expected value. If A's observed estimate is 110 in this fixed-reference calculation, its error is 10. The assertion “A's method has mean error zero” does not turn that observed 110 into $E[V]$.

A genuine bidder inference such as $E[V\mid I,W_b]$ needs a model for uncertain V and its relationship with the available information I, rival behavior and the candidate's winning event $W_b$. It can differ from $E[V\mid I]$. We have not specified those value probabilities in this fixed-reference experiment, so we have not derived a Bayesian valuation or an optimal bid. The [MIT auction lecture](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-271-industrial-organization-i-fall-2022/mit14_271_f22_lec22slides.pdf) keeps common-value information and bidding strategies explicit; our four-pair construction isolates selection instead.

<details>
<summary>Check a particular bidder’s winning error</summary>

Fair tie-breaking makes A win the two-negative pair with probability $1/8$, the A-positive/B-negative pair with probability $1/4$, and the two-positive pair with probability $1/8$. A never wins the A-negative/B-positive pair. Thus $P(W_A)=1/2$, and:

$$E[\varepsilon_A\mid W_A]=\frac{(-10)(1/8)+(10)(1/4)+(10)(1/8)}{1/2}=5.$$

That conditional average matches the population winner's mean error **under these symmetric joint and tie assumptions**. It is not the error conditional on an already observed estimate, nor a formula for a bidder's value posterior in a different information model.

The −5 profit figure averages one winner per auction. For A, mean profit conditional on an award is also −5 here. A wins half the opportunities, so its mean profit per opportunity is $(1/2)(-5)=-2.5$; its non-awarded rows contribute zero.

</details>

## Follow a fresh measurement

<MCQ client:visible

  options={[{"text":"Its mean returns to the reference; a realized measurement can still rise.","correct":true},{"text":"Its realized value must fall; fresh error cannot repeat a positive result."},{"text":"Its mean stays at the earlier maximum; the reference was selected upward."},{"text":"Its realized value equals the reference; unbiasedness removes all noise."}]}

Return to the finite case. After selecting M, take a fresh measurement $Y=100+\eta$, where $\eta$ is again −10 or +10 with probability one half, independent of both earlier errors. The reference does not change.

The old maximum is 90 with probability $1/4$ and 110 with probability $3/4$. Combine each possibility with the fresh coin flip:

| Old maximum M | Fresh measurement Y | Joint probability | Change Y − M |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 90 | 90 | 1/8 | 0 |
| 90 | 110 | 1/8 | +20 |
| 110 | 90 | 3/8 | −20 |
| 110 | 110 | 3/8 | 0 |

The probabilities sum to one. The new mean is $E[Y]=100$, so the expected change is $100-105=-5$. You can also calculate $20(1/8)-20(3/8)=-5$. Yet Y exceeds M with probability $1/8$ and equals it with probability $1/2$.

## Extend to independent uniform errors

<MCQ client:visible
  question={"Replace the binary errors with a continuous uniform distribution. Can the old four-row average be reused unchanged?"}
  options={[{"text":"No; derive the maximum under the new distribution and dependence assumptions.","correct":true},{"text":"Yes; every unbiased distribution has the same expected maximum error."},{"text":"Yes; symmetry fixes the maximum’s mean without the number of draws."},{"text":"No; continuous errors prevent any exact expected-maximum formula."}]}
  explanation={"The finite table was tied to its error law. A new law requires a new calculation; continuous distributions can still have exact expectations."}
  pretest
  className={"not-prose"}
/>
Now take N independent, identically distributed (iid) errors, each **uniform on $[-a,a]$**. Uniform means probability is proportional to interval length; the endpoints bound each error. “Identically distributed” means the same law is used for every bidder. Let N be a positive integer and $a\geq0$, with the same fixed V and mechanical raw-estimate selection.

For these conditions, the exact formula is:

$$E[\max_i\varepsilon_i]=a\frac{N-1}{N+1},\qquad E[M]=V+a\frac{N-1}{N+1}.$$

For V = 100 and a = 30, all amounts below are credits. Approximate columns round to two decimals; fractions stay exact.

| N | Exact mean winning error | Approximate error | Exact mean highest estimate | Approximate estimate |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0 | 0.00 | 100 | 100.00 |
| 2 | 10 | 10.00 | 110 | 110.00 |
| 4 | 18 | 18.00 | 118 | 118.00 |
| 6 | 150/7 | 21.43 | 850/7 | 121.43 |
| 10 | 270/11 | 24.55 | 1370/11 | 124.55 |
| 20 | 190/7 | 27.14 | 890/7 | 127.14 |

For example, with six bidders the error is $30(6-1)/(6+1)=150/7$. Add the reference: $100+150/7=850/7$. With twenty bidders it is $30(19)/21=190/7$, not 29. With ten it is $270/11$, not exactly 25. For positive a and finite N the expected error is below a, approaching that ceiling as N grows.

<details>
<summary>Optional derivation using cumulative probability and area</summary>

Assume $a>0$; zero noise gives zero error directly. Write $Z=\max_i\varepsilon_i$. For a cutoff $t\in[-a,a]$, a uniform error is at most t with probability $(t+a)/(2a)$. The maximum is at most t exactly when every error is. Independence gives:

$$P(Z\leq t)=\left(\frac{t+a}{2a}\right)^N.$$

This is a **cumulative probability**: the chance that a variable is at or below a cutoff. Set $H=Z+a$, so H lies between zero and $2a$. A nonnegative bounded variable's expectation is the area under its exceedance probability, $P(H>y)$. Integrating that area gives:

$$E[H]=\int_0^{2a}\left[1-\left(\frac{y}{2a}\right)^N\right]dy=2a-\frac{2a}{N+1}.$$

Subtract a to return to Z:

$$E[Z]=2a-\frac{2a}{N+1}-a=a\frac{N-1}{N+1}.$$

The proof uses elementary integration; the main table and exercises only require substitution into the stated formula. The formula is our derivation for this distribution, not an auction-equilibrium theorem from the cited lecture.

</details>

More N increases the expected maximum **under this unchanged iid law**, and increasing a scales its expected error. A fresh batch of fifty can move the other way by chance. The exact table, rather than a particular batch, establishes the expectation.

There is also a simpler pointwise fact: when you add an observation to the **same existing set**, its maximum cannot decrease. That fact does not require independence. The closed uniform formula does. Neither fact states that rational auction bids or realized averages from separate batches must move in one direction.

### What the shared offset does

The simulator can subtract $c=a(N-1)/(N+1)$ from **every** bid. The ranking is unchanged because $S_i-c>S_j-c$ exactly when $S_i>S_j$. It does not reduce a particular bidder's chance of winning by making only that bidder more cautious.

The resulting winning payment is $M-c$, with expectation $E[M]-c=V$. In the six-bidder example, $(850/7)-(150/7)=100$, so the ensemble's mean winner profit becomes zero under the imposed rule. This is a centering identity, **not an optimal strategy**, a best response or a guaranteed profit. Those claims require the bidder's information, rival strategies, payment and alternatives.

| Errors A, B | Probability |
|---|---:|
| −10, −10 | 1/2 |

Use the transfer to test whether you have a joint model or merely several separate error summaries. If bidders rely on the same source, knowing each source's accuracy in isolation does not determine the maximum's behavior. Specify how errors move together before drawing a selection conclusion.

## Use selection as a diagnostic

<MCQ client:visible
  question={"A real winner has the highest bid. What is the next useful step before treating that as evidence of overpayment?"}
  options={[{"text":"Specify how bids relate to signals, value, allocation and payment in that setting.","correct":true},{"text":"Subtract the uniform maximum-error formula regardless of the signal model."},{"text":"Reject the selected estimate as the least accurate observation by definition."},{"text":"Infer a loss because a high ranking directly determines the realized value."}]}
  explanation={"A rank is not a full value model. Bids can reflect information, costs, private value and strategy; profit also depends on payment and applicable costs."}
  pretest
  className={"not-prose"}
/>
A usable diagnosis names the object being averaged and the information held fixed:

1. State the value model: fixed reference for a selection experiment, or uncertain V for a bidder's valuation problem.
2. Specify the joint signal/error law and how bids use it, including ties and participation.
3. Compute the selected statistic under that rule. Do not replace a maximum with a mean.
4. Apply the actual payment and cost rules before discussing profit.
5. Check which model inputs changed before carrying an offset or inference elsewhere.

In the finite experiment, “mean winner profit −5” describes the imposed raw-bid mechanism. It does not choose a bid or prove that a rational bidder must accept a negative expected profit. [Thaler's abstract](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.2.1.191) distinguishes the difficulty of solving a common-value bid from an empirical claim about actual behavior.

The tempting rule “trust the winning estimate least” is too broad. The highest estimate is the most optimistic **under a shared-reference raw-signal rule**; accuracy rankings can differ if information quality, private value or bidding strategies differ. Selection prompts an audit, not automatic distrust of every winner.
