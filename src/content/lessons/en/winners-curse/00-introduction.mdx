Imagine a fictional jar holding a hundred dollars, with bidders estimating its contents imperfectly. If each bids that estimate, selecting the highest offer can select an unusually high error. The auction does not select the most accurate estimate by design.

The **winner's curse** is the risk of overlooking what winning tells you about an uncertain common value when deciding what to pay. Winning can revise the relevant value downward. Whether it produces a loss still depends on the bid, payment rule, costs and information. A well-chosen bid can profit; winning alone does not prove overpayment.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The one-sentence version">
Before choosing a common-value bid, ask which cases it wins, what those cases are worth and what you would pay. Compare the resulting expected profit with a feasible alternative, including abstention.
</Callout>

## See it in one machine

This simulator illustrates one imposed rule: independent uniform estimation errors around a fixed reference value, followed by selecting the highest estimate. The numbers are invented. A short random batch need not match its expectation.

Its shared shading offset changes every bidder's payment by the same amount without deriving a best response. Treat it as a demonstration of the expected maximum, not an equilibrium calculation or proof of how much to bid in another auction.

## What you'll walk away with

The course builds a distinction between value and price, examines selection, considers bidding adjustments and applications, then tests the limits before a worked audit:

1. **Common value vs private value** — distinguish shared uncertainty from a known personal value and recognize mixed cases.
2. **The selection mechanism** — explore how selecting an extreme can differ from averaging estimates, while keeping the model assumptions visible.
3. **Shading your bid** — consider winning information and the payment format before choosing an offer; a discount needs a model rather than a universal percentage.
4. **Where it bites in the wild** — examine possible applications in acquisitions, allocations and other competitive settings, where context and alternative explanations matter.
5. **Where the model lies** — distinguish selection from guaranteed losses, correct strategy from professional status, and missed profitable bids from justified abstention.
6. **[Audit a Winning Bid](/winners-curse/06-audit-a-winning-bid)** — rebuild the winning set for every candidate, separate payment and costs, and compare expected opportunity profit with taking no lot.

## How to use this course

Every lesson opens with a quick guess, teaches the idea through a concrete story and
worked numbers, and checks that it stuck. Don't skip the guesses — trying to answer
*before* you know is one of the most reliable ways to actually remember. You'll drive
the auction simulator from several angles and meet a few sorting and matching
exercises along the way.

This is an **expert-tier** course, so it leans on a few models you've ideally met
already. You'll get far more from it if you're comfortable with **thinking in
probabilities** (weights, conditions and expected values),
**regression to the mean** (extreme measurements tend to be followed by more
ordinary ones, because the extreme was partly luck), and **mechanism design** (how
the *rules* of an auction shape how people bid). When you've finished the six
teaching lessons, a graded final exam pulls it all together — it's one-way, so once
you submit an answer it's locked.

<Callout variant="warning" title="A habit to build">
Before submitting a bid, ask: “What does this bid win, what would I pay in those states, and how does the overall result compare with my alternatives?” The objective is a justified decision, not a required number of wins.
</Callout>
