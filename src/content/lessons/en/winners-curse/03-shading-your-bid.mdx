A discount is easy to announce. Choosing the bid that best serves a stated objective takes more work. **Bid shading** means bidding below a value reference; it is not a universal percentage or proof that the remaining offer is profitable.

Here you will compare every allowed whole-credit bid in a small constructed auction. The task is to choose before the uncertain state is known, not to inspect the outcome and then pick a price. Later you will change the permitted increment and the catalogue itself.

## Fix the information and the allowed actions

A sealed lot has an uncertain common gross value $V$: the same value to either bidder before payment. Our information $I$ is the complete hypothetical joint catalogue below. The actual row is unknown before bidding. Its relative weights define probabilities after normalization; they are not counts from three observed auctions.

All value, bids and profit use fictional **credits**, abbreviated **cr**. We prefer the largest expected net credit amount, with linear utility and no extra risk or budget constraint.

| State given our information | Gross value V in cr | Highest fixed rival bid R in cr | Probability weight |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Low | 80 | 70 | 1 |
| Middle | 120 | 110 | 1 |
| High | 160 | 150 | 1 |

Each row has probability $1/3$, so $E[V\mid I]=(80+120+160)/3=120$ cr. This is a value assessment conditional on our information, not an assumption that an observed signal is unbiased.

We may submit any **integer** bid from $0$ through $200$ cr. A zero bid means abstention: no lot, no payment and zero profit for us. A positive bid $b$ wins only if $b>R$; **ties lose**. This is first price: if awarded the lot, we pay our own bid. Otherwise our profit is zero. There are no entry fees, reserves, handling costs or additional outcomes. The rival rule stays fixed across our candidate choices. A **best response** here is the permitted action with the highest expected net profit against that stipulated rival rule.

Try a bid of $95$ cr, below the earlier mean of $120$ cr.

The sole award has gross value $80$ cr and costs $95$ cr: net profit $80-95=-15$ cr. That award occurs with probability $1/3$, so expected profit per opportunity is $(-15)/3=-5$ cr. “Below our earlier mean” has not prevented an expected loss.

Use a complete catalogue when you want to expose what a proposed bid wins. Its joint rows matter: separate averages of value and rival bids would not recover their relationship. A real application would need evidence for that relationship and for the stated costs and constraints.

## Rebuild the event when the bid changes

A bid acts like a filter over the catalogue. Raising the filter can admit additional rows, while also increasing the amount paid on every row it wins. The analogy is limited: the rows here are stipulated possibilities, not objects whose values change when the filter moves.

Let $W_b=\{R<b\}$ be the award event for a positive bid. The net profit $\Pi_b$ is $V-b$ on that event and zero otherwise. With a positive award probability,

$$E[\Pi_b\mid I]=P(W_b\mid I)\left(E[V\mid I,W_b]-b\right).$$

The winning conditional mean averages only awarded rows. Expected profit per opportunity averages all rows, including unawarded rows with zero profit. If no row wins, the winning conditional mean is undefined; the opportunity profit is still zero. Compute it directly rather than inventing a conditional value of zero.

Compare these candidates:

| Bid in cr | Winning states | Award probability | Gross value if awarded in cr | Profit if awarded in cr | Expected profit per opportunity in cr |
| ---: | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0, abstain | None | 0 | — | — | 0 |
| 70 | None, low tie lost | 0 | — | — | 0 |
| 71 | Low | 1/3 | 80 | 9 | 3 |
| 80 | Low | 1/3 | 80 | 0 | 0 |
| 95 | Low | 1/3 | 80 | −15 | −5 |
| 111 | Low and middle | 2/3 | 100 | −11 | −22/3 |
| 151 | All | 1 | 120 | −31 | −31 |

At $71$, the winning margin is $80-71=9$, and $(1/3)\times9=3$ cr per opportunity. At $80$, the bid equals its own selected conditional gross value, leaving zero. That is break-even, **not** the best feasible result. A conditional-value adjustment alone has not solved the optimization.

At $111$, the selected gross mean is $(80+120)/2=100$, so the conditional margin is $100-111=-11$. Weighting by $2/3$ gives $-22/3$. The conditional mean now divides by two selected weights; the opportunity average still divides by three.

## Cover the whole integer grid

You do not need to inspect two hundred similar rows by eye. Between rival thresholds, the winning set stays fixed. Inside such a band, increasing the first-price bid raises payment without admitting a new state. Expected profit therefore decreases strictly on every band with a positive chance of an award.

The lower endpoint that actually **wins** is the candidate to check in each band. Lost ties make it the next permitted integer, not the rival amount itself.

| Admissible bids in cr | Winning set | Expected profit in cr | Best candidate in that band | Its expected profit in cr |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | Abstain | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 through 70 | None | 0 | Every bid in this band | 0 |
| 71 through 110 | Low | (80 − b)/3 | 71 | 3 |
| 111 through 150 | Low and middle | (200 − 2b)/3 | 111 | −22/3 |
| 151 through 200 | All | 120 − b | 151 | −31 |

For the middle band, add both winning profits and retain all three opportunity weights:

$$\frac{(80-b)+(120-b)+0}{3}=\frac{200-2b}{3}.$$

For the last band every state wins, giving $(80+120+160-3b)/3=120-b$. The bands and abstention cover all $201$ integer actions. Comparing their best values gives $3>0>-22/3>-31$, so **the unique best integer bid is $71$ cr**, with expected profit $3$ cr per opportunity under this model.

Do not read “decreases within a band” as “decreases everywhere.” A move from $110$ to $111$ admits the middle state: expected profit jumps from $-10$ to $-22/3$, an increase. The jump remains below the global maximum. You must compare the starts of all winning bands, not assume the first threshold always wins.

A later workshop uses the same reference catalogue but the narrower menu $\{0,75,95,120,155\}$. Its best first-price candidate is $75$, with $(80-75)/3=5/3$ cr. It does not allow $71$. Both conclusions are correct for their different comparison sets; the later exam's finite-menu question keeps that narrower menu.

Use interval reasoning when the payment rule and winning set make profit simple between thresholds. Entry costs, alternative tie rules or rival responses can change that structure.

## Specify precision before claiming a maximum

A staircase has a first step above a mark. A continuous ramp has no first point strictly above that mark. This analogy concerns the set of permitted actions, not a physical claim about auction systems.

A **maximum** is a payoff achieved by an admissible action that does at least as well as every alternative. A **supremum** is the smallest value that no possible payoff exceeds; an action need not achieve it. A nonempty finite menu has a maximum. Changing the permitted precision changes that menu.

For the same reference catalogue and strict first-price rule:

| Permitted increments | Lowest winning bid above the low rival in cr | Best expected profit in cr |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Whole credit | 71 | 3 |
| Half credit | 70.5 | 19/6 |
| Quarter credit | 70.25 | 13/4 |

The half-credit result is $(80-70.5)/3=19/6$. The quarter-credit result is $(80-70.25)/3=13/4$. In each finite grid, the other winning bands still give negative values and no-award actions give zero, so these are its global maxima.

Now allow arbitrary real bids in $[0,200]$. On the low-only winning interval $70<b\le110$, write $b=70+\varepsilon$, where $0<\varepsilon\le40$:

$$E[\Pi_b\mid I]=\frac{10-\varepsilon}{3}<\frac{10}{3}.$$

As positive $\varepsilon$ approaches zero, profit approaches $10/3$. But a bid of exactly $70$ loses the tie and gives zero. It does not attain that limiting payoff.

Every low-only winning bid has a smaller winning alternative: $(70+b)/2$ remains strictly above $70$ and lowers payment. The other winning intervals give negative payoffs; no-award actions give zero. Therefore $10/3$ is the global supremum, **with no maximizing real bid** in this constructed model. The missing maximum comes from the strict allocation jump, not from failing to search hard enough.

Use this boundary to ask what precision, tie-breaking and submission rules actually permit. It is a mathematical reason to specify the protocol before naming an optimum, not a recipe for submitting infinitesimal real-world offers.

## Transfer to different values and weights

Return to the original integer grid, first-price payment, lost ties and zero outside option. In a separate constructed problem, our information $J$ specifies a different catalogue:

| State given the new information | Gross value in cr | Highest fixed rival bid in cr | Probability weight |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Lower | 70 | 60 | 1 |
| Higher | 130 | 90 | 3 |

The lower row has probability $1/4$ and the higher $3/4$. The earlier mean is $(70+3\times130)/4=115$ cr. These weights remain attached to their rows whether one state wins or both do.

Before opening the solution, compute the winning set and expected profit for bids $61$, $90$ and $91$. Then cover all integer bands and compare their maxima with abstention. Do not transplant the reference problem's choice of the lowest winning threshold.

$$\frac{(70-91)+3(130-91)}{4}=\frac{-21+117}{4}=24.$$

The complete bands are:

| Admissible bids in cr | Expected profit in cr | Best candidate in band | Its expected profit in cr |

Within each positive-award band profit decreases. Their best values are $9/4$ and $24$, with zero outside. The unique best integer bid is therefore $91$, not the first winning threshold. Crossing that second threshold adds a heavily weighted valuable state, so the increase in expected value outweighs the higher own payment. It still loses $21$ cr if the lower state occurs: maximizing expected profit does not promise a profit on every award.

## Keep the strategy claim within its model

A common subtraction from every bidder is a different exercise from choosing our bid while rival bids stay fixed. For example, subtracting ten from offers of eighty and ninety gives seventy and eighty: the ranking is unchanged. In general, $p_i-c>p_j-c$ exactly when $p_i>p_j$. Such a shared offset does not establish that one bidder wins less often or that its bid is a best response.

Nor does more competition determine a universal discount direction. A changed rival field requires a new joint account of information, rival behavior and payment. An **equilibrium** requires mutually consistent choices by the bidders; optimizing only our action against an imposed rival rule does not establish one.

The private-value second-price truth-telling result has its own conditions: a known personal value and a payment fixed by rival bids on an awarded state. It does not establish that bidding an earlier common-value mean is optimal. [MIT's auction lecture](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-271-industrial-organization-i-fall-2022/mit14_271_f22_lec22slides.pdf) separates these information and format settings. The [Royal Swedish Academy's account](https://www.kva.se/en/news/ekonomipriset-2020-2/) likewise places bidding in the context of what each bidder knows and believes others know. Neither source supplies this invented catalogue or its grid optimum.

Finally, zero awards are not intrinsically a failure. In our reference model abstention forgoes a better feasible action. If every feasible positive-award action instead had lower expected net value than the outside option, declining those awards could be best. A win count alone does not tell you which comparison applies.
