A new platform gives you more each period. The migration fee fits on one line. Before saying “we're trapped” or “obviously switch”, put a date on the comparison. A moving van is paid once per move; cheaper rent arrives month by month. Comparing the two without saying how long you'll stay leaves the decision unfinished.

This workshop prices one organisation's continuation from an inherited platform. It does not model a whole market or a group that must coordinate. Every number below is constructed, and every future benefit is known before the plan is chosen.

## Set the future ledger

A **continuation plan** specifies the platform used in every remaining period. A **horizon** is the number of periods included in that comparison. For two periods, the complete list is AA, AB, BA and BB. The letters name period choices; the platform already installed is recorded separately.

Imagine a small print workshop choosing a scheduling platform. Its planning scale is **credits**, abbreviated **cr**: a common measure of benefit and cost for this fictional decision, not a currency or an estimate from a real business. A “period” is one equal planning interval. We do not mix daily benefits with annual fees.

| Input | Stipulated value or rule |
|---|---|
| Platform installed before period one | A in the main case |
| Benefit from A | 6 cr per period |
| Benefit from B | 9 cr per period |
| Horizon initially compared | 2 periods |
| Migration cost K | 7 cr **per change**, in either direction |
| Routine operating costs | Already included in each benefit figure |
| Timing | Change at a period boundary, then receive that period's benefit |
| Information | All period benefits and fees known before choosing |
| Feasible choices | Either platform in each period; no refusal, budget or availability restriction |
| Outside the model | Discounting, uncertainty, learning gains, external network effects and terminal resale value |

“Gross” below means **before migration**, not before every operating expense. Our objective is total future benefit minus every new migration cost. All plans end at the stated horizon; no plan receives an unlisted benefit afterwards.

The repeated fee models transactional work or disruption that must be paid again on returning. Some learning costs behave differently: once you know both tools, returning may require no new learning. [Farrell and Klemperer's survey, sections one and two](https://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/economics/papers/2006/w7/Farrell_KlempererWP.pdf), distinguishes these mechanisms. Do not reuse our fee rule for a case with a different cost structure.

### When to use this setup

Use a finite ledger when the remaining options, periods and costs can be stated. If benefits are forecasts, begin with explicit scenarios or an uncertainty model; this known-benefit calculation does not make a forecast certain.

## Count every transition

Treat the installed platform as the starting square on a board. You pay when you cross from one platform to the other, including the first step. Merely seeing a B somewhere in a plan does not tell you how many times you moved.

For installed A, **BA** follows A → B → A. It changes before the first period and again before the second: two fees. **AB** follows A → A → B: one fee. Both plans earn 6 + 9 = 15 cr before migration, but their costs differ.

**Future net = sum of chosen period benefits − K × number of platform changes**

| Plan, installed A | Gross benefit, cr | Changes | Migration cost, cr | Future net, cr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AA | 12 | 0 | 0 | 12 |
| AB | 15 | 1 | 7 | 8 |
| BA | 15 | 2 | 14 | 1 |
| BB | 18 | 1 | 7 | 11 |

AA is best among **all four feasible plans** in this model. B's higher per-period benefit is real; across only two periods, its extra 6 cr does not repay the initial 7 cr migration. Staying is a forward-looking result, with no appeal to an old purchase invoice.

Now change **only the installed platform to B**. Future benefit figures, fees and the two-period horizon remain identical.

| Plan, installed B | Gross benefit, cr | Changes | Migration cost, cr | Future net, cr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AA | 12 | 1 | 7 | 5 |
| AB | 15 | 2 | 14 | 1 |
| BA | 15 | 1 | 7 | 8 |
| BB | 18 | 0 | 0 | 18 |

BB now wins with 18 cr. The inherited state changes which future moves are costly. These are different starting states, so we have not held every present fact identical. Nor have we proved anything about which original purchase was best: that requires its original options and benefits.

### When this distinction matters

Use the transition trace when a pilot, rollback or later reversal is possible. A reversible move can still be expensive: being allowed to return does not erase the second migration fee. If actual return costs differ, replace the rule before calculating.

## Give benefits enough time

Guess before expanding the horizon: can one extra period make a switch worthwhile without improving B at all? Keep A installed and K = 7 cr. Both platforms now have **three known periods** at the same respective benefits.

| Three-period plan | Gross benefit, cr | Changes | Migration cost, cr | Future net, cr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AAA | 18 | 0 | 0 | 18 |
| AAB | 21 | 1 | 7 | 14 |
| ABA | 21 | 2 | 14 | 7 |
| ABB | 24 | 1 | 7 | 17 |
| BAA | 21 | 2 | 14 | 7 |
| BAB | 24 | 3 | 21 | 3 |
| BBA | 24 | 2 | 14 | 10 |
| BBB | 27 | 1 | 7 | 20 |

BBB now wins: 27 − 7 = 20 cr, compared with AAA's 18 cr. Every mixed plan is listed, so an unexamined “switch later” plan cannot be hiding above the winner.

For the particular comparison **switch immediately and remain on B** versus **remain on A**, constant period benefits give:

**Incremental net = T × (B benefit per period − A benefit per period) − K**

The extra benefit is 3 cr per period. Across two periods: 2 × 3 − 7 = **−1 cr**. Across three: 3 × 3 − 7 = **2 cr**. This expression compares those two constant-platform plans; the full table establishes which plan wins among every allowed continuation.

### Equality is a result too

With installed A, two periods and **K = 6 cr**, the four net values are AA **12**, AB **9**, BA **3**, BB **12**. AA and BB tie. The ledger should mark both; the calculation supplies no strict preference between them. An omitted inconvenience cannot be invoked to break the tie while claiming the model is unchanged.

Justify the horizon from the actual remaining use before comparing plans. Choosing a duration only after seeing which platform wins can bias the decision. Use this check whenever a one-time cost is compared with a recurring benefit.

A percentage improvement is not an exit rule. Its value depends on the base benefit, duration, transition costs and feasible alternatives. Even a modest improvement can accumulate enough benefit; a large one can fail to repay a sufficiently expensive transition.

## Keep the state, remove the invoice

The installed platform is useful information. “We once paid a large amount for it” is a different claim. A **sunk invoice** is already paid and unrecoverable under every continuation. Changing its amount while holding today's state and every future consequence fixed changes none of the four future nets.

| Past irrecoverable invoice, cr | Installed platform | Future AA net, cr | Future BB net, cr | Preferred two-period plan |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | A | 12 | 11 | AA |
| 2000 | A | 12 | 11 | AA |

If you subtract the past invoice from every plan for a wider accounting total, you subtract the **same constant** from each. For two plans X and Y, (future X − P) − (future Y − P) = future X − future Y. Neither the size of P nor the unpleasantness of remembering it changes that difference.

This is not an instruction to erase history. Past decisions may leave trained staff, usable equipment, contracts, refunds or deadlines that alter future benefits and costs. Those consequences belong in the current state. A refundable amount is not sunk; a fee not yet paid is not a past invoice. The test is whether a consequence differs between the choices still open.

### When to use the separation

Use it when “we've invested too much to leave” mixes an old payment with real future consequences. Keep the consequences and compare them. Do not charge the old invoice again as a new migration fee.

## Transfer to a changing payoff path

Keep installed A, **K = 7 cr** and **three periods**. Change only B's third-period benefit from 9 to **3 cr**. Everyone knows this schedule before choosing; there is no new information arriving halfway through the plan.

| Platform | First-period benefit, cr | Second-period benefit, cr | Third-period benefit, cr |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 6 | 6 | 6 |
| B | 9 | 9 | 3 |

Before opening the solution, choose the best continuation among all eight. In particular, test whether two good B periods repay moving there **and back**.

### When to repeat the calculation

Reopen the ledger when credible information changes a benefit, cost, feasible choice, installed state or horizon. If the real decision is uncertain, compare plausible schedules and the consequences of being wrong. Neither a sunk invoice nor a slogan about lock-in replaces that work.

## Audit the claim before scaling it up

The workshop's preferred plan depends on an inherited state and future frictions. That is one mechanism by which past commitments matter. It does **not** establish an increasing-returns adoption process, multiple market equilibria or the inferiority of a historical standard.

In a network case, your benefits may depend on who else switches. You would need to model their choices, compatibility and incentives as well. A private ledger does not establish a collective welfare gain: an exit payment to a vendor may be a private cost and a transfer to someone else. Keep the decision maker and objective explicit.

A usable audit records the installed state, the payoff schedule and horizon, every permitted transition and its cost, and the objective used to rank complete plans. Then state what observation would change an input. “B is nicer” leaves several of those boxes empty.
