A system can be hard to leave without being impossible to leave. The useful question is what must change: the benefit of the alternative, the cost of moving, the behaviour of other users, or the task itself.

This lesson revisits the escape routes and the evidence needed to diagnose a lock-in. Some earlier examples in the course describe the stronger claim of a harmful historical accident. Keep that claim separate from the broader facts that an installed choice can make switching costly, and that a persistent standard may still be good. The [next workshop](/path-dependence-and-lock-in/07-price-an-exit-over-time) calculates a complete private decision over time.

## External shocks change inputs

A flood can release a logjam by changing the forces holding it together. An **external shock** is an event originating outside the modelled process that changes its costs, benefits, constraints or available options. A new rule or a technological change can do that. “External” describes its origin; it does not guarantee a successful transition.

Consider a constructed supplier example. A new rule requires future equipment to support a common connector. That can reduce incompatibility among new purchases. It does not instantly replace old equipment, train its users or fund conversion. To judge the transition, specify which installed assets remain, when replacements occur and what users may actually choose.

**Pitfall:** a shock can strengthen an incumbent as well as weaken it. Gradual improvements can also change the exit calculation. Neither a discontinuity nor a sudden collapse is necessary for every exit.

### When to use this route

Use it to identify the particular input that changed. Recalculate the available plans; do not treat the word “shock” as a prediction.

## A useful advantage must repay the actual transition

A cheaper rental can justify paying for a moving van if the saving lasts long enough. No universal ratio tells you that it must be ten times better. Compare benefits and costs on a common scale and over an explicit horizon.

For one decision maker, a small recurring advantage may eventually exceed a one-time migration cost. A large advantage may still fail to repay an expensive transition or provide enough benefit over a short remaining horizon. Uncertainty and discounting change that comparison when present; they must be modelled rather than replaced by a slogan.

In a network case, the alternative's benefit also depends on whom you can interact with after moving. “Technically faster” does not measure the loss of compatibility. Nor does the description “ten times better” establish that other users will switch.

**Pitfall:** the QWERTY debate does not supply a universal numerical hurdle for migration. A disputed speed advantage cannot establish what every typist should do over every horizon. The next workshop shows a horizon reversal with an unchanged benefit gap.

### When to use this route

Use it when an improvement is offered as sufficient proof of adoption. Ask what future gain it creates for each affected chooser, what transition it requires and what assumptions support those gains.

## Coordination changes who must move

A group that wants to sit down in a crowded room may need a shared plan: sitting alone blocks your view. **Coordinated switching** aligns the actions of interdependent participants. It can involve compatible transition arrangements, commitments or a jointly executed schedule. Naming a date does not itself supply willingness, resources or compliance.

Here is a constructed **one-period** example, separate from the next workshop. Two teams each choose A or B. Each payoff is that team's **net benefit**, already including all its costs. Higher is preferred. The figures use arbitrary net-benefit units for this separate game. Both using A gives each 4; both using B gives each 7; a mismatch gives each 0. There is no additional fee to subtract.

Given the other team chooses A, staying on A gives 4 rather than 0. Given the other chooses B, using B gives 7 rather than 0. Thus **AA and BB are both pure Nash equilibria**: at either matched outcome, neither team improves by changing alone. Neither strategy is strictly dominant, because its ranking depends on the other team's action.

### When to use this route

Use it when benefits depend on other participants' actions. Check incentives and execution separately from the existence of a shared announcement. A private export fee can create switching friction even when no other user has to move.

## Sponsorship changes who pays

A newcomer may pay for data conversion, subsidise early users or fund compatible complements. **Sponsored switching** changes adopters' private costs or benefits by having a sponsor bear some of the burden. It can also help assemble participants whose actions depend on each other.

A subsidy does not have to cover every cost to change a decision: an adopter may already be near indifference. Conversely, even a large subsidy may leave an unattractive benefit schedule or an unresolved coordination problem. “Half-funded” tells you the fraction of a proposed budget, not whether a chooser's actual threshold was crossed.

For a network strategy, ask what sustains participation after support ends. Reaching a posited threshold can support continued adoption under the model's assumptions; changes in compatibility, prices, user needs or competing options can alter those assumptions. A temporary useful pilot is not automatically worthless merely because it does not take over a market.

**Pitfall:** an adopter's gain does not establish the sponsor's gain or a social gain. A subsidy is a transfer as well as an incentive; real conversion work consumes resources. Name whose objective and costs you are evaluating.

### When to use this route

Use it when a migration offer changes the cost borne by the chooser. Recompute the net comparison and test continued participation rather than declaring that any partial subsidy fails.

## A new task can change which commitments bind

You may need a new workspace without replacing your old one. A new device class, use case or compatible intermediary can let an alternative grow alongside installed tools. Call this **moving to a new layer**: identify the new task and test which inherited constraints carry over.

In a constructed organisation, a legacy system stores archival records while a separate tool handles new short projects. If the projects genuinely need no old integrations, the new tool may avoid those migration costs. If they must read the archive or report into the old system, compatibility and training still matter. “New layer” does not mean “no legacy” by definition.

**Pitfall:** success in the new task is not proof of displacement in the old task. Compatibility tools, partial adoption and coexistence can reduce switching burdens without a market tipping to one universal winner. The five routes in this lesson are useful diagnostic categories, not an exhaustive theorem about every possible transition.

### When to use this route

Use it when the proposed alternative serves a different task or can interoperate. State what is retained, what is replaced and which costs have actually disappeared.

## Test the historical claim separately

An installed tool can impose a private switching cost even without adoption feedback. The stronger **historical lock-in hypothesis** studied earlier asks how a sequence of choices helped produce a persistent outcome. An early chance event amplified through a specified feedback mechanism is one important case. If the claim is additionally that the result is **harmful or inferior**, that adds a separate burden of proof.

### Require a counterfactual for harm

“It's old and I dislike it” provides neither a better feasible alternative nor a comparison of transition costs. Specify **better for whom, on which objective, over what horizon and with what transition**. A from-scratch design comparison and an exit decision from today's installed state answer different questions.

The QWERTY debate remains a reason to demand evidence, not a licence to treat either inferiority or efficiency as automatic. Persistence alone cannot tell you that a superior alternative is absent. It can reflect current merit, migration costs, coordination or a combination.

### Identify what history changes

A geographical advantage alone does not demonstrate a contingent, amplified historical mechanism. It also does not rule out such a mechanism operating alongside geography. Inspect the proposed trigger, reinforcement, alternative history and evidence rather than declaring that every large cause excludes path dependence.

Nor must every historical claim prove that its winner is worse. A convention can depend on earlier coordination and still be useful. The empirical claim of harm must be earned separately from the mechanism.

### Check the friction before assuming permanence

No network effect is not the same as no switching cost. A standalone tool can still require data conversion, a contract payment or retraining. Conversely, a high adoption share does not prove switching friction or a perpetual monopoly. Test the actual obstacle and what could change it. General claims about how often all markets tip require evidence beyond one simulator or one case.

[Farrell and Klemperer](https://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/economics/papers/2006/w7/Farrell_KlempererWP.pdf) distinguish buyers' switching costs from network effects among users, and discuss cases with different competitive and efficiency consequences. Our constructed examples illustrate distinctions; they do not estimate the frequency of harmful lock-in.
