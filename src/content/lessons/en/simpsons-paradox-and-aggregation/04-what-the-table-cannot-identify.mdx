Suppose a manager asks: “Would these same documents have done better if all had used A?” That question changes the task. The completed table tells us what happened under the chosen routes. It does not contain each document’s result under the route it did not use.

The census contains two hundred documents, one hundred assigned to each route. A **potential outcome** in our constructed model is the binary success the same document would have under a stipulated route. Write them as $Y_i(A)$ and $Y_i(B)$ for document $i$. Only its chosen-route outcome is observed.

For the following logical examples, assume two fixed potential outcomes per document, no dependence on other documents’ route assignments and **consistency**: the chosen-route potential outcome equals the recorded result. These are declared conditions of the possible worlds. The observed count table has not established them for a real export system.

A causal comparison for this finite population would contrast the mean of all $Y_i(A)$ with the mean of all $Y_i(B)$, using the same two hundred documents in each hypothetical assignment. That is different from comparing the two groups who actually chose different routes.

## Complete the missing outcomes in two ways

Keep every observed MAIN record unchanged. In the first possible world, give every actual-B document an unseen A outcome of one and every actual-A document an unseen B outcome of zero. The records already observed under A still have forty-five successes; those under B still have seventy-five.

The first contrast is $(145-75)/200=7/20$, or thirty-five percentage points. The opposite completion gives $(45-175)/200=-13/20$, or minus sixty-five percentage points. Neither completion alters an observed routine or complex cell. Every recorded total and every standardized index from those cells is identical in both worlds.

The chart describes the two completed mathematical worlds. It does not display two empirical experiments. Their disagreement proves **non-identification from the supplied table**: those observations alone do not select one causal contrast. A larger observed table with the same missing-outcome issue would not fix the logical gap just by printing more digits.

### When a completion proof helps

Use it when someone claims that the observed table mathematically forces an intervention effect. You need only exhibit compatible alternatives to refute unique identification. The two extreme completions do not show which world is plausible, measure uncertainty or supply a recommendation for the real system.

## Audit how a selected subset was made

Here is a separate four-record example. Both routes have one success and one failure in the full record set. An audit rule deliberately retains A’s success and B’s failure.

The full fractions are one half for A and one half for B. The selected subset instead has A $1/1$ and B $0/1$. The unselected subset has A $0/1$ and B $1/1$. The denominator changed according to a rule that used both route and outcome. That explicit mechanism explains this constructed selection change; it is not a diagnosis of every real subgroup comparison.

It is also not the MAIN case’s strict within-every-stratum reversal: the selected and unselected strata point in opposite directions. Keeping these patterns distinct prevents “Simpson” from becoming a name for any inconvenient percentage.

## Ask what would justify the causal claim

For a real intervention question, specify the population, timing, assignment process, treatment versions and outcome. Ask whether potential outcomes can be related to assigned routes under a defensible design, what measured or unmeasured factors affect selection and success, and whether the relevant groups have support. Merely adjusting for every recorded variable is not a proof: a variable’s role and timing matter.

This course does not offer a universal rule to pool or stratify for all causal questions. Its arithmetic is an audit of what the supplied counts identify. A designed experiment or an observational analysis with justified causal assumptions is additional evidence, not a result generated by the common-reference selector.
