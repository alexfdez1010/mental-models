A price changes. You still have to decide what to buy, produce or substitute. The price does not contain the recipe for your product, your budget or the quality of the alternative in your storeroom. Those are part of what makes the same quotation mean different things to different users.

Hayek’s communication analogy concerns using dispersed knowledge. A shared quotation can help people fit local plans together without telling each person the whole distant story. We will examine that claim with a small constructed market, not a simulation that ranks institutions.

## Add the local information

A **price signal** is information conveyed by trading terms that can affect choices. It is **additional** information: a user combines it with what it already knows about its own situation. It need not reveal why other participants’ costs or uses changed. Whether that missing cause matters depends on the decision and its horizon.

Think of a delivery charge. To decide whether to collect an item yourself, you also need distance, time and transport. The charge alone does not select a sensible route. This analogy explains a local comparison; it does not explain how the charge was set.

Calling a quotation a **sufficient statistic** would require a separate, specified statistical claim. Our deterministic records assign no probabilities. Here the question is narrower: **which additional inputs does this particular decision require?**

### When the source of a change can matter

A one-session substitution may need only current terms and local methods. Planning a long-lived process can also depend on duration, delivery reliability or quality. Identify that decision before declaring the distant cause irrelevant. The workshop later in this course will put different methods behind the same price and make you do the comparison.

## Check a finite market quotation

For one constructed session, three sellers can each deliver one identical widget and three buyers can each use one. Values are maximum willingness **and ability** to pay; seller costs include opportunity cost. Everyone takes a posted quotation as given. Units are mutually compatible, budgets cover values and there are no additional costs or outside effects. Credits **cr** are fictional. Declining trade leaves zero gain.

A buyer wants a unit when its value exceeds the quotation; a seller offers when the quotation exceeds its cost. **Quantity demanded** and **quantity supplied** are those wanted and offered quantities at the same terms. A quotation that makes the plans compatible is a **clearing quotation** in this model. Equal counts do not alone document completed trades.

At **5 cr**, Z’s value of **6 cr** still exceeds the quote. All three buyers want a unit; only A offers. A would gain **5 − 4 = 1 cr**, not merely break even, if its sale were completed. At **8 cr**, X/Y and A/B form compatible two-unit plans. Strictly between **7 and 9 cr**, the same participants are willing, so the clearing quotation is not unique. Endpoints involve indifference and are excluded from this exercise.

### When this finite check is useful

Use a threshold table to test a given set of terms. Do not turn it into an unprovided process: nothing here says who changes a quote, how quickly that happens or whether an actual negotiation converges.

At **9.5 cr**, X wants one and B offers one. Quotations strictly between **9 and 10 cr** support that same strict plan. Y compares the higher quotation with its own value of **9 cr**; it does not need A’s cost report for that stated one-unit choice.

### When not to infer the whole event

Y’s comparison uses its own value and the new quote. A mine report could matter to another user’s delivery forecast or technology. Neither Y’s limited information need nor our table proves that the cause is irrelevant to every decision.

## Separate equilibrium from information discovery

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  question={"An analyst has every value and cost in a small model and solves its clearing conditions. What has not yet been shown?"}
  options={[{"text":"How people with partial information actually discover and implement compatible terms","correct":true},{"text":"Whether a stated buyer value exceeds a stated quotation in the solved table"},{"text":"Whether the supplied input numbers can be compared inside the stated model"}]}
  explanation={"Solving conditions given all inputs and explaining a process with dispersed knowledge are different tasks."}
  pretest={true}
/>

A **static equilibrium check** asks whether given plans fit at stated terms. An **information and adjustment process** specifies who observes what, how they respond, how terms change and what is executed. The first does not substitute for the second.

This distinction matters in Hayek’s discussion of economic calculation. Lists of tonnes and hours do not by themselves provide the valuations and opportunity costs for a common net-benefit comparison. And even when an analyst is given all inputs, solving the model does not explain how participants with partial information coordinate. This lesson demonstrates finite checks; it does not settle a historical institutional contest.

In particular, a program that sorts every private cost and value can compute a toy allocation. That does not show that the numbers were discovered through local trade. If another program is instructed to hold a stale quotation fixed and allocate by a value-blind queue, their difference follows partly from those declared rules. It is not evidence that every actual institution must behave that way. We therefore use explicit records here rather than treating such a comparison as an experiment on real economies.

### When to ask for the process

Ask for it whenever a static diagram is used to promise rapid adaptation, truthful revelation or institutional superiority. A model can impose price-taking, complete records and a particular allocation rule; those assumptions are not observations of the system the diagram is meant to explain.

## Inspect what the signal leaves out

<MCQ
  client:visible
  className={"not-prose"}
  question={"A user has chosen the cheapest listed method at a quoted price. What can still be missing from an aggregate evaluation?"}
  options={[{"text":"Costs imposed outside the private decision and the objective used to compare outcomes","correct":true},{"text":"Nothing, because a private comparison automatically includes every affected person"},{"text":"Only a longer name for the quotation, without any additional records"}]}
  explanation={"Private costs and the accounting boundary need not coincide. An aggregate evaluation requires its own stated information and objective."}
  pretest={true}
/>

A price can change incentives without being a full report on its cause, the resources physically available or every effect on other people. An **externality** is a cost or benefit to someone outside the relevant private decision that is not accounted for there. If such an effect matters to your objective, extend the account rather than assuming that the quoted price contains it.

A fixed price, delayed information, market power or uncertain delivery can also change the relationship between quoted terms and decisions. These are possible mechanisms to investigate, not diagnoses you can read uniquely from a queue or a price spike. The same observed price can be compatible with more than one underlying record. The later workshop will make that ambiguity exact.

### Use prices as one input to an audit

Specify the choice, its alternatives and the assumptions under which a quote helps. Then check what you can infer from the report and what further records would change the answer. This retains the information insight while making its limits testable.
