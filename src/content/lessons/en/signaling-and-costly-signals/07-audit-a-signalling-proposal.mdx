A certificate looks convincing. That does not tell you whether anyone wants to earn it, whether the organiser should accept it, or whether the belief behind that decision fits the proposed behaviour. Those are three different questions. This workshop makes you check all three on one small, completely specified game.

## Write the whole game before judging the signal

Think of the proposal as a timetable: every stop needs an entry, including stops nobody expects to use. Unlike a train timetable, the passengers here choose routes in response to the rules. A missing response can change their choice.

An applicant is the **sender**: they know a private **type**, H (ready for this demonstration) or L (not ready). These labels concern one demonstration, not a person’s worth. The exhibition organiser is the **receiver**: it sees a certificate or its absence, then accepts or declines. A **strategy** specifies an action at every situation that player could face. A **belief** is the receiver’s probability of H after each observation. An **assessment** combines both strategies and those beliefs.

| Base-game rule | Stipulated value |
|---|---|
| Prior probability of H / L | 1/4 / 3/4 |
| Private value of acceptance, either type | 10 cr |
| Certificate cost, H / L | 2 cr / 12 cr |
| No-certificate cost | 0 cr |
| Organiser payoff from accepting H / L | +6 cr / −6 cr |
| Declining: payoff before any certificate cost | 0 cr for both players |

A credit (cr) is a fictional payoff unit, not a measured price. Certificate costs are paid even when declined; they are not a transfer to the organiser. The certificate is perfectly observed and does not improve readiness. Both choices are feasible; cost is not a budget constraint. Everyone knows the rules and maximises their own payoff in this single encounter. There is no reputation, other signal, refund, later encounter or enforcement benefit. We omit broader exhibition and social effects: this is an incentive audit, not a welfare ranking.

### When to use this first step

Before comparing signal costs, specify who observes what, every feasible action, and the payoff at every endpoint. Otherwise a hidden refund, a different acceptance benefit or an unspecified response can do the work you attributed to “credibility”.

## Condition on the observation the proposal produces

Sorting letters into trays changes the contents of each tray. A certificate is informative only insofar as the proposed choices put different mixes of types in its tray. The stamp’s price alone does not calculate that mix.

Represent the prior by a catalogue with four equally likely entries: H, L₁, L₂, L₃. The subscripts merely name three L entries. This is a stipulated probability law, not a claim that every real batch of four people contains exactly one H. In the formula, P(H,s) is the probability of both H and observation s.

$$q(s)=P(H\mid s)=\frac{P(H,s)}{P(s)},\qquad P(s)>0.$$

| Who certifies in the proposal | H among certificate entries | H among no-certificate entries |
|---|---|---|
| Neither type | No entries; undefined by Bayes | 1/4 |
| Only H | 1/1 | 0/3 |
| Only L | 0/3 | 1/1 |
| Both types | 1/4 | No entries; undefined by Bayes |

### When the calculation stops

An observation with positive probability is **on path**. An observation assigned zero probability is **off path**. An empty tray does not contain a zero proportion of H: the fraction has no denominator. We will need an additional belief to audit the response there.

## A belief still needs a decision rule

A weather forecast and a decision to hold an outdoor event are different objects. The decision also depends on what getting it wrong costs. Here the receiver’s **best response** is an action with the highest expected payoff at its stated belief.

Write q for the probability of H after the observation. Accepting gives

$$E_R(q)=6q-6(1-q)=12q-6\text{ cr}.$$

Declining gives zero. Accept if q is above 1/2; decline if below. At exactly 1/2 both actions, or any mixture, are best responses. We have not imposed a tie-break. Under separation, certificate means q = 1 and acceptance gives 6 cr; no certificate means q = 0 and acceptance would lose 6 cr. Under either pooling pattern, the observed signal leaves q = 1/4: accepting yields 1.5 − 4.5 = −3 cr, so declining is better.

### When to use the receiver check

Do it even when the sender story looks persuasive. “They are willing to certify” does not mean “the organiser should accept”. The loss from L and the reward from H belong in that second calculation.

## Hold the response fixed and test both deviations

A unilateral **deviation** changes one player’s choice while the other strategy stays fixed. You cannot rescue an unprofitable move by quietly awarding it a different response. The comparison is like changing one cell in a spreadsheet while locking the rule that calculates its consequences.

Audit proposal S: H certifies, L does not; accept certificates and decline no certificate; beliefs q(certificate) = 1 and q(none) = 0. Both observations occur, so Bayes supplies both beliefs. The previous section checked both receiver responses. Now compare the sender’s prescribed and alternative action, holding that receiver policy fixed.

| Type | Prescribed payoff | Payoff from switching signal | Profitable deviation? |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | 10 − 2 = 8 cr | 0 cr | No |
| L | 0 cr | 10 − 12 = −2 cr | No |

All three checks pass. We have verified this separating assessment in this game. We have not proved it unique or shown that a population will learn it. Spence’s lecture stresses that choices and receiver beliefs/rewards must fit together; the fixed prize alone is only part of that loop.

### Cheaper imitation breaks this proposal, not every possible one

Change only L’s certificate cost from 12 to 6 cr. Keep proposal S fixed. Its beliefs still follow Bayes and its receiver actions remain best responses, but L now gains 10 − 6 = 4 cr by certifying instead of zero. The sender check fails.

Two tempting replacements also require their own audits. If both types certify, a belief of certainty in H contradicts the on-path catalogue: q is 1/4 and accepting loses 3 cr. If both certify and the organiser declines both observations, each sender can avoid the certificate cost by dropping it. These are separate failed proposals, not a predicted adjustment sequence.

## An unseen signal needs an explicit belief

Return to the base costs. Suppose neither type certifies. No certificate is observed with certainty and leaves q = 1/4, so decline is optimal. What should the organiser do after a certificate that this proposal says never arrives? Bayes cannot divide by zero to answer.

| Same sender proposal: neither certifies | Belief after unseen certificate | Best receiver response there | Sender audit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assessment P | q = 0 | Decline | H: 0 vs −2; L: 0 vs −12. Neither gains. |
| Assessment P′ | q = 1 | Accept | H could gain 8 instead of 0. Fails. |

P passes the **weak perfect Bayesian equilibrium** check used here: on-path Bayes consistency, receiver best responses at every observation, and no profitable sender deviation. The off-path belief is an additional stipulated part of the assessment. P′ fails despite having the same proposed sender behaviour.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Passing this check has a boundary">
This does not recommend pessimistic beliefs about real applicants. Stronger equilibrium refinements or specified learning models can restrict off-path beliefs and exclude assessments that pass a weak check. Fudenberg and He study such restrictions under explicit learning assumptions. We have not applied their selection theorem to this constructed game.
</Callout>

### Equal costs can separate in a different game

Keep the base prior and receiver payoffs, but make the certificate cost 2 cr for **both** types. Now change private acceptance values to 10 cr for H and 1 cr for L. Under accept-certificate/decline-none, H gets 8 rather than 0, whereas L would get −1 rather than 0. On-path beliefs are still 1 and 0 and the receiver’s responses still fit. Separation is possible because benefits differ. This is a separate model: do not carry the cheaper-L cost of 6 into it.

## Transfer: a majority is not an acceptance threshold

New card, separate encounter. The prior is H with probability 3/5 and L with 2/5. Acceptance still gives the applicant 10 cr; certificate costs remain H = 2 and L = 12 cr, paid even when declined. The organiser now receives +6 cr for accepting H and **−18 cr** for accepting L; decline gives zero to both before certificate costs. All other base-game information and feasibility assumptions remain.

| Type | Signal | Accept: applicant, organiser | Decline: applicant, organiser |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | Certificate | (8, 6) | (−2, 0) |
| H | None | (10, 6) | (0, 0) |
| L | Certificate | (−2, −18) | (−12, 0) |
| L | None | (10, −18) | (0, 0) |

Audit two complete proposals before opening the solution. T: only H certifies; beliefs after certificate/none are 1/0; accept certificate and decline none. U: both types certify; beliefs after certificate/none are 3/5 and an explicitly stipulated off-path zero; accept certificate and decline none. In each case check beliefs, receiver choices and both sender deviations.

The receiver calculation is

$$6q-18(1-q)=24q-18\text{ cr}.$$

It equals zero at q = 3/4. Accept strictly above that threshold; decline strictly below; both are best responses at equality. In U, q = 3/5, so the organiser should decline: 3.6 − 7.2 = −3.6 cr. U also tells L to pay 12 for a reward of 10, giving −2 cr instead of declining without a certificate for zero. In T, acceptance after a certificate pays the organiser 6; decline after none avoids losing 18. H compares 8 with 0 and L compares 0 with −2. T passes. We checked two assessments, not every possible equilibrium.

### When to transfer the method

Use the checklist when a proposed signal changes a decision: an exhibition slot, a warranty offer, a qualification rule. Replace the fictional payoffs with justified values before using it in practice. Unknown costs, mixed behaviour, productive certificates and repeated encounters can change the answer. A posterior shift is not itself a decision benefit.

### Sources and boundaries

The constructed numerical game and all variants belong to this lesson. Spence’s [Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/spence-lecture.pdf), introduction and “The simplest job market signaling model”, motivates checking choices, beliefs and rewards together and warns against treating a stripped-down education model as an empirical claim about education. Fudenberg and He’s [Learning and Type Compatibility in Signaling Games](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.01819v3), introduction, toy example and signalling-game notation, explains the importance of off-path beliefs and how additional learning assumptions can restrict equilibrium predictions. Neither source supplies our fictional parameters or validates a real selection policy.
