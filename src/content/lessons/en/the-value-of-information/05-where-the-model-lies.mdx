A workshop compares buying a report with doing nothing. There is also a safe method the team could use today. The arithmetic may be flawless; the comparison has left an option out.

This lesson audits the **decision model**: the available actions, consequences, probabilities and timing used to calculate value. The examples use invented work-minute costs and explicitly stipulated probabilities. They describe expected work across the model's possible cases, not promised results from a real sample.

## Start from the policies you can actually use

A **policy** specifies what you will do with the information available at each point. “Use the standard method” is a fixed policy. “Use the jig after a favourable report, otherwise use the standard method” is a report-dependent policy. Your comparison needs the best feasible policy in each case, with the same objective and time horizon.

For actions $a$, states $s$ and report results $r$, the gross comparison is:

$$
V_0=\max_a\sum_s P(s)U(a,s)
$$

$$
V_{\text{report}}=\sum_r P(r)\max_a\sum_s P(s\mid r)U(a,s),\qquad EVSI=V_{\text{report}}-V_0
$$

Here $U$ is the payoff for one action in one state; the policy values average those payoffs. The jig uses work-minutes saved relative to the standard method. Later batch examples instead minimise expected additional work. The maximisation is over actions that are actually available. [Berkeley's decision-network derivation](https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/vpis/vpi.html) expresses the comparison in terms of expected utility; the payoff table and action menu determine what it means in a particular case.

### A missing alternative changes both sides

Return to the earlier jig model: suitability has probability **40%**, the jig saves **400 minutes** when suitable and adds **300 minutes** when unsuitable. The standard method has relative payoff zero. A report is correct **80% within each state**, and costs **30 minutes** of effort per project.

Now include a third option that had been omitted: a safe method saving **50 minutes in either state**. It remains available after a report. All options complete the same required work to the same quality; report effort is additional.

| Quantity | Only standard method and jig | Safe option also available |
|---|---|---|
| Best payoff without a report | 0 | 50 |
| Best gross report-policy payoff | 92 | 120 |
| Gross EVSI | 92 | 70 |
| Incremental net after 30 minutes of effort | 62 | 40 |

Without a report, the jig's expected payoff is **0.40 × 400 − 0.60 × 300 = −20**, so the safe option's 50 wins. A favourable report occurs with probability **0.40 × 0.80 + 0.60 × 0.20 = 0.44**. Choosing the jig in that branch contributes **0.32 × 400 − 0.12 × 300 = 92** to the full expected payoff. Choosing the safe option there would contribute only **0.44 × 50 = 22**.

The unfavourable branch has probability **0.56**. The safe option contributes **0.56 × 50 = 28**; the jig would contribute **0.08 × 400 − 0.48 × 300 = −112**. The optimal report policy therefore gives **92 + 28 = 120**. Its improvement over the best no-report policy is **120 − 50 = 70**, leaving **40 minutes** after report effort.

### When to check the menu

Do this before pricing a test or declaring information worthless. An irreversible launch decision might leave later maintenance, mitigation or replacement decisions open. Information arriving after launch has zero launch-choice benefit if that choice cannot change; its value for a remaining decision needs a separate comparison.

Likewise, a proxy is not irrelevant merely because it measures something other than the final outcome. It can inform that outcome through a defensible conditional relationship. If it changes neither the relevant beliefs nor any feasible payoff or action, it adds no benefit for this objective. Measuring the easy variable is a problem when its connection to the decision is missing, not because every indirect measurement is useless.

## Price a rare report against its consequences

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest={true}
  question={"A positive report points to a genuine rare fault in 9 of 108 model cases. Can that fact alone show that buying the report is a poor decision?"}
  options={[{"text":"Yes: more false positives than true positives makes any report too costly."},{"text":"No: compare the consequences, available responses and report costs.","correct":true},{"text":"Yes: an 8.33% posterior is below every rational action threshold."}]}

A workshop processes batches. A rare fault makes an unprotected batch require **10,000 extra work-minutes**. Preparing a fallback costs **100 work-minutes in either state** and prevents that extra work completely. Otherwise an unprotected batch adds no work. Both responses remain available after the report, and testing has no other effects. The objective is to minimise expected additional work per batch.

The model assigns a **1% fault probability**, **90% sensitivity** (a positive report given a fault) and **90% specificity** (a negative report given no fault). The following catalogue contains 1,000 equally likely hypothetical cases representing those joint probabilities:

| Model cases | Positive report | Negative report | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fault | 9 | 1 | 10 |
| No fault | 99 | 891 | 990 |
| Total | 108 | 892 | 1,000 |

A positive report gives fault probability **9 ÷ 108 = 1/12 ≈ 8.33%**. Most positives are false alarms, but preparing the fallback is justified when **fault probability × 10,000 > 100**: the threshold is **1%**. The positive crosses it. A negative gives **1 ÷ 892 ≈ 0.112%**, below the threshold.

Without a report, never preparing the fallback costs **0.01 × 10,000 = 100 minutes on average**. Always preparing it also costs 100; either fixed policy is optimal in this model. With the report, prepare only after a positive:

- Preparation contributes **108 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 10.8 minutes per batch**.

If each report requires **20 additional minutes**, the complete expected cost is **40.8 minutes** and the expected saving is **59.2 minutes**. At a report cost of **80 minutes**, the complete cost becomes 100.8 and the expected saving is **−0.8 minutes**. The very same information can be useful and overpriced.

### When the base rate matters

Use the prior to calculate each report's conditional probabilities, then apply the actual payoff threshold. A low posterior does not by itself establish a small EVSI. Here the avoidable loss is large relative to the fallback cost; another consequence table could make the same report unhelpful.

## Put delay on the affected branches

For this example use the original **two-action** jig model: standard method or jig, with no safe 50-minute alternative. Suitability is 40%, the report is correct 80% within each state, and report effort is 30 minutes. At the original timing, the report's gross value is 92 and its incremental net is **92 − 30 = 62 minutes**.

Now suppose waiting for the report loses a setup slot. Choosing the jig afterwards adds **150 work-minutes in either suitability state**. Using the standard method is unaffected. The revised jig payoffs are **400 − 150 = 250** when suitable and **−300 − 150 = −450** when unsuitable.

Recalculate the best response to each result:

| Report | Joint contribution if the jig is chosen | Contribution from standard method | Best response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Favourable | 0.32 × 250 − 0.12 × 450 = 26 | 0 | Jig |
| Unfavourable | 0.08 × 250 − 0.48 × 450 = −196 | 0 | Standard method |

The gross report-policy value falls to **26**, so the complete incremental net is **26 − 30 = −4 minutes**. A report that paid under the original timing no longer pays under these changed consequences. The best immediate no-report action still has payoff zero.

There is an equivalent accounting here. The jig is still chosen only after a favourable report, which has probability **0.44**. The expected payoff erosion is therefore **0.44 × 150 = 66 minutes**, not 150 on every project. Starting with the original gross gain gives **92 − 66 − 30 = −4**.

### When delay needs a new model

Put a consequence where it occurs. A fixed fee can be subtracted once; a missed slot, an expired alternative or a report that changes the system can require a new action menu, payoff table or transition model. Reoptimise the response after those changes before using a shortcut based on the old response policy.

The [National Academies discussion of decision uncertainty](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12568/chapter/7) treats data collection, delayed action and later decision revision as costs to consider. The calculations here are authored examples of that accounting, not measurements from its environmental cases.

## Test the inputs before trusting the total

A correct calculation can still use the wrong report model. Sensitivity measured on one population or operating process need not describe a different one. Record where each input came from, which cases it covers and what uncertainty remains.

Return to the rare-fault batch model. Keep the 1% prior, 10,000-minute unprotected fault loss, 100-minute fallback and 90% specificity. This time consider **60 minutes of report effort** under both candidate models. Compare the original 90% sensitivity assumption with an alternative 60% assumption:

| Candidate input model | Fault positives / missed faults | No-fault positives / negatives | Expected policy cost before report effort | Gross EVSI | Incremental net after 60 minutes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sensitivity 90% | 9 / 1 | 99 / 891 | 20.8 | 79.2 | +19.2 |
| Sensitivity 60% | 6 / 4 | 99 / 891 | 50.5 | 49.5 | −10.5 |

For the second row, positive reports occur in **6 + 99 = 105** catalogue cases. Preparing after positives costs **105 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 10.5 minutes** on average. Four missed faults cost **4 ÷ 1,000 × 10,000 = 40 minutes**. The policy costs **50.5** before report effort, improving on the 100-minute baseline by **49.5**. At a 60-minute report cost, that improvement is insufficient.

A positive still justifies the fallback in both models: **6 ÷ 105 ≈ 5.71%** also exceeds the 1% action threshold. A negative in the second model gives **4 ÷ 895 ≈ 0.447%**, still below that threshold, so the optimal response remains to prepare only after a positive. The response policy can stay the same while the expected benefit changes enough to reverse the purchase decision.

### When to use the input audit

Use it when plausible input changes alter the selected policy or whether research pays. Seek evidence about the relevant process and examine coherent alternatives or ranges. If the states, probabilities or objectives cannot be defended, retain the conditional scenario comparisons rather than presenting one precise total as established fact. The course on deep uncertainty develops choices when a single agreed probability model is unavailable.

## Audit a different batch

A second operation has a **2%** chance of a fault. An unprotected fault adds **2,000 work-minutes**; fallback preparation costs **80 minutes in either state** and prevents that loss. The report has **80% sensitivity**, **95% specificity** and **15 minutes of additional effort per batch**. All actions remain available in time, with no other test effects. Minimise expected additional work.

The stipulated catalogue is:

| Model cases | Positive report | Negative report | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fault | 16 | 4 | 20 |
| No fault | 49 | 931 | 980 |
| Total | 65 | 935 | 1,000 |

Calculate the best fixed policy, response to each result and complete expected cost before opening the solution.

### When this transfer is valid

The objective is expected work, and the supplied actions and probabilities define the comparison. A deadline, a hard limit on individual losses or another objective needs to enter the model explicitly. Do not carry this expected-work decision into a different objective by relabelling its answer.
