A museum's print room can use its standard method for an exhibition's labels or switch to a faster process. Three people propose three different compatibility tests. One is cheap, one is thorough, and one has the best expected contribution to the decision. Those need not be the same test.

This is a **hypothetical planning exercise**, with all outcomes measured in staff-minutes saved relative to the standard method. A **decision policy** specifies what action to take for each result. We will compare complete policies: obtain a result, choose an action and account for the test's effort. The original course example had a no-test value of zero. Here, acting without a test already has a positive expected value, so forgetting that baseline would substantially overprice the information.

The uncertain state is whether this batch of material is **compatible** with the faster process. There are only two actions and two states in the model:

| Action | Compatible batch | Incompatible batch |
|---|---|---|
| Use the faster process | +400 staff-minutes saved | −100 staff-minutes saved, meaning extra work |
| Keep the standard method | 0 | 0 |

The prior probability of compatibility is **30%**. Treat the table and probabilities as stated assumptions, not measured museum results. Quality is held constant; both methods are feasible, and the objective is expected total staff time. We assume no separate safety, deadline or maximum-loss constraint overrides this objective. A team with such a constraint needs a different feasible-action set or objective.

With a current probability of compatibility called **p**, switching has expected savings of **400p − 100(1 − p) = 500p − 100**. It beats the standard method when **p exceeds 100 ÷ 500 = 20%**. At exactly the threshold, the two actions tie; use the standard method as our tie convention.

Before testing, expected savings from switching are **0.30 × 400 − 0.70 × 100 = 50 minutes**. That beats the standard method's zero, so the **best no-test policy is to switch**, with value **50**. A probability below half does not automatically mean “do not act”: the payoff asymmetry puts the decision boundary at 20%.

Perfect information would let us switch only for compatible material: **0.30 × 400 = 120 minutes**. Its incremental value is therefore **EVPI = 120 − 50 = 70 minutes**, not 120. The ceiling concerns improvement over a decision we could already make.

### Pitfall and use

Use a threshold when an evidence result must be translated into an action. A fixed “sufficient confidence” percentage cannot replace the payoff comparison. With different gains, losses or available methods, derive a new threshold. The fact that a prior is lopsided is also insufficient to decide a test's value without the possible signals and stakes.

Protocol B returns **favourable** or **unfavourable**. It reports favourable for 80% of compatible batches and unfavourable for 80% of incompatible batches. These are sensitivity and specificity, both set to 80% here; an overall accuracy figure alone would not specify a general asymmetric test.

Imagine 100 comparable batches solely to make the probabilities visible. This is an expected-frequency calculation, not a record of 100 trials:

| Signal from B | Compatible, prior count 30 | Incompatible, prior count 70 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Favourable | 30 × 0.80 = 24 | 70 × 0.20 = 14 | 38 |
| Unfavourable | 30 × 0.20 = 6 | 70 × 0.80 = 56 | 62 |

After favourable news, compatibility has probability **24 ÷ 38 = 12/19 ≈ 63.16%**, above the 20% action boundary: switch. After unfavourable news, it has probability **6 ÷ 62 = 3/31 ≈ 9.68%**, below the boundary: keep the standard method.

The policy must be valued **before the result arrives**. The favourable branch switches; its contribution can be calculated directly from the joint probabilities. The unfavourable branch uses the standard method and contributes zero:

**Gross value with B = 0.24 × 400 − 0.14 × 100 + 0 = 82 minutes**

Equivalently, the favourable branch's conditional value is **400 × 12/19 − 100 × 7/19 = 4100/19 ≈ 215.79**; multiply by its probability **0.38** to obtain the same 82. Use the exact fractions rather than rounding the posterior before doing the payoff calculation.

This is the preposterior comparison: choose the best action for each possible result, then average across those results. [MIT's lecture on expected value of sample information](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/ids-333-risk-and-decision-analysis-fall-2021/1-oOSVfO8iRYQ4m-fO3pJ0WViPcW1-l6a_transcript.pdf) uses this decision-by-result construction and stresses how much it depends on the input assumptions.

Subtract the best no-test value to price the information: **EVSI(B) = 82 − 50 = 32 minutes**. Running and interpreting B takes **16 staff-minutes in total**, so its incremental net value is **32 − 16 = 16 minutes**. The complete B policy has expected net savings **82 − 16 = 66**, which also equals **50 + 16**.

The 16-minute cost is counted once. The production payoffs exclude this test effort; the stated test cost includes preparation, execution and interpretation. Results arrive before the method decision, with no additional delay loss or change to the material state in the base case.

## Compare protocols by the value they add

The initial menu allows **no test or one protocol**. Each protocol is evaluated for the same material, prior, payoffs and decision time. This is a comparison of those policies, not a claim to have optimised every possible sequence of experiments.

| Protocol | Correct signal within each state | Total test effort in staff-minutes |
|---|---|---|
| A, quick assessment | 70% | 8 |
| B, standard assessment | 80% | 16 |
| C, detailed assessment | 90% | 38 |

Each leads to switch-after-favourable and standard-after-unfavourable. You can reproduce the entire comparison from the joint favourable-signal probabilities:

| Protocol | Compatible and favourable | Incompatible and favourable | Gross policy value | EVSI over baseline 50 | Incremental net |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| No test | Not applicable | Not applicable | 50 | 0 | 0 |
| A | 0.30 × 0.70 = 0.21 | 0.70 × 0.30 = 0.21 | 0.21 × 400 − 0.21 × 100 = 63 | 63 − 50 = 13 | 13 − 8 = 5 |
| B | 0.24 | 0.14 | 82 | 32 | 32 − 16 = 16 |
| C | 0.30 × 0.90 = 0.27 | 0.70 × 0.10 = 0.07 | 0.27 × 400 − 0.07 × 100 = 101 | 101 − 50 = 51 | 51 − 38 = 13 |

B has the highest incremental net value, **16**, followed by C's **13** and A's **5**. C's extra accuracy adds **51 − 32 = 19** minutes of information value over B, but requires **38 − 16 = 22** extra minutes of effort. Its incremental disadvantage is **19 − 22 = −3**. A cheaper protocol and a more accurate protocol are both available; B wins this specified comparison.

## Check reliability near both boundaries

The action boundary and the purchase boundary answer different questions. In this case a sufficiently weak test changes beliefs without changing the method at all.

Consider a symmetric **60%** cross-check taking **5 minutes**. Favourable news gives compatibility probability **0.18 ÷ (0.18 + 0.28) = 9/23 ≈ 39.13%**. Unfavourable news gives **0.12 ÷ (0.12 + 0.42) = 2/9 ≈ 22.22%**. Both remain above the **20%** switching boundary. The team switches either way.

Its gross policy value remains **50**, so **EVSI = 50 − 50 = 0** and incremental net value is **−5**. The report can change a probability estimate while adding no value to this action choice. It does not follow that all research about this material has zero value: B already provides a counterexample.

Within the lab's **50–100%** correct-signal range, write the symmetric rate as a fraction **r** (for example, **0.80**). The unfavourable result crosses the action boundary only when **r exceeds 12/19 ≈ 63.16%**. At exactly that rate the actions tie after unfavourable news, so gross EVSI is still zero. This is the boundary for **positive information value**, not for paying any particular price.

Above that boundary, gross test-policy savings are **0.30r × 400 − 0.70(1 − r) × 100 = 190r − 70**. Subtract baseline 50 and cost 16 for protocol B: its incremental net is **190r − 136**. It beats no test only when **r exceeds 136/190 ≈ 71.58%** under these assumptions.

For example, if B's effective reliability were **70%**, with effort still **16**, its information value would be **13**, and net **13 − 16 = −3**. Its signal could change the method, yet this protocol would fail the cost comparison. Recompute the menu if validation changes a rate; do not keep the original ranking merely because the calculation once looked precise.

### When to use the sensitivity check

Use it when the choice depends on a test rating, prior or payoff estimate that may not fit the work at hand. Here, sensitivity and specificity were deliberately equal. If they differ, use both conditional rates separately; a single reliability slider cannot represent every test. Also check whether the sample and eventual production share the assumed material state.

## Stop by checking the next available experiment

Suppose B has already reported. Someone proposes adding the weak 60% cross-check. For this calculation, assume its result is **conditionally independent** of B's result given the true material state: once that state is fixed, learning one result does not change the other test's stated likelihoods. Shared samples or a repeated reading of the same evidence may violate this assumption.

Update from B's posterior, not the original 30%. A favourable cross-check has probabilities 0.60 and 0.40 in the two material states; an unfavourable one reverses those likelihoods:

| B result | Current compatibility probability | After favourable cross-check | After unfavourable cross-check | Best action after either |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Favourable | 12/19 | (12 × 0.60) ÷ (12 × 0.60 + 7 × 0.40) = 18/25 = 72% | (12 × 0.40) ÷ (12 × 0.40 + 7 × 0.60) = 8/15 ≈ 53.33% | Switch |
| Unfavourable | 3/31 | (3 × 0.60) ÷ (3 × 0.60 + 28 × 0.40) = 9/65 ≈ 13.85% | (3 × 0.40) ÷ (3 × 0.40 + 28 × 0.60) = 1/15 ≈ 6.67% | Standard method |

Within either row, both possible cross-check results leave the best action unchanged. Its **additional EVSI is zero**, conditional on what is already known, and its additional five-minute effort makes the net **−5**. Stop before ordering that check. Remaining uncertainty does not require another test when its possible signals cannot improve this decision.

This is a local stopping rule for the specified next experiment. A stronger study, a different action, or a jointly evaluated sequence may have value. Several weak signals together can cross a boundary even when one cannot. If those policies are available, compare their complete contingent actions, joint likelihoods and costs. [Shachter's work on information value](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.6739) makes the information available before each decision explicit; the order and dependence assumptions belong in the model.

### Keep the cost ledger consistent

At this stage B's effort is already spent. Price the **additional** check against the best action now available; do not charge B again to the stopping decision. If testing also delays production, include the lost time or changed payoff once, where it belongs. A delay already included in a protocol's total effort or adjusted payoff must not reappear as a second deduction for the same loss.

## Transfer the policy comparison

A library is considering a different labelling method. Its hypothetical payoffs are **+30 minutes** if suitable and **−70** otherwise; keeping the current method gives zero. Its prior is **60%** suitability. A report costs **2 minutes** and has equally probable results leading to posteriors of **55%** or **65%**. The results arrive in time, and there are no other effects or uses in this exercise.
