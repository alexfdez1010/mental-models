A warehouse spreadsheet says a batch of parcels will be ready in twenty minutes.
The stopwatch says twenty-five. Before adding a mysterious “reality factor,”
check that both timings cover the same work, then inspect what the spreadsheet
left out.

This workshop turns the map–territory warning into a repeatable audit. You will
write down the decision a model serves, compare predictions with observations,
and use the pattern of errors to choose what to change.

## Start with the decision

A kitchen timer is useful only after you decide what “done” means. Timing a soft
egg and timing sterilisation are different jobs. Likewise, a **model audit** is a
structured comparison between a model, relevant observations, and the decision
the model is meant to support. Its engineering home is **model validation**:
assessing how well a representation matches the world for an intended use.
[NASA's modelling glossary](https://public.ksc.nasa.gov/mns/ms-definitions/)
explicitly ties validation to that use.

For our warehouse, let $n$ be the number of parcels in a batch and
$\hat{T}$ its predicted preparation time, in minutes:

$$\hat{T} = 8 + 2n$$

The model allows eight minutes to open the workstation and two minutes per
parcel. For six parcels, $8 + 2 \times 6 = 20$ minutes. It omits small differences
between individual parcels. That omission can be acceptable without being true
in every detail.

Before looking at fresh results, the supervisor writes this model card:

| Question | Recorded answer |
|---|---|
| What decision will use the prediction? | Choose a collection slot for an ordinary batch |
| What exactly is timed? | From starting workstation setup until the last parcel is sealed |
| Where has the model been checked? | Weekday batches of one to ten standard parcels, handled sequentially |
| What error is acceptable for this audit? | At most three minutes early or late for each checked batch |
| What would require another check? | A process change, a larger batch, or a tighter deadline |

The three-minute rule is the supervisor's chosen operational requirement, not a
statistical law. Recording it first prevents quietly loosening the rule after
seeing an inconvenient result. Passing this small audit will support limited
use; it will not establish a guarantee for every future batch.

### When to use it

Write a model card when someone says “the model has been validated” without
stating what it will decide. A common mistake is to demand maximum detail before
defining the job. Detail earns its place when it changes a relevant prediction
or decision.

## Compare the same thing on both sides

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="The formula is calculated correctly, but the measured duration includes a lunch break that the prediction excludes. What should you repair first?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Make the predicted and measured durations comparable', correct: true },
    { text: 'Increase the fixed allowance to absorb every lunch break' },
    { text: 'Discard the formula because arithmetic cannot help here' },
  ]}
  explanation="A mismatch in the definition of the output is not yet evidence about the model's preparation-time accuracy. Align the timing rules before diagnosing an error pattern."
/>

Checking a ruler against another ruler is pointless if one starts at the edge
and the other starts at the first printed mark. Here, prediction and measurement
must use the same start, finish, units, and parcel type.

First, **verification** checks that the calculation follows the specified model.
Entering six parcels should produce twenty minutes. Next, **validation** compares
that prediction with relevant observations. A perfectly implemented formula can
still describe the wrong process. NASA distinguishes these two checks in its
[definitions of verification and validation](https://public.ksc.nasa.gov/mns/ms-definitions/).

The supervisor times four new batches that were not used to choose the formula.
For each batch, the **prediction error** is the observed time minus the predicted
time. A positive error means preparation took longer than forecast. In a fitted
statistical model, the corresponding observed-minus-fitted difference is called
a **residual**. [NIST uses these differences to inspect model adequacy](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmd/section4/pmd44.htm).

| Parcels | Prediction in minutes | Observation in minutes | Error in minutes | Absolute error in minutes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 12 | 13 | +1 | 1 |
| 4 | 16 | 15 | −1 | 1 |
| 6 | 20 | 21 | +1 | 1 |
| 8 | 24 | 23 | −1 | 1 |

For the first batch, the prediction is $8 + 2 \times 2 = 12$ minutes; the error is
$13 - 12 = 1$ minute. For the second, $15 - 16 = -1$ minute. The **absolute
error** drops the sign and keeps the size of the miss.

The average signed error is $(1 - 1 + 1 - 1)/4 = 0$ minutes. The average absolute
error is $(1 + 1 + 1 + 1)/4 = 1$ minute. Zero average signed error does not mean
perfect predictions: early and late errors can cancel. Each observed miss is
within the three-minute requirement, so this check passes under the recorded
conditions. Four batches cannot tell us how often an unusually bad miss occurs.

### When to use it

Use signed errors to look for a consistent direction of failure and absolute
errors to judge the size of misses. Preserve batch-level results: an average can
hide a single miss that matters to the decision. Check the measuring process
before treating its readings as unquestionable truth.

## Diagnose what changed before adding a correction

A coat that was always a little loose differs from a coat that suddenly stops
fitting. **Approximation error** can come from details the model deliberately
omits. **Model drift**, in this workshop, means a formerly useful relationship
has stopped matching because the process or its conditions changed. An error
alone does not identify which explanation applies.

The warehouse adds an access check before each batch. The timing definition still
starts before all setup work. New observations are:

| Parcels | Old prediction in minutes | New observation in minutes | Error in minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 12 | 17 | +5 |
| 4 | 16 | 21 | +5 |
| 6 | 20 | 25 | +5 |
| 8 | 24 | 29 | +5 |

Every miss has the same direction and size across these batch sizes. That pattern
suggests a missing fixed step, rather than a larger per-parcel allowance. Direct
observation supplies the missing link: the new access check takes five minutes
per batch in these trials. The error pattern helped find the explanation; it did
not prove the cause by itself.

The candidate revision is therefore $\hat{T} = 13 + 2n$: the fixed allowance rises
from eight to thirteen minutes. It is a candidate, not a new truth issued by
spreadsheet decree. If the access check sometimes queues behind other batches,
its delay may vary; a constant correction would then need reconsidering.

### When to use it

Compare errors by time, workload, or operating condition when misses become
consistent. A stable old error can still be unacceptable; a recent error can
come from a faulty measurement. Diagnose before labelling either one “drift.”

## Keep, revise, restrict, or replace

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="You adjust a rule until it matches the batches used to adjust it. What would provide a stronger check of the revision?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Predict fresh comparable batches before timing them', correct: true },
    { text: 'Recalculate the same adjusted batches more precisely' },
    { text: 'Show only the adjusted batches with the smallest misses' },
  ]}
  explanation="The adjustment used those observations already. New comparable cases test whether the correction travels beyond the examples that suggested it."
/>

**Calibration** means adjusting a model's numbers to improve agreement with
observations. Our fixed-allowance revision is calibration. Checking only the
observations that suggested the adjustment would give the candidate an unfair
advantage. The same separation between fitting and fresh evaluation appears in
[Google's guide to dividing datasets](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/overfitting/dividing-datasets).

Before changing anything else, the supervisor records the revised predictions
for two further ordinary batches:

| Parcels | Revised prediction in minutes | Fresh observation in minutes | Error in minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 19 | 20 | +1 |
| 7 | 27 | 26 | −1 |

For three parcels, $13 + 2 \times 3 = 19$ minutes, so $20 - 19 = 1$ minute. For
seven, $13 + 2 \times 7 = 27$, so $26 - 27 = -1$ minute. Both pass this audit's
three-minute rule. They provide limited support for using the revised rule for
ordinary, sequential batches while continuing to monitor results.

Now distinguish four decisions:

| Decision | Evidence that would support it | Warehouse application |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep** | Relevant fresh errors meet the requirement, with no concerning pattern found | Keep the original rule before the access change |
| **Revise** | A diagnosed omission has a correction that survives a fresh check | Include the measured access step |
| **Restrict** | Evidence supports only some conditions or a less demanding decision | Use the revised rule for standard batches, not oversized parcels |
| **Replace** | The representation misses a relationship essential to the current job | For overlapping batches, use a method that accounts for the queue |

Suppose a weekend sale produces twenty-parcel batches and several teams share
the same access desk. Simply inserting twenty into the revised formula gives
$13 + 2 \times 20 = 53$ minutes. The arithmetic is valid; its use is unvalidated.
Both batch size and queueing conditions have left the checked domain. For that
job, suspend reliance on this rule until a suitable model or direct process
assessment is available. You can still keep it for the smaller sequential job.

### When to use it

Choose among these actions when a model meets resistance from evidence. Replacing
a map can be right for one decision without declaring it useless everywhere.
Record the version, conditions, evidence, and next review trigger so “we checked
it once” does not become a permanent exemption from checking.

### Sources and provenance

The warehouse and repair cases are constructed teaching examples, not measured
industry datasets. Their arithmetic can be reproduced from the tables.

- [NASA — modelling and simulation definitions](https://public.ksc.nasa.gov/mns/ms-definitions/) supports the distinctions between intended use, verification, validation, calibration, and operating limits.
- [NIST — assessing model fit](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmd/section4/pmd44.htm) explains observed-minus-fitted errors and why their patterns matter.
- [Google — separating fitting and evaluation data](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/overfitting/dividing-datasets) explains why fresh evaluation cases provide evidence beyond the cases used to adjust a model.
