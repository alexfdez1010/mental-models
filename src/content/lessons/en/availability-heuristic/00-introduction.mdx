Someone asks how often a support problem occurs. You can picture last week's incident immediately, but the register is in another tab. The speed of that memory gives you an answer-shaped feeling before you have counted anything.

The **availability heuristic** is the shortcut of estimating frequency or probability from how easily relevant examples come to mind. A **heuristic** is a practical rule used in place of a fuller calculation. Easy recall can be informative, but other influences can also make a case accessible. The question is whether the shortcut fits the frequency you are trying to judge.

The shortcut replaces a target question—“how frequent is it?”—with an easier attribute—“how readily can I retrieve an example?” That replacement is called **attribute substitution**. Here it describes a possible process, not a method for reading someone's mind.

Imagine estimating the stock in a warehouse by the first item you can picture. A box beside the door is easy to remember; a box at the back may be plentiful and unremarkable. The analogy captures unequal access. Human memory is more complicated than a warehouse: it can reconstruct or misremember, and judgments can use several sources of information.

| Claim | What you would need to examine |
|---|---|
| “I can recall an incident easily” | A report or measure of retrieval experience |
| “I recalled many distinct incidents” | A validated count with duplicate IDs removed |
| “This type is frequent in the register” | A defined set of incidents and the relevant denominator |
| “The recent story caused this estimate” | Evidence that distinguishes that causal explanation from alternatives |

An anecdote can be accurate and relevant. It can reveal a failure nobody had recorded, explain a mechanism or supply a current fact. It does not supply a population frequency merely by being memorable. Equally, a table can use the wrong population or miss recent changes. Check what the evidence answers.

## What the course will let you check

Tversky and Kahneman analysed availability in their 1973 article. Later work by Schwarz and colleagues separated the content recalled from the difficulty experienced while recalling it. These studies give us concepts and testable mechanisms, not a guarantee about every person or task. [Tversky and Kahneman](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(73)90033-9); [Schwarz and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.61.2.195).

Vividness, recency, emotion and repetition can matter for what comes to mind. Their presence alone does not prove an estimate is inflated. A recent report can contain genuinely new evidence, and an unfamiliar problem can be underestimated. The practical task is to compare the judgment with suitable evidence rather than automatically correcting it downward.

News coverage is selected for purposes other than counting every event proportionally. Accurate reports can therefore be an unsuitable sample for a frequency estimate. There is no universal rule that more coverage means fewer events. Distinct reports can also describe the same incident, which is why the course separates coverage, recorded events and relevant exposure.

### When to use the model

Use availability as a question to investigate when a frequency estimate rests heavily on what is easy to remember. Define the outcome, population and period; check the denominator and selection; then ask what evidence could change the estimate. Do not diagnose a bias from a person's fear or final choice alone. A decision may also depend on severity, duration, alternatives and cost.
