One bridge closure generates a breaking report, an interview, an update, and a
video shared again tomorrow. You have encountered several items of coverage,
but not several bridge closures. When those items come easily to mind, the
availability heuristic can quietly replace “how often did this happen?” with
“how much have I encountered it?”

The useful correction is to identify what each number counts. Coverage is not
inherently the inverse of frequency, and a dramatic report is not automatically
evidence of a rare or exaggerated danger. It may contain an important new fact.
The task is to separate that evidence from the amount of attention it receives.

## Separate accurate stories from a representative sample

  explanation="Items of coverage and distinct events have different counting units. Repetition does not establish either an event count or a universal inverse relationship with frequency."

A playlist that repeats one song tells you what is being played, not how many
songs were released. A **selection effect** arises when the way examples enter
your information changes what they represent. A collection of selected stories
need not be a representative sample of all events, even if the individual
stories are accurate.

Keep two questions separate: **is this report supported?** and **can this set of
reports estimate the frequency I care about?** Fact-checking the report addresses
the first question. It does not automatically answer the second. Reuters's
[editorial standards](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/), for
example, require accuracy and context; that is different from treating published
items as a random sample of events.

An event might receive attention because of its consequences, local relevance,
a change in conditions, or an ongoing investigation. A common problem can also
receive sustained coverage. There is no general equation saying that more
coverage means lower frequency.

### When to use it

Use this distinction when many of your remembered examples trace back to the
same incident. Deduplicate the cases before counting. An independent outlet can
corroborate a fact about one event without turning it into another event.

## Compare attention with recorded events

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A chart compares the share of news items about each disruption with its share of recorded disruptions. Does either share directly give your chance of being delayed on a trip?"

  ]}
  explanation="The denominators are news items and recorded events. A per-trip question needs a journey-based denominator and an outcome definition appropriate to that trip."

Consider a constructed town example covering eight weeks. The selected outlets
publish 100 transport-news items. Each item is assigned to one main category.
A separate complete record for this example contains 200 distinct disruptions,
also assigned to one category each. An update about an existing event is not
another event in that record.

| Category | News items | Share of those items | Recorded disruptions | Share of those disruptions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bridge closures | 60 | 60% | 2 | 1% |
| Bus delays | 25 | 25% | 100 | 50% |
| Ticket-machine faults | 15 | 15% | 98 | 49% |
| Total | 100 | 100% | 200 | 100% |

For bridge closures, the coverage share is $60/100 \times 100 = 60\%$; the
recorded-event share is $2/200 \times 100 = 1\%$. Both calculations are correct
because they answer different questions. For bus delays,
$100/200 \times 100 = 50\%$ of recorded disruptions are delays, despite their
smaller share of coverage.

in the teaching table, not estimated fatality data or measurements of public fear.

This table demonstrates a mismatch in the constructed example. It does not
establish how any real outlet distributes attention or how any population feels.
Nor does a low event share make a bridge closure unimportant: one closure can
affect many journeys for a long time. The table does not record that impact.

### When to use it

Before reading a percentage as a risk, finish the phrase “percent **of what**?”
A share of events is not a share of people exposed, trips affected, or time lost.
Check the event definitions, coverage of the record, place, and period as well.

## Put the exposure back into the comparison

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="One bus route has more delayed departures than another but runs many more departures overall. What must you check before calling it more delay-prone?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Delayed departures relative to the relevant departures on each route', correct: true },
    { text: 'Which route generated the larger number of stories in the local news' },
    { text: 'Whether the larger delay count is easier to remember than the smaller' },
  ]}
  explanation="A larger service has more opportunities to record a delay. An exposure-based comparison must include the number of relevant departures, not just the delay count."
/>

A larger building can have more faulty bulbs simply because it contains more
bulbs. Comparing the fault counts alone leaves that difference out. The **numerator** counts
the specified outcome. The **denominator** defines the relevant opportunities
or population in which that outcome was counted. Statistical guidance from
[DHSC](https://fingertips.phe.org.uk/static-reports/public-health-technical-guidance/Basic_statistics/Overview_basic_stats.html)
distinguishes counts, proportions, and rates and explains why denominators
matter for comparisons.

For a separate route comparison, define the outcome as **a recorded departure
more than ten minutes late**. Count each departure once. Both routes use the
same definition, measurement method, and eight-week observation window.

| Route | Delayed departures | Recorded departures | Delayed departures per 1,000 recorded departures |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 20 | 2,000 | 10 |
| B | 30 | 10,000 | 3 |

Let $D$ be delayed departures and $N$ all recorded departures. The frequency
per thousand, $F_{1000}$, is:

$$F_{1000} = \frac{D}{N} \times 1000$$

For A, $20/2000 \times 1000 = 10$. For B, $30/10000 \times 1000 = 3$. B has more
delayed departures in total, but A has the higher observed proportion per
departure. Both statements can be true.

These are frequencies for the recorded departures, not guarantees for your next
journey. Your departure time, direction, weather, or service type may differ
from the mix in the table. Also, a comparison of delay frequency does not say
which delays are longest.

### When to use it

Match the denominator to the outcome: trips for a per-trip question, deliveries
for a per-delivery question, and relevant time exposed when that is the measure
needed. Dividing by the town's population would answer a different question
from dividing by departures.

## Keep the time window and the record comparable

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A service records more delays after doubling its departures. Does the larger count establish that the chance of a delayed departure rose?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; compare the delay counts with the departures in each period', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; a larger count always means a larger per-departure frequency' },
    { text: 'No; once service volume changes, no comparison can be calculated' },
  ]}
  explanation="The opportunity count changed too. A comparable exposure-based measure may rise, fall, or stay the same even when the raw delay count rises."
/>

Use a different constructed service to examine a headline about a rise in delays.
The first and second periods each last four weeks and use the same definition
of a delayed departure:

| Period | Delayed departures | Recorded departures | Delayed departures per 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–4 | 20 | 5,000 | 4 |
| Weeks 5–8 | 30 | 10,000 | 3 |

The count rises from twenty to thirty. But $20/5000 \times 1000 = 4$, while
$30/10000 \times 1000 = 3$: the recorded frequency per departure falls. A headline
about “more delays” can be literally true while leaving the exposure change
unstated. You need not assume dishonest reporting to identify the missing context.

Before comparing periods, check dates and duration, outcome threshold, covered
services, and recording method. If the operator starts recording minor delays
that were previously omitted, a change in the series may reflect the method as
well as the service. Consistent denominators do not repair inconsistent outcomes.

Data also have limits. A short observation window may not capture seasonal
conditions or provide a stable prediction of future performance. The
[ONS guidance on uncertainty](https://www.ons.gov.uk/methodology/methodologytopicsandstatisticalconcepts/uncertaintyandhowwemeasureit)
explains both sampling uncertainty and errors that remain even when a whole
population is covered, such as inaccurate responses or processing errors.

### When to use it

When someone compares “this year” with “last year,” ask whether both periods are
complete and measured alike. When they compare counts across periods of
unequal length or different service volume, inspect exposure before interpreting
the change. Describe what the data support without inventing a cause.

## Use a report as evidence without using attention as probability

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A current, verified report says the bridge you need is closed. Should you dismiss it because bridge closures were a small share of the earlier event record?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; the current condition is relevant evidence for this journey', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; any vivid report should automatically lower your estimate of danger' },
    { text: 'Yes; a historical event share overrides every current observation' },
  ]}
  explanation="The historical share does not answer whether this bridge is open now. Check the report's place, time, and source, then use the relevant current information."
/>

A weather vane and a diary answer different questions. Historical frequency is
useful background; a verified current condition can change what applies now.
The correction to availability is to examine evidence, not to reverse every
feeling or discount every well-covered event.

A report may also contain the data you need: linked records, a transparent
method, a denominator, and a relevant reference period. Assess those elements.
The same statistic does not become invalid simply because a journalist reports
it, and a table does not become complete merely because it looks official.
The [AIHW glossary](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports-data/population-health-monitoring/glossary)
defines risk over a specified period and identifies the population at risk as
the denominator for a rate.

For a frequency question, follow a short procedure:

1. Specify the outcome, place, group or exposure, and period you need.
2. Distinguish repeated items about one case from distinct events.
3. Trace the number to its source and check how the record was assembled.
4. Compare like outcomes with relevant denominators and time windows.
5. Use applicable new evidence, while keeping severity and uncertainty separate
   from the amount of coverage.

### When to use it

Use this process when coverage makes a frequency feel obvious. A mismatch is a
reason to investigate, not a rule that the familiar risk must be smaller.
Decisions may depend on consequences and available actions as well as frequency.

10 of 1,000 delivered parcels receive at least one complaint. In the next
four-week period, 15 of 3,000 delivered parcels do. Count complaints within seven
days of each parcel's delivery, with all follow-up complete. Each parcel is
counted at most once and both periods use the same complaint definition.

| Period | Parcels with a complaint | Delivered parcels | Parcels with a complaint per 1,000 delivered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earlier | 10 | 1,000 | 10 |
| Later | 15 | 3,000 | 5 |

The calculations are $10/1000 \times 1000 = 10$ and
$15/3000 \times 1000 = 5$. These are constructed records for comparison, not a
claim about a real delivery company.

### Sources and case scope

All town, route, service, and parcel numbers in this lesson are constructed
teaching data. They demonstrate counting and comparison; they are not estimates
of real fatalities, public fear, or actual transport performance.

The Reuters standards support the distinction between checking a report and
requiring context. The DHSC and AIHW references explain statistical units and
denominators. The ONS reference explains why recorded or estimated numbers
still require attention to error and uncertainty.
