A team chat fills with warnings about a shared tool. Is the tool failing more often, are the same failures circulating, or are people reacting to one another's warnings? Those explanations can coexist. Counting messages does not choose between them.

This lesson follows social amplification and then builds an evidence check. It connects availability with base rates without treating either concept as a universal explanation of the other. The next workshop will audit a complete register through presentation and recall.

## A cascade is a feedback mechanism

A microphone can pick up its own speaker and feed the sound back into the room. An **availability cascade** similarly describes a self-reinforcing social process: a claim becomes more publicly available, people react, and their reactions help make it more available or plausible. Unlike the microphone, people interpret sources, disagree and respond to incentives. The loop can stall, reverse or remain confined to a group.

Kuran and Sunstein distinguish **informational** and **reputational** channels. In an informational channel, someone treats others' apparent acceptance as information. In a reputational channel, someone adjusts a public statement because of the social consequences of expressing it. Public agreement need not match private conviction. Both can operate together. Their framework does not define a cascade by a constant underlying event rate, and greater attention alone does not prove that the proposed mechanism occurred. [Kuran and Sunstein, 1999, sections III.B–F](https://sites.duke.edu/timurkuran/files/2016/10/availability-cascades-1.original.pdf).

### What would distinguish the explanations

Now change the premises. Jon has independently inspected a new failure, and Rui has a complete current register. Rising concern can then include genuine learning. Social amplification and new evidence can also occur together. Investigate source dependence, timing, actual records and the reasons people report; do not treat reported motives alone as decisive causal proof.

An **availability entrepreneur** deliberately tries to initiate or sustain a cascade to advance an agenda. That describes a role, not a verdict that the claim is false or the goal harmful. Nor does repetition alone establish someone's intention. Kuran and Sunstein discuss both beneficial and harmful social consequences; no blanket rule about which causes to support follows from the label.

## Base rates and retrieval ease answer different questions

A **base rate** is a relevant background frequency. **Base-rate neglect** means giving that background insufficient weight in an inference. **Availability** specifies a shortcut based on retrieval ease. One concerns the weighting of information, the other a proposed judgment process. They can interact; neither definition makes one the sole cause of the other.

Consider a constructed export log. Under a fixed software version and a consistent definition, 20 of 2,000 completed exports failed last month. The recorded failure frequency is $20/2000=1/100$. A colleague explicitly says, “I will ignore that log and estimate frequency from how effortlessly I recall the dramatic failure.” Under those declared premises, the colleague both sets aside relevant baseline information and uses availability. Merely ordering a repair would establish neither process.

A current incident may instead reveal a new software version or a changed failure definition. Then the old frequency needs an applicability check. Starting with a base rate does not mean freezing it against all new evidence.

### When to use a background frequency

Check that it describes the outcome, population, exposure and period relevant to the question. Compare case evidence with it; do not mechanically substitute a broad average for current applicable facts. A vivid observation can be important without establishing how frequent its type is.

## Measure before diagnosing

The classic studies are useful partly because their tasks separate things that an everyday anecdote leaves mixed together. Even so, the results need their design and limits.

| Published study | What the article reports | Limit to retain |
|---|---|---|
| Tversky and Kahneman, letter-position task | For K, typical-text counts cited in the paper had twice as many third-position words as initial-position words; a majority judgment favoured initial position | This is a particular text-frequency comparison and task, not a fixed ratio for all English corpora or proof that every person erred |
| Tversky and Kahneman, famous names | Lists contained 19 famous names of one sex and 20 less-famous names of the other; recall and frequency judgments were collected from different groups | The classic comparison does not, by itself, isolate experienced ease from recalled content within each individual |
| Schwarz and colleagues | Tasks requested 6 or 12 examples of assertive or unassertive behaviour; the studies assessed difficulty and self-ratings and varied the diagnosticity of ease | The first experiment's interaction was marginal; subsequent studies and the misattribution manipulation supply additional evidence, not a universal law about listing more reasons |

Tversky and Kahneman's definition and discussion support asking about assessed ease. Schwarz and colleagues explicitly identify the content-versus-ease ambiguity in earlier demonstrations. Their published article includes both supporting results and limits, including an unsuccessful diagnosticity manipulation in their second experiment. [Original 1973 article, hosted copy](https://noorsiddiqui.com/papers/TverskyKahneman.pdf); [published 1991 article, university-hosted copy](https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/psyifp/aeechterhoff/sommersemester2012/sozialekognition/schwarzblessstrack_etal.pdf).

## An evidence check for a real decision

Treat a feeling of obviousness as a prompt to investigate, not a diagnostic test. A useful check has a sequence:

1. **Name the target.** What outcome, whose experience, which period and denominator? “How common?” is incomplete until those are fixed.
2. **Trace the input.** Distinguish unique events, repeated reports and other people's endorsements. A source may convey relevant new data.
3. **Check the record.** Use consistent definitions and relevant exposure. A personal memory is still selected, even after a year of ordinary experience.
4. **Separate measures.** Counted recall, reported ease and a frequency estimate need separate observations. Exposure alone does not identify a mental cause.
5. **Update and decide.** Assess current applicability and uncertainty. Then consider consequences and response costs alongside frequency.

### A complete comparison

For a hypothetical four-week office log, every incident is recorded once with its full interruption duration. All follow-up is complete. The types are exclusive; the table counts incidents, not worker-specific risk.

| Type | Distinct incidents | Minutes per incident, fixed in this example | Total interruption minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brief fault | 10 | 1 | 10 |
| Long fault | 1 | 60 | 60 |

The brief type is more frequent: $10/11$ of incidents. The long type nevertheless accounts for $60/(10+60)=6/7$ of interruption minutes. Replaying the long fault cannot change the incident count, but its duration is relevant evidence. Neither fraction alone determines which intervention is effective or worth its cost.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Check personal experience too">
Stable firsthand experience can be useful. To evaluate its accuracy, compare remembered events with a contemporaneous log of the relevant opportunities and outcomes. Familiarity alone does not certify equal selection or recall across types.
</Callout>

## Transfer and recap

A different team receives a current, verified notice that a tool is unavailable today. The old annual log shows few outages. A member postpones a task. Before reading the answer, state why this action alone cannot establish availability bias or base-rate neglect.
