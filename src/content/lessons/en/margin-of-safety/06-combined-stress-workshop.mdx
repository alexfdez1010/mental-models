A museum expects a group of visitors and prepares spare audio guides. The organiser checks two things: the pool can handle a larger group, and it can handle some broken devices. Both checks pass. The opening can still be short of guides if those two stresses happen together.

This workshop turns “leave a buffer” into a decision you can reproduce. You will define the promise, test a bounded set of combined demands, compare the effort needed for different reserves and identify what would invalidate the conclusion. The museum and all quantities below are hypothetical.

The promise for this constructed event is: **each visitor in the group can receive a working guide at 10:00**, when the tour starts. Each visitor needs a separate device for the whole tour; devices are not reused within the group. The case assumes the handover staff and preparation schedule can support the stated pool. We are counting resources at a deadline, rather than estimating a queue or structural breaking strength.

Use **90 visitors** as a reference forecast. The covered demand range is **80 to 110 visitors**. For any candidate pool below, **up to 12 prepared devices may be unusable at the opening**. This is an absolute count bound, not a failure percentage that scales with the pool. The larger group and the unavailable devices may occur together.

For now, all prepared devices are physically at the handover point by the opening. No extra arrivals, battery replacements or transport can be counted after the deadline. We relax the readiness assumption later.

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| N | Devices prepared and present by the opening | Guides |
| F | Prepared devices unusable at the opening | Guides |
| D | Visitors needing guides at that opening | Visitors, with one guide each |
| N − F | Usable capacity for this group | Guides |
| N − F − D | Headroom remaining after serving the group | Guides |

**Headroom** is what remains after the demand is served. A negative value is a shortage. A value of zero means the count serves the stated group exactly, with no remaining spare. That is an availability statement for this case, not a general engineering rule about ultimate failure loads.

[NASA’s decision-analysis guidance](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/6-8-decision-analysis/) starts with the intended outcome and evaluation criteria, and calls for documenting assumptions and limitations. Here those choices determine which count we need before choosing a percentage.

Start with **N = 120** prepared guides. The pool is **30 above the reference forecast**. Watch the separate checks and their combination:

| Covered case | Demand D | Unusable F | Usable N − F | Remaining headroom |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reference attendance, no losses | 90 | 0 | 120 | 120 − 90 = +30 |
| Higher attendance only | 110 | 0 | 120 | 120 − 110 = +10 |
| Device losses only | 90 | 12 | 108 | 108 − 90 = +18 |
| Both upper stresses together | 110 | 12 | 108 | 108 − 110 = −2 |

The pool passes each isolated stress, yet lacks **2 working guides** in the combined case. The spare count was not wrong; the claim made from the separate tests was too broad.

To cover every allowed pair of demand and loss counts, require:

**N − F ≥ D**, or equivalently **N ≥ D + F**.

Within the stated ranges, more demand and more unusable units can only reduce headroom. The most demanding pair is therefore **D = 110 and F = 12**. It requires **110 + 12 = 122 prepared guides**. Relative to the reference of 90, that is **20 for additional visitors plus 12 for unavailable units: 32 extra guides**.

This is coverage of a **bounded scenario set**: all combinations allowed by the stated ranges and readiness assumptions. It does not require the stresses to be statistically independent, because we are not multiplying probabilities. It also does not assign a probability that the real opening will fall inside those ranges. The boundaries are assumptions whose credibility still matters.

### When to use this calculation

Use it when the same reserve must absorb more than one simultaneous demand on it. First check that the categories are distinct: if a quoted unavailable count already includes late devices, adding the late count again would double-count the loss. Here the loss allowance concerns unusable devices among those already present.

Keep **120 prepared guides on site** and the combined case of **110 visitors and 12 unusable guides**. The usable count at 10:00 is still **108**, leaving the shortage of **2**. Suppose another **8 guides** can be fetched, but only arrive at **10:20**. They can raise later availability to **116** if they work, but they were not available at the promised opening.

A backup may be genuinely useful without meeting this particular deadline. If the museum instead accepts a delayed start or a different form of guide, it has changed the service requirement and must evaluate that alternative explicitly. More inventory and a different promise should not be quietly treated as the same plan.

## Compare the cost of the covered reserve

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest={true}

    {
      "text": "The most impressive percentage, even if its denominator differs across options."
    }
  ]}
  explanation={"Define feasibility first, then compare the chosen cost measure. A bigger buffer may have other benefits, but those benefits and costs need to be stated rather than assumed."}

Assume preparing each guide uses **3 volunteer-minutes**, including work spent on a unit later found unusable. The available preparation allocation is **400 volunteer-minutes**. All listed preparation can be completed before the opening under the case’s staffing and scheduling assumptions. Each guide also needs storage; that resource is adequate here and is not separately priced.

The narrow objective is **to meet every covered opening case with the least preparation time**. Compare:

| Prepared pool N | Usable with 12 unavailable | Headroom for 110 visitors | Preparation effort | Covers all stated combinations? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 110 guides | 98 | −12 | 110 × 3 = 330 volunteer-minutes | No |
| 120 guides | 108 | −2 | 120 × 3 = 360 volunteer-minutes | No |
| 122 guides | 110 | 0 | 122 × 3 = 366 volunteer-minutes | Yes |
| 130 guides | 118 | +8 | 130 × 3 = 390 volunteer-minutes | Yes |

Under these assumptions, **122** is the smallest feasible pool and uses the least preparation time. The reference pool of 90 would use **270 minutes**. Covering the combined bounds therefore adds **32 guides and 96 volunteer-minutes**, because **32 × 3 = 96**.

Moving from 122 to 130 buys **8 further working guides of headroom in the upper covered case** and costs another **24 volunteer-minutes**. Those minutes could be used for other preparation. We have not valued the displaced tasks or the consequences of a shortage, so this is not an all-purpose optimum for a real museum.

### What extra reserve buys beyond the stated bound

At demand of 110, the larger pool can absorb **20 unusable guides**, since **130 − 20 = 110**. The smaller pool cannot: **122 − 20 = 102**, a shortage of **8**. That is an additional condition covered by the larger pool. It does not show how likely that condition is or make the larger pool immune to every shared failure.

A pool of 122 uses all its headroom in the covered upper case. Choosing further reserve could be reasonable if the bound is doubtful or the objective requires a working spare after serving the group. State that added requirement and its cost. [The Green Book’s appraisal guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-green-book--2/the-green-book-2026) asks decision makers to compare options and examine how uncertain assumptions affect their results.

The arithmetic can be correct while its boundary is unreliable. For example:

- The demand range excludes walk-ins, a merged booking or visitors omitted from the count.
- The loss allowance came from a limited check and does not cover the actual battery, content or access failures.
- A shared configuration fault disables the whole prepared pool. In that stipulated case F equals N, usable capacity is zero and adding more identically affected devices does not restore service.
- The spare is present but cannot be issued by the opening, contrary to the readiness assumption.

These are reasons to investigate the model’s scope. A bound should have a recorded basis: what it covers, which period and operating conditions it describes, and what evidence or enforceable constraint supports it. A label such as “conservative” supplies none of those details by itself.

A useful review plan checks attendance commitments, working-unit tests, shared dependencies and readiness before relying on the reserve. If group demand exceeds the covered limit, losses exceed their allowance or readiness changes, recalculate or revise the service promise. [NASA’s technical-risk guidance](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/6-4-technical-risk-management/) includes monitoring measures and triggers for reconsidering mitigation as conditions change.

The conclusion is **conditional coverage**, not a measured zero probability of failure. No failure probability has been supplied in this workshop. Nor have we claimed that the proposed reserve is suitable for a real event without verifying the inputs.

### Transfer to a sketching session

A community sketching session needs **up to 24 working kits at its start**. Each kit is used for the whole session. In every covered case, **up to 3 of the kits present at the start are unusable**. Late kits are a separate category, disjoint from those unusable present kits. Preparation costs **2 volunteer-minutes per kit in the total prepared pool**, including kits arriving late. All other resources are adequate.

| Plan | Total prepared | Arrive only after the start | Present at the start | Usable after the covered loss | Preparation effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 32 | 6 | 26 | To calculate | 64 volunteer-minutes |
| B | 34 | 4 | 30 | To calculate | 68 volunteer-minutes |
| C | 30 | 0 | 30 | To calculate | 60 volunteer-minutes |

Plan A has **32 − 6 − 3 = 23 usable kits**, so it fails the upper demand by one. B has **34 − 4 − 3 = 27**; C has **30 − 0 − 3 = 27**. Both B and C leave **3** working kits after serving 24 people. C uses **60 minutes**, versus B’s **68**, so C answers the stated least-effort objective. A’s larger total inventory does not compensate for arriving late.

This conclusion still depends on the declared upper loss, lateness and work assumptions. If “unavailable” already included late kits, subtracting them again would be wrong. The categories have to match the definition before the formula travels to a new case.

### Sources and scope

The linked NASA and HM Treasury guidance supports defining criteria, comparing alternatives, stating uncertainty and reviewing assumptions. The event datasets and deterministic calculations are invented for teaching. They are not measurements of museum demand, certified device reliability, a structural safety-factor standard or a probability-of-failure estimate.
