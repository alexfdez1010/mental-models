## Define the amount and the period first

We will use a **constructed test archive**, measured in **MB**, megabytes. The numbers are declared model quantities, not measurements of a real storage service. One **period** is one completed processing round. The initial state is round zero; the first update produces the state after one round.

**Additive growth** adds the same amount each period. **Compounding** applies each proportional change to the base left by previous changes. In a **multiplicative update**, a factor is applied to that current amount; a constant-factor growth rule is one case. A **factor** is a dimensionless multiplier: multiplying by 1.10 keeps the whole amount and adds another tenth. The corresponding **rate** is 0.10, or 10%, per round. Multiplying by 0.10 would retain only one tenth; it is a different operation.

Picture adding a strip of fixed area to a sheet versus making an extra piece equal to a chosen fraction of its current area. The analogy tracks an amount and its base. It does not say that a real sheet can grow without limits, or that an archive naturally generates data at a fixed rate.

| Rule card | Operation at the end of each round |
| --- | --- |
| Batch | Add 100 MB, regardless of the current size |
| Proportion | Retain the current size and add 10% of it |
| Replacement | Replace the current size with 10% of it |

### When to use these definitions

Use them before accepting a claim like “it grows by ten percent.” Ask: ten percent of which quantity, applied when, with what else added or removed? Without those details, the formula is still waiting for its instructions.

## Same first gain, different later bases

Start both archives at **1000 MB**. Batch adds **100 MB per round**. Proportion multiplies by **1.10 per round**. No other data arrives or is deleted; there is no capacity limit in this exercise. Updates use unrounded quantities.

| Completed rounds | Fixed addition, MB | Fixed factor, MB | Gain in this factor round, MB |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|0|1000|1000|—|
|1|1100|1100|100|
|2|1200|1210|110|
|3|1300|1331|121|
|4|1400|1464.10|133.10|
|5|1500|1610.51|146.41|

The second multiplicative update is **1100×1.10=1210 MB**. Its gain is 110 MB because the previous amount is 1100 MB. Batch still adds 100 MB. The amount added by Proportion grows while its rate stays constant. Batch keeps its added amount constant, while that addition becomes a smaller percentage of its current size.

The first update matches because we chose **100=0.10×1000**. It is a designed comparison, not a rule that all linear and compound processes begin together. If a third rule added 200 MB per round, it would reach 2000 MB after five rounds, above 1610.51 MB. A compound rule does not beat every possible straight line over every finite horizon.

### When to use this comparison

Use matched rules to expose what changes the next base. Keep the starting amount, horizon, update timing and outside flows consistent. Otherwise a difference in the final result might answer a different question.

## Write the rule, then compress it into a formula

Let $P$ be the initial amount and $n$ the number of completed rounds. We write $A_n$ for the amount after those rounds, so $A_0=P$.

For a fixed added amount $d$:

$$A_{n+1}=A_n+d,\qquad A_n=P+nd$$

For a constant factor $q$:

$$A_{n+1}=qA_n,\qquad A_n=Pq^n=P(1+r)^n$$

Here $r=q-1$ is the rate as a decimal. The course also uses $t$ for the number of periods; it plays the same role as $n$. The exponent counts **updates**, not the number of entries printed in a list. The list 1000,1100,1210 contains three states but only two updates, so the last value is $1000\times 1.10^2$.

The fixed-addition formula can also be written $P(1+rn)$ **if** you set $d=Pr$. That is how the grey chart line matches the first compound gain. An arbitrary fixed addition does not come with that same rate automatically.

[OpenStax’s arithmetic and geometric sequence definitions](https://openstax.org/books/college-algebra-2e/pages/9-3-geometric-sequences) distinguish a common difference from a common ratio. Do not confuse the amount in one term with the cumulative sum of several terms: growing step lengths and the total distance walked are different quantities.

### A factor can also shrink the amount

For a positive start, a factor above one increases it; one leaves it unchanged; a positive factor below one reduces it. For example, **1000×0.90=900**, then **900×0.90=810**. There is no upward-growing curve in that example. A growing debt can be harmful while its numerical factor exceeds one; “bad for somebody” and “negative percentage change” are different claims.

## A rate belongs to its period

A **2% monthly rate** means multiplying by **1.02 once per month**. Three monthly updates form one quarter in this example:

| Completed months | Amount from 1000 MB |
| --- | --- |
|0|1000|
|1|1020|
|2|1040.40|
|3|1061.208|

The quarter’s factor is $1.02^3=1.061208$. Its percentage growth is $(1.061208-1)\times 100=6.1208\%$. Simply adding the three rates gives 6%, which belongs to a different calculation.

If the same monthly rule continued for twelve updates, the annual factor would be $1.02^{12}\approx1.26824179456$, or about 26.8242% growth. This is not a promise about future data production. It answers a conditional question about a repeated rule. [BEA’s explanation of annualized rates](https://www.bea.gov/help/faq/122) makes that distinction explicit: annualization compounds a shorter-period rate under a continued-pace assumption.

| Monthly-rate answer | Quarter’s percentage growth |
| --- | --- |
| Pine |1.061208%|
| Oak |6%|
| Maple |24%|
| Elm |6.1208%|

### When converting periods

Match the rate to the number of updates. An effective monthly rate cannot generally be multiplied by twelve to obtain an effective annual rate, nor can an effective annual rate simply be divided by twelve to obtain an equivalent monthly rate. Specify the update convention and the horizon first.

## Variable factors: multiply what actually happens

A multiplicative process can have a different rate in each period. Its final amount is the initial amount times **all the factors**:

$$A_n=Pq_1 q_2\cdots q_n$$

When every factor equals the same value $q$, the product becomes $Pq^n$. Our next constructed archive has two rounds:

| Schedule | First-round rate | Second-round rate | State after first round | Final amount |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Variable |20%|0%|1200 MB|1200 MB|
| Constant |10%|10%|1100 MB|1210 MB|

Both rate lists have **arithmetic mean 10%**: add the two rates and divide by two. Yet their factors give **1.20×1.00=1.20**, versus **1.10×1.10=1.21**. The resulting amounts differ by 10 MB. The arithmetic mean preserves a sum; it need not preserve a product.

### When to use the product

Use it for a known schedule of proportional changes. Listing the actual factors helps catch a missed period or a factor applied to the wrong base. Do not replace them with an average merely because the average is easier to announce.

## What an equivalent average does, and does not, mean

The **equivalent constant factor** is the factor that, repeated for the same number of periods, preserves the known product. For two periods, it is the square root of the product: the number that multiplies by itself to give that product.

For the variable schedule above:

$$q_{eq}=\sqrt{1.20}\approx 1.095445115,\qquad r_{eq}=q_{eq}-1$$

That is about **9.5445% per round**, not the arithmetic average 10%. Repeating that equivalent factor twice would reach 1200 MB from 1000 MB. It would not reproduce the actual first-round amount 1200 MB. [USDA’s compound-period growth definition](https://apps.fas.usda.gov/gats/calculations.aspx) likewise describes an evenly distributed rate matching a known interval’s endpoints.

This is a description of the known span. It does not show that its actual rate was constant, or that its future rate will equal the equivalent average. Even a series created by fixed additions can be rewritten as changing ratios between successive positive values. Ratios are calculable; the generating rule still needs evidence.

### When to use an equivalent rate

Use it to describe a completed span on a common per-period basis. Keep the actual path when timing, thresholds or intermediate quantities matter. Do not call an equivalent rate a forecast without a separate justification.

### When to apply this model

Before extending a process, specify its operation, unit, period, outside flows and horizon. Check capacity and whether the supposed rate has evidence behind it. “Knowledge compounds” can be a useful analogy for reinforcement; it does not define a measured knowledge unit growing at a guaranteed percentage.
