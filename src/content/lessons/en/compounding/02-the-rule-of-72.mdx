A shortcut says “about nine periods.” The counter after nine updates reads just under twice its starting value. Both can be correct: a useful estimate need not identify the first completed update that meets a target. We will keep the shortcut and check the boundary.

All quantities below are constructed mathematical examples. Each stated factor stays fixed for its stated period. There are no outside additions, withdrawals, fees or rate changes. Values are not observed returns or a forecast. Fractions describe the updates; displayed decimal values may be rounded.

## Give the rate a period

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="Before using a doubling shortcut, what must the growth description specify?"
  options={[{"text":"A constant effective rate and the period to which it applies.","correct":true},{"text":"Only the starting quantity and its unit."},{"text":"Only the largest value visible on a graph."},{"text":"An average rate without how individual updates work."}]}
  explanation="The shortcut describes repeated growth under one constant effective factor. Its time unit follows the stated period."
  pretest={true}
/>

An **effective rate per period** is the proportional change after one complete period, including whatever compounding that period’s definition includes. Let $R$ be the percentage number and $r=R/100$ its decimal form. At 8% per period, $R=8$ and $r=0.08=2/25$. The update factor is $1+r=27/25$. A positive initial quantity $P$ follows

$$A_n=P(1+r)^n.$$

Here $n$ counts completed periods. If the period is a month, ten updates mean ten months; if it is a year, they mean ten years. A rate quoted for one interval cannot be silently attached to another. The same relative target, such as twice the start, cancels $P$: doubling requires $(1+r)^n\geq2$, whatever the positive starting amount.

The factor, period and treatment of outside flows make this a defined model. A label such as “average growth” supplies too little information to replace a changing sequence by a constant factor.

Use this setup when someone offers a doubling estimate. Ask for the effective factor and period first. If rates vary, return to the product of their factors from the previous lesson.

## Use the shortcut as an estimate

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="A mental shortcut returns nine. What does that result establish on its own?"
  options={[{"text":"That the ninth completed update must reach the exact target."},{"text":"An approximate time under the shortcut’s stated conditions.","correct":true},{"text":"That the rate can change without affecting the result."},{"text":"A guarantee about a future measured quantity."}]}
  explanation="A shortcut is an approximation. Its error and the availability of fractional periods matter when a decision has a boundary."

The **rule of 72** estimates doubling time for constant positive growth:

$$T_{72}=\frac{72}{R}.$$

Use the percentage number: at 8%, calculate $72/8=9$, not $72/(2/25)$. The answer has the same time unit as the rate’s period. Investor.gov presents this as an [approximate doubling shortcut](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/information/youth/teachers-classroom-resources/what-compound-interest).

For a calculated crossing time, solve $(1+r)^t=2$. The **natural logarithm**, written $\ln$, tells you the exponent needed when the base is the number $e$, approximately 2.718. Its power rule gives $t\ln(1+r)=\ln2$, hence

$$t_* = \frac{\ln2}{\ln(1+r)},\qquad r>0.$$

This permits a real-valued $t$: for example, a number between nine and ten. It interpolates the exponential formula between updates. It does not establish that an account, a counter or a machine actually updates at fractional periods. OpenStax derives [doubling time for an exponential model](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/2-8-exponential-growth-and-decay); the growth constant in its continuous notation is $k=\ln(1+r)$ for this interpolation.

The table compares times, not promised results. Its decimals are rounded. Near zero growth, $\ln(1+r)$ approaches $r$ in relative terms, giving the approximation $100\ln2/R$, whose numerator is about 69.3. Seventy-two is convenient because several small whole numbers divide it evenly: 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12 are divisors. That list is not a multiplication giving 72, nor evidence of why someone historically chose the shortcut. Neither constant is exact for every positive rate.

### When to use it

Use the shortcut for scale: roughly ten stated periods, rather than a precise completed update. Use the defined model when the difference between adjacent periods changes a decision.

## Check the first completed crossing

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="For the eight-percent case, the calculated time lies just above nine. Which completed-period test decides the first crossing?"
  options={[{"text":"Compare only the rounded shortcut with the starting amount."},{"text":"Compare the ninth and tenth updated quantities with twice the start.","correct":true},{"text":"Treat a fractional period as an update that must be available."},{"text":"Round the percentage itself to the nearest whole target."}]}
  explanation="Under positive constant growth, quantities increase each step. The first completed crossing is the first integer whose updated quantity meets the target."
  pretest={true}
/>

Start at 100 model units and target at least 200. With factor $27/25$, the ninth update gives

$$A_9=100\left(\frac{27}{25}\right)^9\approx199.900463<200.$$

The tenth gives

$$A_{10}=100\left(\frac{27}{25}\right)^{10}\approx215.892500>200.$$

So the first completed crossing is **10**, although the shortcut is **9** and the interpolated crossing is about **9.006468**. These answer different questions. In general, for this positive constant-growth model, the first completed crossing is the least integer $n$ satisfying $(1+r)^n\geq2$, equivalently the ceiling of $t_*$. A **ceiling** is the smallest integer at least as large as a number. Exact powers settle a boundary that rounded decimal times can obscure.

Try also the 24% case in the figure. Its factor is $31/25$: after three updates the quantity is exactly 190.6624, and after four it is exactly 236.421376. The shortcut’s three periods do not identify the first completed crossing there either.

### When to use it

Use completed-period comparisons for a deadline, a count of updates or any threshold assessed only after a full step. A real-valued time belongs to an interpolation unless the application supplies continuous updates.

## Separate direct decline from a price reciprocal

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="A quantity falls by eight percent each period. A different case has prices rising by eight percent. What needs checking before treating their halving times as identical?"
  options={[{"text":"Whether both formulas contain the same percentage sign."},{"text":"Whether both quantities use the same update factor.","correct":true},{"text":"Whether either case has a larger starting number."},{"text":"Whether the shortcut returns a whole number."}]}
  explanation="Direct percentage decline and purchasing power under rising prices use different factors. The repeated factor determines the model."
  pretest={true}
/>

For **direct decline** by 8%, retain $1-2/25=23/25$ of the current quantity. From 100 units, the target is at most 50. The interpolated halving time is

$$t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln(1/2)}{\ln(23/25)}\approx8.312950.$$

After eight updates the quantity is about 51.321887; after nine it is about 47.216136. Its first completed halving is **9**. The logarithm formula is valid because the factor lies strictly between zero and one.

Now stipulate that one unchanged basket’s price increases by 8% each period, while nominal cash stays fixed. **Purchasing power** here means the number of those baskets that cash can buy. Normalize its initial value to 100. Each update retains $25/27$ of that purchasing power, the reciprocal of the price factor $27/25$:

$$Q_n=100\left(\frac{25}{27}\right)^n.$$

This is not a direct 8% decline: the retained factor is different. Its decline fraction is $1-25/27=2/27$, not $2/25$. After nine updates the index is about 50.024897, still above half; after ten it is about 46.319349. Its first completed halving is **10**. Price doubling and this reciprocal purchasing-power halving share the same crossing condition. The model assumes a fixed basket and cash amount, rather than forecasting actual prices.

### When to use it

Use a reciprocal when your quantity is explicitly a fixed numerator divided by a growing denominator. For a directly shrinking quantity, use its own retained factor. At zero growth the positive quantity does not double; a positive factor below one has no future doubling of that quantity under the fixed rule.

## Read the doubling ladder with an exact model

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="In a model that doubles after every equal block of time, why do later blocks add larger amounts?"
  options={[{"text":"The update rule activates only after several blocks."},{"text":"Later blocks secretly last longer than earlier blocks."},{"text":"The same factor acts on a larger current quantity.","correct":true},{"text":"The rate increases each time the quantity doubles."}]}
  explanation="With a constant doubling factor, the addition over a block equals the quantity at that block’s start. Larger starting quantities give larger additions."
  pretest={true}
/>

Use a separate ideal model: start at 1,000 units and **double exactly after each block of nine periods**. One step in the next figure means one whole nine-period block. This is not the 8% model. Its update is $B_{j+1}=2B_j$, so $B_j=1000\cdot2^j$.

At block four, corresponding to period 36, the quantity is 16,000. At block five, period 45, it is 32,000. That last block adds 16,000 units; the entire growth before it was $16000-1000=15000$. The update rule never changed. In a general positive constant-factor model, the next absolute increment is $A_{n+1}-A_n=rA_n$: a fixed percentage can correspond to a growing absolute addition.

The apparent “hockey stick” depends on the plotted scale and interval. It does not identify a magic activation point, nor establish that a measured process will keep the same factor.

## Transfer the calculation to new inputs

A constructed counter starts at 250 units, multiplies by $28/25$ after each completed period, and must reach at least 500 units. There are no outside flows and the factor stays fixed. The effective rate is 12% per period. Work out the shortcut estimate and first completed crossing before opening the calculation.

<summary>Compare your calculation</summary>

The shortcut is $72/12=6$ periods. But $(28/25)^6<2$ and $(28/25)^7>2$, so the first completed crossing is **7**. Its interpolated time is about 6.116255 periods. Changing only the initial quantity while keeping this factor and a doubling target would leave the crossing count unchanged. Here both the factor and initial quantity changed from the earlier case, so calculate from the new data.

If a future rate changes or an outside flow occurs, this constant-factor result no longer describes that sequence. A calculation under stipulated updates is not evidence that those updates will persist.
