A test machine changes a quantity by multiplication. You can let it complete every update, or tell it to stop updating after the quantity crosses a boundary. Before choosing, you need to know what “stop” does, when it happens and which possible paths you are averaging.

This is a **constructed model**, measured in **model units**. Its quantities and update rules are stipulated. Nothing is added or withdrawn. A stopped quantity stays where it stopped, with no storage loss or cost. Those assumptions let us inspect the arithmetic before importing a real-world story.

## Count paths before averaging endpoints

A **factor** multiplies the current quantity. Our machine starts at **100 units**. At each completed step, U multiplies by **3/2 = 1.50** and D multiplies by **3/5 = 0.60**. U increases the quantity by 50%; D reduces it by 40%. These are different proportional changes, not opposite percentages.

Each next letter has probability **1/2**, independently of the previous letters: learning the past does not change those declared chances. A **potential path** is a complete sequence of letters that could occur. With two steps there are four paths. Each has probability $(1/2)(1/2)=1/4$. We call this probability its **weight** in the calculation.

Think of four equally weighted tickets, one for each complete sequence. Two tickets may name the same final quantity; that does not turn them into one ticket. The tickets are a counting aid, not information about which future path the operator will see.

For UD, the first step produces $100\times1.50=150$, and the second produces $150\times0.60=90$. DU also ends at 90: $100\times0.60\times1.50=90$. There is no stopping rule yet, so all declared factors apply.

The **weighted mean**, also called the **expected value**, multiplies each possible final quantity by its weight and adds the contributions. Equal weights let us add the four endpoints and divide by four:

$$
\frac{225+90+90+36}{4}=110.25\text{ units}.
$$

Grouping the two 90s gives the same calculation: $225/4+90/2+36/4=56.25+45+9=110.25$. Averaging only the three distinct values would instead give $(225+90+36)/3=117$. That silently replaces the path weights with a different model.

### When to use it

Use a weighted mean when one result can arise through several paths. First define the complete outcomes and their weights. Counting each distinct result once is justified only if those distinct results really are equally likely.

## A mean can rise while most paths finish lower

Now allow **four steps**, with the same independent U/D probabilities and factors. The **horizon** is the number of steps we evaluate: four here, even if the machine stops updating sooner. There are $2\times2\times2\times2=16$ potential sequences, each with weight $1/16$. The count doubles at each additional letter. We group them by their number of U letters only because, without stopping or outside flows, multiplication gives the same endpoint regardless of order.

For two U letters, choose their two positions among the four. The six sequences are UUDD, UDUD, UDDU, DUUD, DUDU and DDUU; each ends at **81**. Across the five groups, the counts **1+4+6+4+1=16** recover the full catalogue.

The weighted sum is

$$
\frac{506.25+4(202.5)+6(81)+4(32.4)+12.96}{16}=121.550625\text{ units}.
$$

The **mean factor for one step** is $(1.50+0.60)/2=1.05$. Independence also lets us check the four-step mean as $100(1.05)^4=121.550625$. This is a shortcut for this model’s weighted mean, not the factor sequence that every individual path experiences. A specific path still uses its actual U and D factors.

The groups with zero, one or two U letters end below the starting 100. Their counts add to **1+4+6=11**. Thus the mean exceeds the start even though **11/16** of the endpoints are lower. The large endpoints pull the weighted mean upward. Indeed, no path ends at the mean itself.

### When to use it

Use the full distribution—the quantities and their weights—when a mean seems to answer a different question from “how often does it finish below the start?” A mean, a probability of a lower endpoint and a minimum describe different properties. None supplies the other two.

## Specify what happens at the stopping boundary

A **policy** is a rule for what the machine does at each step. Compare the original policy, **apply every factor**, with a second policy:

1. Start at 100 units, above the boundary of 60.
2. Apply the next U or D factor to the current quantity.
3. Observe the updated quantity. If it is **60 or less**, stop updating permanently.
4. Retain that attained quantity for all remaining steps. Otherwise continue, up to four completed steps.

This is an **observed stopping rule**: the decision uses the quantity reached so far, without seeing future letters. The boundary is inclusive: reaching exactly 60 stops the process. Stopping happens **after** the update, so crossing below 60 is possible. It is not a promise to preserve at least 60, nor an instruction to restore any lower quantity to 60.

For two steps, UU gives $100\to150\to225$ and UD gives $100\to150\to90$. Any path beginning D reaches **60 after the first step** and freezes there. Thus DU and DD both end at 60 under the rule, although their unused second letters differ.

### When to use it

Before evaluating a boundary rule, specify the observation timing, inclusive or strict comparison, retained quantity and all later updates or costs. A rule that clips the amount at a floor, sells for a fee or permits restarting is a different model and needs another calculation.

This keeps the underlying uncertainty fixed: the rule changes which factors are applied, rather than quietly changing how likely a sequence is. The operator never receives this whole future catalogue as advance information; it is our bookkeeping for comparing all possible cases.

### When to use it

Use common potential paths to compare rules under the same uncertainty. State the objective and horizon before calling one policy better. Keep weights attached when several potential futures produce one observed stopped history.

### When to use it

Repeat the audit when factors, probabilities, horizon, boundary or post-stop treatment change. Equal means can coexist with different distributions. A calculation for the first machine cannot settle the second by analogy alone.

An audit needs a starting quantity and unit, an update period, possible factors and their probabilities, a finite horizon, the information available at each decision, and the complete stopping and retention instructions. Enumerate full potential paths, execute the rule on each, and only then group endpoints with their combined weights. Compare the measures that answer the stated objective.

The four-step limit is part of our model. A longer horizon, dependent letters, costs or a revised factor distribution requires a new calculation. None of these finite results promises a realized outcome or validates a growth law in a business, skill or account. A stopped amount in this model remains positive; it is neither reset to zero nor replenished to the boundary.
