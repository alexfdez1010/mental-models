This is the final exam for **Compounding**. It pulls the whole course together: the distinction between adding amounts and applying factors to a base, with the period and constant-rate conditions, the formula $A = P(1+r)^t$ and what each symbol does, the Rule of 72 and the doubling time it hands you in your head, the ladder of doublings that explains the famous hockey stick, the effect of withdrawals, drawdowns and resets on the base the cruel loss-recovery asymmetry where a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to get even, the difference between a proportional change’s direction and whether it harms someone, and finally how the starting amount and number of periods interact in a finite comparison. Reason each one through; several look easy until you spot the trap — assuming a 50% gain undoes a 50% loss, that the curve "kicks in" late, or that doubling time depends on the size of your pile.

<Callout variant="success" title="Key takeaways">
Compounding applies proportional changes to a base that previous changes have already altered. Fixed addition and a constant factor are different rules: one preserves the amount added, the other its share. Variable rates require their factors to be multiplied, with explicit units, periods and outside flows. The calculation depends on those rules; a graph’s shape does not establish a future law. The **Rule of 72** (doubling time ≈ 72 ÷ rate, set by the rate alone) turns the curve into a
**ladder of doublings** and reveals the hockey stick for what it is: a constant percentage that
only *looks* like it erupts late, because a doubling of a big number is big. The magic is
**fragile** — withdrawals, drawdowns, and resets change the base, and equal, opposite, nonzero percentages never
cancel (a −50% loss needs a +100% gain, erasing ~9 years at 8%), so the first job is to
**survive, then compound**, leaving a margin of safety against the rare wipeout. A debt balance can increase numerically and harm the debtor; that harm does not change the rate’s sign. A label such as technical debt needs its own quantity and numerical rule. The archive card compares starting amount, factor and rounds together; no position in the formula gives time universal priority. The one-percent-per-round counter is a stipulated model, not a measurement of skills or habits. *What here is feeding on its
own output, and which direction is it pointed?* is the whole course in one question.
</Callout>
