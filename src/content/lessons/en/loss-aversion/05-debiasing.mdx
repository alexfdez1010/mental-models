Recognizing a possible bias gives you a question to investigate. It does not automatically tell you which option to choose. A good audit retains the feasible alternatives, the objective, the uncertainty and the consequences of being wrong.

We will use a constructed additive credit process to check a popular claim: “Evaluate more favorable plays together and the chance of a total loss always falls.” The word *always* is doing work the arithmetic has not agreed to.

## Define what is being aggregated

**Broad framing** evaluates a set of decisions jointly; **narrow framing** evaluates them separately. To calculate an aggregate, first specify what combines, over what horizon, and with what dependence. A basket is useful as an analogy only after you know what each item contributes.

In this invented process, each play adds $-20$ or $+30$ credits with equal probability. There are no fees, changing stakes, stopping rules or resource restrictions in this first calculation. All selected plays occur. **Independence** means one play's outcome does not change the others' probabilities.

One play has monetary expectation $(-20+30)/2=5$ and probability $1/2$ of a negative total. “Negative” here means total change strictly below zero at the chosen endpoint, not a loss at any earlier moment.

With two independent plays there are four equally likely sequences:

| Sequence | Total change | Probability |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Lower, lower | −40 | 1/4 |
| Lower, higher | 10 | 1/4 |
| Higher, lower | 10 | 1/4 |
| Higher, higher | 60 | 1/4 |

The total is negative only in the first sequence. Its probability is $1/4$. The expected total is $(-40+10+10+60)/4=10$. The mean doubled; the worst total also changed from minus twenty to minus forty.

Use aggregation to expose a joint distribution, not to hide a downside behind its average.

## One extra play can raise the finite loss probability

For three independent plays there are eight equally likely sequences. Group them by the number of higher outcomes. Three sequences have exactly one higher outcome; another three have exactly two.

| Higher outcomes | Total change | Number of sequences | Probability |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | −60 | 1 | 1/8 |
| 1 | −10 | 3 | 3/8 |
| 2 | 40 | 3 | 3/8 |
| 3 | 90 | 1 | 1/8 |

The negative probability is $(1+3)/8=1/2$. The mean is $(-60-30+120+90)/8=15$. Across one, two and three plays, expectations rise five, ten, fifteen; negative-total probabilities go **one half, one quarter, one half**. The latter sequence is not monotone.

This does not disprove long-run concentration results under their assumptions. It disproves a claim about **every finite horizon**. A long-run result also does not choose a useful deadline, price the worst case or establish that the decision-maker can complete the sequence.

## Dependence can remove the diversification comparison

Now change just dependence for two plays: both share one fair outcome. With probability one half both are lower; otherwise both are higher. Each play separately still has the same two amounts and probabilities as before.

| Joint law for two plays | Possible totals | Negative probability | Expected total |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Independent | −40, 10, 60 | 1/4 | 10 |
| Perfectly linked | −40, 60 | 1/2 | 10 |

The mean is the same, but the distributions differ. Independence was part of the earlier probability calculation, not a reward for putting two rows in a spreadsheet. This constructed comparison does not claim every dependence pattern has this effect.

## Compare the actual alternatives and constraints

Stopping or a severe outcome does not by itself make an expected value undefined. Its existence depends on the resulting distribution; the finite, bounded cases here have an expectation. What changes is the process being averaged and whether that average answers the objective. A rule that stops early changes which later outcomes can occur; a resource constraint can make a proposed sequence infeasible.

For example, suppose the permitted total loss in the constructed credit exercise is at most forty. Every permitted plan must satisfy this bound in every possible outcome. Two independent plays have worst total minus forty; three have worst total minus sixty. Under that *endpoint* restriction, the second sequence is not permitted. If the restriction instead applies after every play, compute the intermediate paths too. These are stated constraints, not a fitted loss coefficient.

Before recommending aggregation, write the objective, feasible actions, horizon, joint law, costs, stopping conditions and consequence limits. A decision to keep the certain alternative can follow from those facts or from ordinary risk preferences; calling it loss aversion does not settle which explanation applies.

## Audit a reference without pretending to control it

A **reference point** is the baseline used to code an outcome as a gain or loss. Naming it can expose an assumption. Merely substituting a happier number does not establish that a person changed their reference, or that a preferred action became better on the chosen objective.

Write both final options before reframing. If one fictional option leaves eighty credits and another one hundred, changing the labels cannot reverse that final-credit ordering. A reference-dependent score may rank risky distributions differently after a reference change; the next workshop computes the certain option too.

For a past expense, separate the unrecoverable amount from consequences still changeable now. A paid entry fee may be sunk, but a refundable deposit, a cancellation cost or a remaining service can affect the current comparison. “Would I choose this today?” is a prompt to construct that comparison, not a complete decision rule.

## Precommit to a reviewable procedure

**Precommitment** chooses a policy before the immediate choice. Its quality depends on the policy. A bad rule executed automatically is still a bad rule. Writing it earlier does not prove that it meets the objective or removes a bias.

For the credit exercise, a procedure can state: calculate the complete endpoint distribution, reject sequences violating the declared loss limit, compare the remaining options using the chosen criterion, and reopen the model if dependence changes. This precommits to a checkable process without inventing a universal order to accept risky options.

Use such a procedure for repeated comparisons with known objectives. Preserve review triggers for changed facts and exceptions that matter. Locking an action against all later evidence would remove useful judgment along with momentary reactions.

## Recall the audit before the workshop

Recover the negative-total probabilities for one, two and three independent plays. Explain why the two-play mean survives the dependence change but its loss probability does not. Then state one thing a reference audit cannot infer from a single refusal.
