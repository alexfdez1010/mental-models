A possible loss can weigh more heavily than an equal gain relative to a person's reference point. **Loss aversion** names that asymmetry. It belongs to a larger theory of risky choice; it does not mean that every cautious decision is a mistake.

The course begins by distinguishing a numerical comparison, a valuation model and an inference about a person. Keeping those separate makes the model more useful when a tempting story arrives with a very confident coefficient attached.

## A monetary mean is one criterion

An invented one-period menu offers a certain final amount of one hundred credits or equally likely final amounts of eighty and one hundred thirty. There are no fees or later plays in this example.

| Alternative | Monetary expectation of final credits |
| --- | ---: |
| Certain one hundred | 100 |
| Equal chances of eighty or one hundred thirty | 105 |

The risky calculation is $(80+130)/2=105$. Its mean exceeds the certain amount by five. That is a correct accounting result. Whether someone prefers the distribution depends on how they value outcomes and on any relevant constraints. A larger mean is not a guarantee about the realized outcome.

Use expectation when the objective calls for a probability-weighted average. To explain or assess a choice, also state the alternatives, probabilities and objective. Positive monetary expectation alone does not diagnose a refusal as irrational.

## Distinguish reference, curvature and weighting

A **reference point** is a baseline used to code an outcome as a gain or loss. Final credits of one hundred are a change of plus ten from a reference of ninety, but minus thirty from a reference of one hundred thirty. The final amount has not changed.

A **value function** maps such changes to a valuation score. **Loss aversion** concerns the asymmetry between equivalent gains and losses. **Diminishing sensitivity** concerns how the slope changes within the gain or loss region. They are distinct features: a piecewise-linear function can have a steeper loss arm without curvature within either arm.

**Probability weighting** assigns decision weights to outcomes; those weights need not be the stated ordinary probabilities. It is distinct from the value function and need not mean the person believes a different numerical probability. The workshop isolates reference and loss weighting while retaining ordinary probabilities.

**Risk aversion** describes preferring a certain amount to a risky distribution with the same monetary mean, under the relevant comparison. It is not synonymous with a loss coefficient. A concave utility of final amounts can favor a certain option without recoding outcomes around a reference.

## Read the evidence within its scope

[Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 paper](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf) develops prospect theory as a descriptive account of choices under risk, with reference coding, a value function and decision weights. The [1992 cumulative version](https://christosaioannou.com/Advances_in_Prospect_Theory.pdf) extends the weighting treatment. Its fitted median loss coefficient was about two and a quarter within a specified parametric analysis; it is not a universal human constant.

A model can generate a testable prediction once its inputs are declared. Evidence about a person requires observations capable of distinguishing that model from alternatives. A demonstration on a website computes its chosen rule; it does not measure the visitor or guarantee how wording changes behavior.

Use the theory to form and test explanations of a choice representation. Keep observed choices, calculated consequences and recommendations separate.
