A report says a person refused a risky option with a higher monetary average. Its author concludes: “Loss aversion, coefficient two.” The report has assigned a mechanism and an exact coefficient without stating the reference point.

This workshop audits that inference using invented credits and known probabilities. You will calculate a declared score, compare it with a certain alternative, and show why another model can produce the same refusal. These credits are a mathematical example, not a proposed investment or gamble.

## Keep the final outcomes visible

There is one decision and one outcome date. The certain alternative ends with one hundred credits. The risky alternative ends with eighty or one hundred thirty, each with probability one half. Nothing else is paid or received: no fee, later rounds, resource constraint or ruin process is included.

| Alternative | Final credits | Probability |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Certain | 100 | 1 |
| Risky, lower outcome | 80 | 1/2 |
| Risky, higher outcome | 130 | 1/2 |

The **monetary expectation** is the probability-weighted average of final credits. The risky value is $(80+130)/2=105$; the certain value is $100$. This criterion ranks the risky option five credits higher. It does not describe the distribution as certain, and it does not oblige every decision-maker to maximize that criterion.

Think of the reference as a zero mark on a ruler. Moving the mark changes recorded distances, not the objects' locations. The analogy stops at the score: the valuation rule can weight negative distances differently.

Use final-outcome accounting first when a description emphasizes “gain” or “loss.” Otherwise a changed outcome can be mistaken for a changed reference.

## Declare a restricted score before using it

Let $R$ be a shared reference in credits and $x=w-R$ the change from that reference when final credits are $w$. A positive $x$ is called a gain; a negative $x$, a loss. Our invented **valuation score** is

$$v(x)=\begin{cases}x,&x\ge0,\\ \lambda x,&x<0.\end{cases}$$

The **loss coefficient** $\lambda$ weights negative changes. We will use values at least one. If $\lambda=1$, the score is linear everywhere; if it exceeds one, the loss arm is steeper. The units are valuation-score units, not credits or measured feelings.

For a certain amount $s$ and equally likely risky amounts $a,b$, compare

$$S=v(s-R),\qquad T=\frac{v(a-R)+v(b-R)}{2},\qquad D=T-S.$$

The declared rule prefers the risky option if $D>0$, the certain one if $D<0$, and is indifferent if $D=0$. **Indifference** means this rule ties; it supplies no tie-breaking behavior.

Start with $R=100$, $\lambda=2$. Compute every outcome, including the certain one:

| Outcome | Final credits | Change from reference | Score | Probability | Weighted score |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Certain | 100 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| Risky, lower | 80 | −20 | −40 | 1/2 | −20 |
| Risky, higher | 130 | 30 | 30 | 1/2 | 15 |

Thus $S=0$, $T=-20+15=-5$ and $D=-5$. The monetary expectation is still $105$; the score ranks the certain option higher. The two calculations use different criteria.

This is a **piecewise-linear reference model with ordinary probabilities**. It omits the curved value function, probability weighting and editing in the broader prospect-theory treatments. The earlier curve explorer includes curvature; interpret its separate demonstration using its own assumptions.

## Solve the coefficient threshold exactly

Keep $R=100$ and every final outcome fixed. The certain score remains zero. The risky lower change is $-20$, so its score is $-20\lambda$; the higher change scores $30$. Hence

$$D=\frac{-20\lambda+30}{2}=15-10\lambda.$$

Setting $D=0$ gives $\lambda=3/2$. Below that threshold the rule prefers risky; above it, certain. Equality is a tie, not a small preference you may round away.

| Coefficient | Certain score | Risky expected score | Risky minus certain |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 0 | 5 | 5 |
| 3/2 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 2 | 0 | −5 | −5 |
| 9/4 | 0 | −15/2 | −15/2 |
| 3 | 0 | −15 | −15 |

A refusal, even if this exact rule and reference were known to apply, would at most constrain the coefficient under a stated tie policy. It would not reveal its exact value. Without those assumptions the inference is weaker still.

Use a threshold when you want to learn whether uncertainty about a parameter changes the ranking. Do not borrow the historical fitted median as a substitute for the calculation.

## Move the reference for both alternatives

Fix $\lambda=2$ and move $R$ to ninety. The certain amount is now a gain of ten, scoring ten. The risky changes are minus ten and plus forty; their scores are minus twenty and plus forty. Their mean is ten. **The comparison is ten against ten, not ten against zero.**

| Reference | Certain change | Certain score | Risky expected score | Risky minus certain |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 80 | 20 | 20 | 25 | 5 |
| 90 | 10 | 10 | 10 | 0 |
| 100 | 0 | 0 | −5 | −5 |
| 130 | −30 | −60 | −50 | 10 |

At $R=80$ all changes are nonnegative, so the score is linear over these outcomes and the monetary advantage remains five. At $R=130$ all are nonpositive: $S=-30\lambda$, $T=-25\lambda$, and $D=5\lambda$. With the positive coefficients allowed here, risky ranks higher. Calling every outcome a loss has not made the curve convex; this restricted rule is linear within each arm.

The final options and their monetary expectations did not change. Only a model input changed. The visual lets you inspect that input; it does not demonstrate that a person will adopt whichever reference an author selects.

Use a shared-reference table when a proposed reframe seems to create value from nothing. Check the certain alternative, not just the conspicuous gamble.

## A different model can refuse the same option

**Identification** asks whether the observed choices distinguish one explanation from alternatives. One refusal does not. Construct a different model: an increasing, smoothly concave utility function of **final credits**, fixed on the interval from eighty to one hundred thirty. Utility is a stipulated desirability score, not a direct emotional measurement.

| Final credits | Fixed utility |
| ---: | ---: |
| 80 | 0 |
| 100 | 24 |
| 130 | 45 |

The certain utility is $24$. Expected risky utility is $(0+45)/2=45/2$, which is lower. This model also refuses the risky option, without a reference parameter or a gain/loss recoding.

<details>
<summary>Check one smooth function producing the table</summary>

On this interval only, define

$$u(w)=49-\left(\frac{150-w}{10}\right)^2.$$

Substitution gives $49-7^2=0$, $49-5^2=24$ and $49-2^2=45$. For a calculus check, $u'(w)=(150-w)/50>0$ on the stated interval and $u''(w)=-1/50<0$. It is increasing and strictly concave there. No extrapolation outside that interval is used.

</details>

This is a counterexample to a diagnostic inference, not evidence that real people use this function. It does not prove loss aversion absent either. To estimate a reference-dependent model, you need sufficiently informative choices, justified reference assumptions, probabilities the participant understands, and comparisons against plausible alternatives. Curvature, decision weights, constraints and noisy responses can otherwise be confounded.

Use a competing model when an observed choice is being treated as a psychological fingerprint.

## Transfer to a new outcome menu

Keep the certain amount at one hundred. Replace the risky final credits with ninety-two and one hundred sixteen, each equally likely. Use $R=100$ and $\lambda=3/2$. Before opening the solution, calculate the monetary expectation, both scores and the coefficient that would make them tie.

<details>
<summary>Check the complete transfer calculation</summary>

The monetary expectation is $(92+116)/2=104$. The certain change is zero, so $S=0$. The risky changes are minus eight and plus sixteen. Their scores at the stated coefficient are minus twelve and plus sixteen, so $T=(-12+16)/2=2$ and $D=2$.

For any allowed coefficient at this reference,

$$D=\frac{-8\lambda+16}{2}=8-4\lambda.$$

The tie is at $\lambda=2$, different from the original menu's threshold. At $\lambda=9/4$, the difference is $-1$, while the monetary expectation remains $104$. A coefficient is not a universal haircut that you can apply to every menu without recalculating.

</details>

## Separate data, assumptions and conclusions

The papers that motivate this audit describe richer models. [Kahneman and Tversky, 1979](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf) distinguish coding relative to a reference, value and decision weights. [Tversky and Kahneman, 1992](https://christosaioannou.com/Advances_in_Prospect_Theory.pdf) use cumulative weighting and report fitted parameter medians under a specified form. Our linear rule deliberately isolates one relationship.

A complete report therefore names final outcomes and probabilities, the certain alternative, the chosen reference, coefficient and score rule, and whether each is observed, estimated or stipulated. A changed description is an intervention to test, not proof that a reference or preference changed. Record choices across informative comparisons and check whether competing models make different predictions.

The useful practical result can be a request for missing evidence rather than a forced label. “The ranking changes within the permitted reference range” is a reproducible model result. “This person's coefficient is two” needs a different argument.
