## Fix the menu before moving its zero

A **reference point** is the baseline used to code an outcome as a gain or loss. Think of moving the zero mark on a ruler: the recorded distances change while the objects stay in place. The analogy has a limit. Our valuation rule can treat negative distances differently from positive ones; a ruler does not.

There is one decision and one outcome date. Nothing else is paid or received: no entry fee, later plays, resource restriction or other consequence enters this invented example. Credits are teaching units.

### When to use this first step

Write final outcomes before discussing gains and losses. It prevents a different payment, probability or fee from being mistaken for a reference change. The old slogan 'same deal' needs an actual unchanged menu behind it.

## Give the curve and the loss multiplier different jobs

A **value function** maps a change from reference to a valuation score. We use this declared power form:

$$v(x)=\begin{cases}x^{\alpha},&x\ge0,\\-\lambda(-x)^{\alpha},&x<0,\end{cases}\qquad \alpha=0.88=\frac{22}{25},\quad \lambda=2.25=\frac94.$$

The exponent **alpha** controls curvature within each arm. **Lambda** multiplies the loss arm. Valuation-score units are arbitrary; they are not final credits or a physical measurement of pleasure and pain. The numbers resemble fitted medians reported in the 1992 paper, but here they are assumptions chosen for an inspectable example.

**Diminishing sensitivity** means that equal increments have less effect farther from the reference. A gain arm is **concave** when its successive slopes decrease. The loss arm is **convex** here: as losses get larger in magnitude, each further equal loss adds less score magnitude than the previous one. The following values are rounded to six decimals.

The first 50-credit gain adds about 31.267532 score units; the next 50 adds $57.543994-31.267532\approx26.276462$. On the loss side, the first 50 has magnitude about 70.351947; the next 50 adds about 59.122039. Those smaller successive increments show curvature. They are different from comparing a gain with a loss of equal size.

For any distance $a>0$, this particular function gives $-v(-a)/v(a)=\lambda$. Thus the 100-credit loss has 2.25 times the score magnitude of the 100-credit gain. That is the stipulated **loss asymmetry**. Setting lambda to one removes that asymmetry while leaving the exponent and curvature intact.

## Compute both alternatives with ordinary probabilities

For now, a risky prospect's score is the mean of its outcome scores using the stated physical probabilities. The certain score has weight one. Call the scores $S$ and $T$, and compare $D=T-S$. Positive $D$ ranks risky higher, negative ranks certain higher, and zero is indifference under this rule. A score is a criterion; it has not recorded anyone's behavior.

## Check what the multiplier cannot explain

Keep the main menu and ordinary probabilities but set lambda to one. At reference 200, the scores become $S=-50^{\alpha}\approx-31.267532$ and $T=-100^{\alpha}/2\approx-28.771997$. Risky still ranks higher. The gain-side scores did not change either. **Removing loss asymmetry has not removed the curvature comparison.**

More generally, when both alternatives have only losses or zero, a common positive lambda multiplies every contribution. It cannot change which of those scores is higher. This observation does not cover a mixed prospect containing both gains and losses, where lambda need not factor out.

For a second control, change the exponent to one while retaining lambda 2.25 and ordinary probabilities. This is a different, piecewise-linear rule. At reference 100, $S=50$ and $T=100/2=50$. At reference 200, $S=-2.25\times50=-112.5$ and $T=-2.25\times100/2=-112.5$. Both comparisons tie. A loss multiplier does not create within-arm curvature. The later workshop develops that linear form using a different menu; preserve its own inputs.

<details>
<summary>What does the often-drawn corner mean at zero?</summary>

For the power form used here, there are no two finite one-sided slopes at zero. With $h>0$, the gain-side secant slope from zero is $h^{\alpha}/h=h^{-3/25}$. The loss-side counterpart is $\lambda h^{-3/25}$. Both grow without bound as $h$ approaches zero. Away from zero, matched nonzero magnitudes can still be compared, and $-v(-a)/v(a)=\lambda$ for $a>0$.

Calling the drawing 'kinked' does not make lambda the ratio of two finite derivatives at zero. Its value is defined by the formula, not by measuring an angle on the screen. The function itself is continuous there because both scores approach zero.

</details>

## Transfer by changing weights rather than chances

So far each risky outcome score has been weighted by its physical probability. A **decision weight** is a coefficient in the valuation rule; it need not be a probability or a belief about how often an outcome occurs. In the broader prospect-theory accounts, value and weighting jointly affect rankings.

Keep the main menu, physical probabilities, exponent and lambda unchanged. The certain score still has weight one. Now stipulate weight $q=0.7=7/10$ for the single **nonzero outcome score** in each reference coding. The zero score contributes zero. At reference 100 the nonzero score belongs to final 200; at reference 200 it belongs to final 100. We have changed the rule's weighting, not the chance of those final amounts. This is an illustrative weight, not a fitted estimate or a complete cumulative-weighting function.

## Use the calculation to ask better empirical questions

Prospect theory comes from research on choice under risk. The [1979 paper](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf) distinguishes reference coding, value and decision weights, while qualifying the proposed shape. The [1992 cumulative paper](https://christosaioannou.com/Advances_in_Prospect_Theory.pdf) develops the weighting treatment and reports fitted parameter medians under a chosen functional form. Those authors discuss the limitations of estimating a complex model.

The exponent 0.88 and loss coefficient 2.25 are useful historical context. They are not universal individual constants. The example's references, weights and unchanged functional form across references were stipulated separately. Its algebra is not a measurement of a participant, an estimate of wording's effect or a recommendation to select the model's preferred action.

Use the function to formulate a checkable explanation when a risky choice may be represented as gains and losses. First keep the final menu visible. Then ask what reference, value shape and weights are justified, whether constraints or competing models explain the same choice, and which observations distinguish them. A changed label is an intervention to investigate, not proof that a reference changed.
