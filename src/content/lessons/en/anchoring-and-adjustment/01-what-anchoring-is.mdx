## What an anchor is, and what the effect describes

An **anchor** is a starting value considered when forming a numerical judgment. It may be supplied by a question, retrieved from memory or produced by an incomplete calculation. **Anchoring** describes a pattern in which judgments depend on that starting value. The usual pattern discussed here is **assimilation**: higher starting values are associated with higher estimates than lower starting values under a comparison procedure.

Think of a starting value as the first landmark on a walk. It can influence the route without being the destination. The analogy helps you ask where the journey began; it does not establish that every mind walks the same route or moves a fixed fraction toward the truth.

A number can also be useful evidence. A measured length from the correct plan deserves consideration; a raffle ticket does not acquire information about the cable because someone read it aloud. Even a relevant reference can receive too much weight. Relevance and appropriate use are separate questions.

### When to use this distinction

Use it when somebody says “the answer was high, therefore they were anchored.” High compared with what? Record the quantity, unit, starting value and comparison. A single high estimate can have several explanations, including relevant knowledge or an ordinary mistake.

## The wheel result: read the statistic correctly

Tversky and Kahneman’s [published report](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124) describes a wheel numbered from **0 to 100**. Participants first said whether the percentage of United Nations member countries that were African was above or below the displayed number, then estimated that percentage. Different groups received different starting numbers.

| Starting number | Reported median estimate |
| --- | --- |
| 10 | 25% |
| 65 | 45% |

A **median** is the middle value after ordering observations, or the average of the two middle values for an even-sized set. It is not generally the **mean**, which is the sum divided by the number of observations. The report gives medians here, not means.

The difference is **45 − 25 = 20 percentage points**. That is a contrast between group medians. It does not mean that every participant moved twenty points, that the same person supplied both answers, or that the unanchored answer would have been their midpoint. We also cannot reconstruct the original response distribution from those two summaries.

The wheel supplied no relevant geopolitical evidence. The report supports an influence of the comparison procedure on group judgments. It does not require us to claim that participants knew the wheel was manipulated. Nor do we need a present-day percentage to explain the historical contrast.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Do not manufacture the missing comparison">
An imagined unanchored answer is not an observed control group. It can illustrate a hypothesis if labelled as invented; it cannot explain how far a particular participant actually moved.
</Callout>

### When to use a reported median

Use it to describe the centre specified by a study. To compare means, spreads or individual changes, obtain those data rather than borrowing the median’s number and changing its name.

## A complete card: three summaries, three questions

The following records are **constructed for this lesson**. They are not the original wheel data, a new experiment or a prediction of how people will respond. Imagine two distinct groups estimating a cable’s length after comparing it with a ticket number. These are all the recorded estimates, in metres, already sorted. Each row is a different record; rows across groups are not matched people.

| Record | Low-ticket group: ticket 20 m | High-ticket group: ticket 80 m |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A / F | 30 | 35 |
| B / G | 40 | 50 |
| C / H | 40 | 60 |
| D / I | 50 | 70 |
| E / J | 90 | 85 |

The ticket is only a comparison cue expressed in metres; it is not a measurement. The low-group median is the third entry, **40 m**; the high-group median is **60 m**. Their difference is **20 m**. But the means are **250/5 = 50 m** and **300/5 = 60 m**, a difference of **10 m**. There is no contradiction: the summaries answer different questions.

Now ask a third question: how many estimates are **strictly above 50 m**? Only 90 qualifies in the low group, so **1/5 = 20%**. In the high group,60,70 and 85 qualify, so **3/5 = 60%**. Exactly 50 does not qualify. The chart’s first-minus-second contrast is **40 percentage points**, with **five records** as each denominator. It is not a difference of lengths.

### What these records cannot establish

They illustrate how to compute summaries. They do not establish a real psychological effect, random assignment, a population effect or a mechanism. Even if they came from a well-designed study, this table would not reveal the response each person would have given under the other ticket. The large low-ticket estimate also shows why a group contrast is not a rule that every high-group answer exceeds every low-group answer.

## Adjustment is an explanation, not the definition of every effect

**Adjustment** means revising a judgment away from a starting value. **Insufficient adjustment** is the proposal that a person stops before an adequately supported revision. The person generally does not know the true answer and choose a measured fraction of the distance to it. A slider that computes that fraction is a mathematical construction, not a measurement of a mind.

A limited analogy is searching down a corridor and stopping at the first door that looks plausible. It illustrates an early stopping rule. It cannot tell you where somebody actually stopped, whether that door was wrong, or whether they searched a corridor at all.

For a constructed example, suppose a worker recalls that an earlier sign-hanging job took 20 minutes and proposes 22 minutes for the new one. If the complete declared tasks below total 26 minutes,22 is 4 minutes short **under those stipulated durations**. That arithmetic does not establish why the worker answered 22. They might have omitted a task, used different duration information, or deliberately adjusted from the old job. You need evidence about those possibilities.

**Selective accessibility** is another proposed process: considering an anchor can make information consistent with it easier to retrieve, and that information can shape the estimate. For example, asking whether a hall is unusually long might bring its long rows of windows to mind. Asking about a short length might bring its compact stage area to mind. Those are candidate pathways, not measurements of a particular visitor’s memory.

| Proposed route | What would be useful evidence? | What is insufficient on its own? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revision from a recalled value | Reports of the starting value and successive revisions, plus a task manipulation that changes the predicted process | One final number close to a starting value |
| Selective retrieval of information | A test of which information becomes accessible and a manipulation separating that account from alternatives | Merely labelling an anchor “external” |

[Epley and Gilovich](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00372) studied self-generated and experimenter-provided anchors. Their verbal-report and experimental tasks supported adjustment in some judgments and differences between those task types. Source was informative in those designs; it is not an infallible classifier for every later judgment. Their own study included reports of adjustment with provided anchors too. A final response is not a brain scan, however confident the commentator sounds.

### When to ask about the mechanism

Ask when selecting an explanation or intervention. Look for predictions that differ between accounts and procedures that test them. Relabelling the source or observing a final answer does not replace that test.

## The multiplication study: same product, different reported medians

The 1974 report also describes two groups of high-school students estimating within **five seconds**:

| Presentation | Reported median estimate | Exact product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1×2×3×4×5×6×7×8 | 512 | 40,320 |
| 8×7×6×5×4×3×2×1 | 2,250 | 40,320 |

Multiplication’s order does not change its product. The ascending first three factors give **1×2×3 = 6**, whereas the descending first three give **8×7×6 = 336**. Extrapolating from a partial calculation could therefore produce different starting values. Tversky and Kahneman offered that account; the medians were consistent with its predictions and both were below the exact product.

The distinction matters: the report establishes those group summaries, not that every student stopped after the same factors. If you try the task, your one guess does not prove your mechanism, and the study does not reveal your personal answer under the other order.

### When this example is useful

Use it to separate an invariant mathematical answer from a judgment affected by presentation. It is a classroom comparison, not a test that diagnoses a person after one timed guess.

## Coherence can coexist with different absolute levels

In [Ariely, Loewenstein and Prelec’s study](https://doi.org/10.1162/00335530360535153),55 MBA students considered six products. They answered a purchase question using the last two digits of their Social Security number as a dollar amount, then stated their maximum willingness to pay under a procedure that could lead to a purchase. The report found higher valuations in higher-digit groups. Those digits did not supply product information, but people were not randomly assigned to possess different Social Security numbers.

The report also describes a large majority maintaining particular relative comparisons: keyboard above trackball, and the higher-rated wine above the lower-rated wine. It does not say every person ranked every product perfectly. The paper calls the coexistence of an anchor-sensitive level and orderly relative valuations **coherent arbitrariness**. Its authors explicitly leave the psychological mechanism open in that discussion.

Here is a separate **constructed** example, in arbitrary valuation points:

| Declared menu | Plain notebook | Notebook with an added feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First menu | 8 | 12 |
| Second menu | 16 | 24 |

The second item exceeds the first in both menus, while both levels double. Order alone cannot select the correct absolute level or establish why the menus differ. Nor does calling an item “better” establish that every buyer must value it more.

### When to check both order and level

Use the distinction when “the answers look consistent” is offered as proof that they are well grounded. Consistency is useful to inspect; the origin of the levels still needs evidence.

## A useful reference still has to earn its weight

For our sign-hanging example, the earlier job took **20 minutes**. For the new job, stipulate three **sequential, nonoverlapping durations**, with no additional travel, waiting or rework in this exercise:

| Included task | Stipulated duration |
| --- | --- |
| Prepare the materials | 6 min |
| Hang the signs | 12 min |
| Pack and check | 8 min |
| Complete declared scope | 26 min |

The complete sum is **6 + 12 + 8 = 26 minutes**. The old job is relevant context, but it does not override information about the new scope. Revising from 20 to 26 can be justified by the stated tasks; it need not be described as a formal Bayesian update.

### When to use the reference

Use prior jobs or measurements when they match the quantity and scope. Explain what carries over and what changes. Scrutiny applies to your independent estimate too; producing it yourself is not a certificate of accuracy.

## Independent transfer: choose the summary before the story

A new **constructed** card concerns estimates of a walk’s duration, in **minutes**. Two distinct groups considered tickets 10 and 70 before answering. Again, the records are complete, sorted and not paired across groups. No claim is made about a real population or random assignment.

| Record | Low-ticket group | High-ticket group |
| --- | --- | --- |
| K / P | 18 | 26 |
| L / Q | 22 | 28 |
| M / R | 30 | 30 |
| N / S | 34 | 48 |
| O / T | 66 | 58 |

Before opening the solution, calculate the two medians, the two means and the fraction of records **strictly above 40 minutes**. Then decide whether equal medians require every other summary to agree. Use the transfer condition in the earlier chart to check the threshold counts.

| Transfer answer | Median difference | Mean difference | High-minus-low threshold contrast |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rowan | 0 min | 4 min | 20 percentage points |
| Willow | 4 min | 0 min | 20 percentage points |
| Alder | 0 min | 4 min | 40 percentage points |
| Beech | 30 min | 38 min | 0 percentage points |

### When to use this audit

Use it when a headline compresses a comparison into “the first number won.” Recover the question, procedure, complete data or reported summary, and appropriate comparator. Then separate what the evidence shows from the mechanism proposed to explain it.

<Callout variant="success" title="Keep the effect and its explanation separate">
A starting value can influence a numerical judgment. Describe the comparison accurately, distinguish group results from individual changes, and test a mechanism with evidence about the process. Keep relevant information, check its weight and scope, and do not mistake a tidy simulation or a consistent ranking for a measurement of somebody’s mind.
</Callout>
