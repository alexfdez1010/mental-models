A job offer pays 200 euros more each month and adds an hour to your daily
commute. Is it worth accepting? The salary alone cannot settle the question.
You also need to consider travel costs, time, and what you value about each job.

A **mental model** is a simplified representation of how something works.
It helps you organise facts into a relationship you can reason with. For this
decision, one useful relationship is that choosing an option means giving up
something else: here, extra pay comes with less time at home.

## From facts to a decision

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="The new commute adds one hour on each of 20 working days a month. What comparison would help you assess the extra pay?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Compare 200 euros with 20 extra travel hours and travel costs', correct: true },
    { text: 'Compare the salaries and ignore the time spent travelling' },
    { text: 'Compare job titles and assume the larger salary is better' },
  ]}
  explanation="The extra commute is 1 × 20 = 20 hours a month. That is 200 ÷ 20 = 10 euros per extra travel hour before travel costs or any tax difference. It is a comparison to consider, not a rule that decides which job to take."
/>

The calculation makes one trade-off visible: 20 extra travel hours for 200 euros
of extra pay, before other costs. Perhaps the new job offers training you want;
perhaps that hour is when you collect your child from school. The model helps
you ask what you give up. Your circumstances determine how much that matters.

The four teaching lessons develop the tools behind this approach:

1. **What a mental model is** — identify the relationship a model keeps and the
   detail it leaves out.
2. **The map is not the territory** — check whether an omitted detail could
   change your decision.
3. **The latticework** — examine the same situation with several relevant models.
4. **Circle of competence** — recognise which judgments you can support and
   where you need help.

## Carry the relationship to a new case

A transferable model lets you recognise a relationship in another setting.
Consider a café that receives 12 orders an hour and can prepare 8. If both rates
stay constant and nobody cancels, its backlog grows by 12 − 8 = **4 orders an
hour**. After three hours, there are 12 more waiting orders than at the start.

The same calculation can describe a support team receiving 12 requests an hour
and resolving 8. The objects have changed; the relationship between arrivals,
completions, and waiting work has stayed the same. Before transferring the model,
check whether the assumptions still hold: customers might leave, and some support
requests take much longer than others.

Facts supply the rates; the model tells you how the rates relate. You need both.

## Practise before the final exam

Use the questions to make a prediction, then compare your reasoning with the
feedback. If you get an answer wrong, identify the step that changed the result.
In each interactive figure, change one control and ask which part of the
explanation accounts for what you see.

The practice exercises let you try again. The **final exam** works differently:
each submitted answer is locked, there is no Back or Restart, and the total score
appears after the last question.

<Callout variant="tip" title="A useful check on understanding">
Try explaining why the answer follows from the example. If you can recognise a
phrase but cannot explain the decision, work through the example once more.
</Callout>
