A shop cuts its price and sells more items, yet earns less on those sales.
A model of demand helps ask how many customers will buy. To assess the decision,
you also need the margin on each sale and the possible reaction of competitors.

A **latticework of mental models** is a connected set of models you use to
examine different parts of a situation. One model may reveal a question another
leaves open. Choose models for the questions they answer; collecting more names
does not by itself improve a decision.

## The man with a hammer

Munger borrowed a proverb to name the danger of owning just one model:

<Callout variant="tip" title="The law of the instrument">
**"To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail."** When you
know one model well, you'll cram every situation into it — even the situations it
fits badly. This is also called the **law of the instrument**.
</Callout>

Suppose a manager uses financial rewards to explain every missed deadline.
A bonus may change what people prioritise. It cannot by itself remove a printer
jam or add capacity to an overloaded review desk. The question is whether the
reward mechanism explains the delay you actually observe.

This connects to the previous lesson: a model describes selected relationships.
Treating those relationships as a complete account of every problem overlooks
what the model leaves out.

## Check different questions

Take the shop's pricing decision. It currently sells 100 items at 10 euros each;
each item costs 6 euros to supply. Its contribution toward fixed costs is
100 × (10 − 6) = **400 euros**. At a price of 9 euros, the contribution per item
falls to 3 euros. Selling 120 items then produces 120 × 3 = **360 euros**.
More sales would still leave less contribution than before.

| Question | Model to consider | What still needs evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How many items will sell at 9 euros? | Demand: quantity can respond to price | The size of that response for these customers |
| What does each sale contribute? | Unit margin: price minus cost per item | Whether the 6-euro cost remains valid |
| Will a competitor also cut prices? | Strategic interaction: each firm reacts to the other | The competitor's options and likely response |
| What will staff prioritise? | Incentives: rewards can influence behaviour | Whether rewards favour volume, margin, or something else |

These questions complement each other because they examine different mechanisms.
Their subject labels do not determine that: several models from economics can
answer distinct questions, and models from different fields can share the same
unsupported assumption.

## A worked example: diagnosing a struggling team

A team receives 12 similar tasks a day. Its preparation stage can complete 16,
but the review desk can finish only 8. With no other route or cancellations,
waiting work grows by **12 − 8 = 4 tasks a day**.

1. **Check the bottleneck:** the review desk limits completed output to 8 tasks
   a day. Faster preparation alone cannot raise that limit.
2. **Check incentives:** if people receive credit for starting tasks, they may
   start more while the review queue grows. Look at the reward rule before
   assuming that anyone lacks effort.
3. **Compare alternatives:** increasing review capacity from 8 to 12 could stop
   this queue growing if the other assumptions hold. Adding four preparation
   slots would leave the review limit unchanged.
4. **Test the explanation:** measure completed reviews and waiting work after a
   small change. If review capacity is actually higher, investigate the mismatch
   rather than keeping the bottleneck story by default.

The models suggest what to inspect and which intervention to compare. The
observations decide whether the explanation fits.

<Callout variant="info" title="A limit on the diagnosis">
This calculation assumes comparable tasks, constant arrival rates, and a shared
review step. If urgent tasks bypass review or task sizes vary, the model needs
those differences before it can predict a real queue accurately.
</Callout>

## The pitfall: a latticework can be abused

Owning many models has its own failure mode, and it's worth naming. With a big
toolkit, it's tempting to rummage until you find the model that gives you the
answer you'd already decided on — then call it "rigorous thinking." That's
**motivated reasoning** in a lab coat.

The fix is the same discipline as before: a latticework is for finding where
models *disagree*, not for cherry-picking the one that agrees with you. If three
models point one way and your favourite points the other, the interesting thing
is the disagreement — not an excuse to trust your favourite.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Breadth isn't a buffet">
A latticework is not a menu you order your preferred conclusion from. Its value is
that the models *check* each other. The moment you're using extra models to
justify a foregone conclusion, you've turned a thinking tool into a rationalising
tool.
</Callout>

## When to reach for the latticework

- **On any decision that crosses domains** — which is most important ones. Hiring,
  pricing, strategy, big life choices all involve psychology *and* economics *and*
  systems at once. One model can't cover them.
- **Whenever you notice you're using your favourite model again.** That noticing is
  the cue to deliberately ask, "what would a *different* field say about this?"
- **Not for trivial, single-domain calls.** Choosing lunch doesn't need game
  theory. Reserve the full latticework for decisions where being one-sidedly wrong
  actually costs you.

You can simplify, you can stay humble about the gap, and you can stack models to
cover each other's blind spots. The last foundational idea ties them together by
asking the most honest question of all: *which territory do you actually know?*
That's the circle of competence.
