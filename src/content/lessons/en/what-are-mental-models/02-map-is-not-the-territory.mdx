**The map is not the territory:** a representation and the situation it
describes are different things. Before using a model to decide, check which
features it represents, which it omits, and whether it is current.

## Which question does the map answer?

The venue plan can answer “where is the entrance?” while missing what you need
for “can 90 guests sit here today?” The useful distinction is between the task
the map supports and the extra detail your next task requires.

| Decision | Detail you need to check |
| --- | --- |
| Find the room | Entrances and connecting corridors |
| Seat 90 guests | Usable floor space and today's seating layout |
| Arrange a projector | Screen position, power, and sight lines |

## When the omission bites

A dropped detail is harmless right up until it isn't. The omission bites in three
recognisable situations — and spotting them in advance is the actual skill here.

### When an assumption stops fitting

Suppose you estimate journey time from a train's normal timetable. The estimate
assumes the usual service is running. During engineering works, that assumption
fails: adding more precision to the normal timetable will not restore the missing
replacement-bus information.

### When the cost of an error rises

A rough estimate may be enough to choose a café. If the same journey is for an
exam with a fixed start time, a delay has a different consequence. Higher stakes
do not make the timetable less accurate; they justify more checking or a larger
time buffer.

### When the situation changes

A road may close after a route was saved. The old route still looks coherent,
but the closure changes whether it works. Check for updates that matter to the
journey rather than assuming a familiar display must be current.

In each case, a coherent-looking display can conceal an omitted detail or an outdated assumption. That's why "the map is not the territory" has to be a habit, not a
fact you nod at once.
</Callout>

## Useful for which task?

A useful model can omit detail and still answer a specific question well. The
venue plan locates the entrance; the train map identifies an interchange. The
omissions become a problem when they change the answer you need.

The familiar phrase “all models are wrong, but some are useful” is a reminder
to assess a model against its intended task. It does not mean that all answers
are equally good or that checking evidence is pointless.

## When to use it (and the trap to dodge)

The map–territory idea isn't a model you *deploy* so much as a permanent posture:

- **Reach for it whenever you feel certain.** Certainty is the warning light. The
  more obviously right your map feels, the more worth asking "what is this leaving
  out, and could that bite here?"
- **Use it to stay humble at the edges and the high stakes.** Mid-range,
  low-stakes? Trust the map and move on. Edge case, big consequences? Slow down and
  check the territory.
- **The trap to dodge — overcorrection.** The point is *not* "all maps are useless,
  trust nothing." That's just paralysis wearing a clever disguise. You still need
  maps to function; you just hold them loosely.
