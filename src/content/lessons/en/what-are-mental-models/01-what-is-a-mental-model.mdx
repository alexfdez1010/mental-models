The connections let you follow a line to an interchange, change lines, and

Now ask whether two stations are close enough to walk between. Their spacing on
the same diagram may mislead you. One drawing can answer the route question and
fail the walking question. **Keep the structure your decision needs** is the
first rule for using a mental model.

## The definition

Here's the precise version, and it's worth reading slowly:

<Callout variant="tip" title="Definition">
A **mental model** is a simplified representation you use to reason about how something works. You can make that reasoning explicit with a drawing, a verbal rule or a calculation. The question determines which relationships to retain and which assumptions to check.
</Callout>

The definition has three parts:

- **Simplified:** it keeps selected features. Which features matter depends on
  the question: train connections for a route, distances for a walk.
- **Representation:** it stands for something outside the model. A heating rule
  describes a heating system; the rule cannot heat the room.
- **Reusable:** it can suggest questions in another case with a similar
  structure. You still need to check that the relationship holds there.

A model may be a picture, a verbal rule, or a calculation. Calling it
“compressed” means it represents a complicated situation with fewer details;
it does not mean every missing detail is harmless.

## A worked example: a thermostat

Consider a simple heating system that is either on at a fixed output or off.
Its control rule is: **turn on below the target temperature and turn off once
the target is reached**. This simplified rule describes a feedback loop: the
measured temperature affects the heating, which then affects the temperature.

| Room | Target | What the model predicts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 18 °C | 21 °C | Heating on |
| 20 °C | 21 °C | Heating still on |
| 21 °C | 21 °C | Heating off |

At 18 °C, raising the target from 21 °C to 25 °C does not increase this heater's
output: it is already on. It changes when the heater will switch off. That
prediction follows from the on/off assumption; a system that varies its output
needs a different model.

We left out pipe layout and gas pressure because they do not determine the
on/off decision in this example. We also left out heating rate, so this model
cannot tell us how many minutes the room will take to reach 21 °C.

<Callout variant="info" title="What the simplification buys">
The rule predicts the controller's action from two temperatures. To predict
warming time or diagnose a fault, you would need more information.
</Callout>

## Representation, system and record

The **system** is what you want to understand: the city or heating system. A **model** represents selected relationships: station connections or the switching rule. A **record** describes a case: a temperature reading at a particular time.

| Role | Example | What it allows |
|---|---|---|
| Represented system | The actual heating system | It is what you observe or act on |
| Model rule | Switch on below the target | Produce a prediction for a reading and target |
| Record | A temperature reading in that room | Check a prediction, if the measurement is relevant and reliable |

A model can incorporate facts, and a relationship can be stated as a true proposition. We separate their roles here: a rule relates cases; a record supplies information about one. Both need checking. An erroneous reading does not become reliable by appearing in a table.

## Write down the input, rule and output

A recipe does more than name a dish: it gives steps you can follow with particular ingredients. Seek that degree of specificity when checking a model. Which question does it answer, what inputs does it need and which output does it produce?

Imagine a card workshop. We want to predict **minutes for a batch from the start of preparation to completion**. The candidate rules assume no prior queue, interruption or overlapping work. Preparation happens once per batch and each card requires the same time within the rule. These are assumptions to check against records, not facts that the formula establishes about the workshop.

The **input** is n, a whole-number count of cards from zero through twelve. Zero means a preparation-only run. The **output** is T, duration in minutes. A **rule** relates them. Two candidates, fixed before the check, are:

| Rule | Preparation per batch | Minutes per card | Calculation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| A | 10 min | 3 min | $T=10+3n$ |
| B | 0 min | 5 min | $T=5n$ |

Multiplying three by n means adding three minutes for each card. Add preparation once. For five cards, A gives $10+3\times5=25$ minutes and B gives $5\times5=25$. An initial five-card record of twenty-five minutes fits both. **A shared success does not distinguish the rules.**

Now open a new eight-card record: thirty-four minutes. A matches; B predicts six minutes too many. A later twelve-card batch records forty-six minutes: A matches again and B predicts sixty. All figures in this example are constructed and exact by stipulation.

| Cards | Record | Prediction A | Prediction B | Observed minus A | Observed minus B |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 5 | 25 min | 25 min | 25 min | 0 min | 0 min |
| 8 | 34 min | 34 min | 40 min | 0 min | −6 min |
| 12 | 46 min | 46 min | 60 min | 0 min | −14 min |

The difference **observed minus predicted** is zero for agreement, negative when the rule predicted too much and positive when it predicted less than the record. For a model fitted to data, this is usually called a *residual*. Here we compare fixed rules; the chart fits no parameters. Real measurements would need a stated measurement procedure and an acceptable discrepancy.

### When to use it

When an explanation sounds persuasive, ask for a concrete prediction and its conditions. If several rules fit what is known, seek a case where they predict different things. Necessary detail depends on the question: the earlier thermostat rule still cannot calculate warming time.

## Revise a rule without confusing fitting and checking

The workshop conditions change. An eight-card batch takes forty-four minutes. A predicted thirty-four: observed minus predicted is ten. That mismatch calls for checking measurement, inputs and assumptions rather than defending the formula at any cost.

As a proposal, keep three minutes per card and change preparation. The eight cards account for $3\times8=24$ minutes under that hypothesis, leaving $44-24=20$ for preparation. Call $T=20+3n$ rule C. We proposed C **using that record**, so matching it again is not a check using new information.

Even these matches do not establish that actual preparation became twenty. Another story, ten minutes of preparation plus one fixed ten-minute interruption and three per card, produces the same totals. Distinguishing those mechanisms requires observing them or designing another check. **Adjusting a number, matching a new case and explaining a cause are different jobs.**

### When to use it

When changing a rule after a mismatch, record which observations helped change it and what you will check next. Avoid presenting records already used to construct it as a check using new information. A result can justify revising a representation while leaving its mechanism unresolved.

## Transfer the method with different data

Another workshop proposes D: six minutes of preparation plus four per card; E: six plus three per card. Both rules assume one preparation and non-overlapping work. Those assumptions also need checking in this workshop. A three-card record takes eighteen minutes. Write both predictions for a new seven-card batch before opening its record.

### When to use it

Carry the sequence question → input → rule → prediction → check into another situation. The values and conditions need their own justification. If the new question requires something the representation omits, extend it or choose another.

## The pitfall: mistaking the model for reality

Here's where beginners trip. Because a good model is so clean and useful, it's
tempting to forget it's a simplification at all — to start believing the subway
map *is* the city. The whole next lesson is devoted to this trap, but plant the
flag now: **a model is always less than the thing it describes.** The moment you
forget that, the model stops helping and starts misleading.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The trap to remember">
A clear representation can serve one question while omitting the decisive detail for another. Check its purpose and assumptions when using it in a new case. Earlier successful predictions do not remove that check.
</Callout>

## When to reach for a model

So when do you actually deploy one? Whenever a situation is too complex to think
about in full — which is most of the time.

- **Use a model when you need to decide or predict fast** and can't afford to
  understand every detail. The thermostat loop lets you act without a plumbing
  degree.
- **Use one to transfer understanding** from a domain you know to one you don't.
  Recognise a feedback loop, identify what is measured and what changes in
  response, then check whether the old model's assumptions still fit.
- **Be more careful when the stakes are high and the situation sits near the
  model's edge** — where the dropped detail might be the detail that matters. That
  caution is the subject of the next lesson.
