The connections let you follow a line to an interchange, change lines, and
reach your destination. The true shape of each tunnel adds little to that decision.

Now ask whether two stations are close enough to walk between. Their spacing on
the same diagram may mislead you. One drawing can answer the route question and
fail the walking question. **Keep the structure your decision needs** is the
first rule for using a mental model.

## The definition

Here's the precise version, and it's worth reading slowly:

<Callout variant="tip" title="Definition">
A **mental model** is a simplified internal representation of how some part of
the world works — one that deliberately throws away detail so you can reason
about it and test whether the relationship applies to new situations.
</Callout>

The definition has three parts:

- **Simplified:** it keeps selected features. Which features matter depends on
  the question: train connections for a route, distances for a walk.
- **Representation:** it stands for something outside the model. A heating rule
  describes a heating system; the rule cannot heat the room.
- **Reusable:** it can suggest questions in another case with a similar
  structure. You still need to check that the relationship holds there.

A model may be a picture, a verbal rule, or a calculation. Calling it
“compressed” means it represents a complicated situation with fewer details;
it does not mean every missing detail is harmless.

## A worked example: a thermostat

Consider a simple heating system that is either on at a fixed output or off.
Its control rule is: **turn on below the target temperature and turn off once
the target is reached**. This simplified rule describes a feedback loop: the
measured temperature affects the heating, which then affects the temperature.

| Room | Target | What the model predicts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 18 °C | 21 °C | Heating on |
| 20 °C | 21 °C | Heating still on |
| 21 °C | 21 °C | Heating off |

At 18 °C, raising the target from 21 °C to 25 °C does not increase this heater's
output: it is already on. It changes when the heater will switch off. That
prediction follows from the on/off assumption; a system that varies its output
needs a different model.

We left out pipe layout and gas pressure because they do not determine the
on/off decision in this example. We also left out heating rate, so this model
cannot tell us how many minutes the room will take to reach 21 °C.

<Callout variant="info" title="What the simplification buys">
The rule predicts the controller's action from two temperatures. To predict
warming time or diagnose a fault, you would need more information.
</Callout>

## Map, model, territory — sorting the three

Keep the representation separate from the system and from individual observations:

| Thing | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The **territory** | The real, full-detail thing itself | The actual city, with every street |
| The **map / model** | A simplified stand-in for it | The subway map; "cold → heat on" |
| A **fact** | A single true statement about the territory | "The blue line has 14 stops" |

A fact is a single data point. A model describes relationships you can use to generate predictions. You
can memorise the fact "the blue line has 14 stops," but the model "subway lines
connect stations in sequence" lets you read *any* transit map you've never seen.

## The pitfall: mistaking the model for reality

Here's where beginners trip. Because a good model is so clean and useful, it's
tempting to forget it's a simplification at all — to start believing the subway
map *is* the city. The whole next lesson is devoted to this trap, but plant the
flag now: **a model is always less than the thing it describes.** The moment you
forget that, the model stops helping and starts misleading.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The trap to remember">
A model feels like the truth because it's clear and it works most of the time.
But "clear and usually works" is not the same as "complete." Every model has edges
where it quietly fails — and you'll only notice if you remember it was a
simplification in the first place.
</Callout>

## When to reach for a model

So when do you actually deploy one? Whenever a situation is too complex to think
about in full — which is most of the time.

- **Use a model when you need to decide or predict fast** and can't afford to
  understand every detail. The thermostat loop lets you act without a plumbing
  degree.
- **Use one to transfer understanding** from a domain you know to one you don't.
  Recognise a feedback loop, identify what is measured and what changes in
  response, then check whether the old model's assumptions still fit.
- **Be more careful when the stakes are high and the situation sits near the
  model's edge** — where the dropped detail might be the detail that matters. That
  caution is the subject of the next lesson.
