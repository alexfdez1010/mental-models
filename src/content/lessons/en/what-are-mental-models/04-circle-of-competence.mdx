You have baked bread in the same oven for years. A colleague asks two questions: “Will this batch be ready by nine?” and “Should we buy a bakery chain?” Your experience may help with the first. The second also needs information about debt, prices and the businesses involved.

The **circle of competence** is a way to ask which particular judgments your knowledge can support. Name the decision before drawing the circle: “I know baking” is too broad to tell you whether you can answer either question.

## The definition

Your **circle of competence** covers the tasks you understand well enough to make informed judgments, with evidence about how those judgments perform and where they stop applying. The idea is widely used in investment thinking, but the question is useful wherever expertise has limits.

There is no instrument that measures the boundary exactly. Treat it as a working estimate: what can you explain, what have you tested, and which changes would make you less certain? Someone can be competent at running a café and still lack the evidence needed to value its shares.

A familiar workshop offers a useful analogy. You know which repairs you can carry out and check. Being able to repair a bicycle does not establish that you can repair a motorcycle’s engine. Shared vocabulary can hide a change of task.

### When to use it

Use this check when a familiar label tempts you to skip a new kind of analysis: a new customer group, a larger machine, a different regulation or a more consequential decision.

## Three working zones

The zones describe the evidence for a judgment. They do not assign a safety rating to the whole situation.

| Zone | What you can say | Appropriate response |
|---|---|---|
| **Supported task** | I can explain the mechanism and have checked comparable predictions | Use that evidence, keep checking and allow for error |
| **Known gap** | I can identify an important part I cannot evaluate | Seek relevant help, gather evidence or decline the task |
| **Uncertain boundary** | It feels familiar, but I cannot show that my judgment transfers | Identify the missing test and limit the commitment |

Knowing that you lack knowledge helps only if you respond to it. A person who recognises an unfamiliar machine and uses it anyway can still cause damage. Equally, an experienced operator can face a fault they have never seen.

## A worked example with four batches

This is a hypothetical bakery. Ana predicts baking time for batches of 40 rolls, using the same dough, oven and temperature each time. She writes down each prediction before baking.

| Batch | Predicted time | Actual time | Absolute error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 28 min | 27 min | 1 min |
| 2 | 28 min | 29 min | 1 min |
| 3 | 28 min | 28 min | 0 min |
| 4 | 28 min | 30 min | 2 min |

An **absolute error** is the size of the miss, ignoring whether the prediction was too high or too low. For batch 1, the error is 28 − 27 = 1 minute; for batch 4, it is 30 − 28 = 2 minutes. The average error across the four batches is (1 + 1 + 0 + 2) ÷ 4 = **1 minute**.

That is limited, relevant evidence for estimating another comparable batch. It does not prove that the next batch will finish within one minute of her prediction. Four observations leave plenty untested, including different dough and equipment faults.

Now the owner asks Ana to promise that a new industrial oven will bake 400 rolls in the same 28 minutes. The batch is ten times larger and the equipment has changed. Her old results do not establish the new timing. A supervised test of the new setup would provide more relevant evidence than adding years to her job title.

### What the comparison leaves out

Baking time is one decision. Predicting demand, negotiating a lease and valuing a company are other decisions. Competence at one can provide useful background for another without settling it. The transfer needs evidence of its own.

## Turn the boundary into an action

Before committing, make the judgment specific enough to check:

1. **Name the task and conditions.** “Estimate this batch’s time in this oven” is testable. “Understand bakeries” is not precise enough.
2. **State your reason and relevant record.** Explain which observations support the estimate and which result would challenge it.
3. **Check what has changed.** Equipment, scale, people and incentives can matter even when the task has the same name.
4. **Match the response to the consequences.** A reversible trial can help you learn. A costly or irreversible commitment may need additional expertise and evidence.

A knowledgeable reviewer can catch mistakes and suggest a better test. Their agreement is one piece of evidence, with limits set by what they actually reviewed. It cannot prove that every part of your judgment is sound.

## Connect it to the other models

Your view of your own knowledge is another **map**. Compare it with results rather than assuming it matches the territory. A **latticework** offers several ways to inspect a problem, but knowing the names of those models does not establish that you can apply them well.

For example, saying “incentives matter” may suggest why a sales forecast is optimistic. You still need to examine the forecast, its assumptions and the sales record. A model helps you ask the question; checking the answer requires work.
