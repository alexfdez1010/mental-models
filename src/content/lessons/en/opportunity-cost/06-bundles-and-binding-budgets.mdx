The best-paying job on your list can fit your Saturday badly. It may leave a gap too small for anything useful, while two less impressive jobs fit together and earn more. A calendar has the same annoying habit as a suitcase: the shape of what you pack matters.

Earlier lessons compared alternative uses of a scarce resource. This lesson changes the menu: you can complete more than one job, but each must fit in full. You will compare whole schedules, name the best alternative to the schedule you choose and recalculate when the time available changes.

## Define what is available and what counts as success

An editor has **six hours** available before tonight's deadline. Four one-off commissions are offered. Each is **indivisible**: the client pays for the completed job, and a half-finished job earns nothing. Each can be accepted at most once. Jobs can be completed sequentially in any order, and their times include their administration. There are no dependencies between them.

For this worked case, the editor wants to maximise **cash contribution**: fee minus the direct cash expenses of doing the job. The amounts are before tax and fixed overheads. The fees, times and expenses below are teaching data, not market estimates.

| Job | Time required | Fee | Direct expense | Cash contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A — Long report | 4 hours | €330 | €30 | €330 − €30 = €300 |
| B — Museum brochure | 3 hours | €230 | €20 | €230 − €20 = €210 |
| C — Exhibition catalogue | 3 hours | €240 | €30 | €240 − €30 = €210 |
| D — Event poster | 2 hours | €110 | €10 | €110 − €10 = €100 |

A **feasible schedule** meets all the stated restrictions. A plus B needs **4 + 3 = 7 hours**, so it is unavailable with six hours. A plus D needs **4 + 2 = 6 hours**, so it is feasible. Doing half of A to squeeze in another commission is unavailable under these contracts.

Keep the objective separate from the restriction. Six hours is the limit; cash contribution is what the editor is maximising. Filling every minute is not itself the goal. If another schedule earned more in less time, unused time would not disqualify it.

### When to use it

Use complete schedules when smaller choices can coexist. Before doing arithmetic, check deadlines, dependencies and whether work can really be split. If the editor also values rest or portfolio development, those considerations belong in the decision; this cash-only comparison does not price them.

## Build the menu from bundles

    { text: "A rule requiring the largest job to be selected first." }

A **bundle** is a set of jobs accepted together. Its time and contribution are the sums for those jobs, because the case assumes no shared setup costs or effects between them. Choosing valuable indivisible items within a capacity limit is the structure of a **knapsack problem**: here the capacity is time rather than the weight a bag can carry. [Google's knapsack explanation](https://developers.google.com/optimization/pack/knapsack)

With four jobs, listing the possibilities is manageable. The table contains **every feasible bundle**, including declining all the work. The unavailable pairs A + B and A + C each require seven hours; every three-job bundle requires at least eight hours, so none fits.

| Feasible bundle | Total time | Cash contribution |
|---|---|---|
| No commission | 0 hours | €0 |
| A | 4 hours | €300 |
| B | 3 hours | €210 |
| C | 3 hours | €210 |
| D | 2 hours | €100 |
| A + D | 6 hours | €300 + €100 = €400 |
| B + C | 6 hours | €210 + €210 = €420 |
| B + D | 5 hours | €210 + €100 = €310 |
| C + D | 5 hours | €210 + €100 = €310 |

The best bundle for the stated objective is **B + C, worth €420**. The next-best complete schedule is **A + D, worth €400**. This is a comparison between two ways to use the afternoon. It is not a choice between A and B in isolation.

### When to use it

Enumerate bundles when the list is short and complete. For hundreds of jobs, use an appropriate optimisation method rather than pretend a long handwritten list proves the best result. If accepting one job changes another's duration or value, update the bundle values instead of adding standalone figures blindly.

## Name the opportunity cost and count cash expenses once

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="After choosing the brochure and catalogue, which counterfactual should the editor value?"
  options={[
    { text: "The report and poster as the best available replacement schedule.", correct: true },
    { text: "Every other feasible schedule as if all could replace it together." },
    { text: "Only the unused minutes, because the chosen work earns money." }
  ]}
  explanation="The best forgone use of the afternoon is the report-plus-poster schedule. Other schedules compete with it; their values do not accumulate into a single available replacement."
/>

**Opportunity cost** is the value of the best feasible alternative given up. OpenStax connects this definition to a budget constraint: an unaffordable option lies outside the available choice set. Here we apply that idea to complete schedules and a time limit. [OpenStax on budget constraints and opportunity cost](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-1-how-individuals-make-choices-based-on-their-budget-constraint)

Choosing B + C gives up the best alternative schedule, A + D. Its opportunity cost on our cash-contribution scale is **€400**. The chosen schedule's **advantage over that alternative** is **€420 − €400 = €20**. The €20 difference is not the opportunity cost: it answers a different question, how much better this schedule performs.

Now check the cash accounting behind that comparison.

| Calculation | B + C | A + D |
|---|---|---|
| Fees received | €230 + €240 = €470 | €330 + €110 = €440 |
| Direct expenses | €20 + €30 = €50 | €30 + €10 = €40 |
| Cash contribution | €470 − €50 = €420 | €440 − €40 = €400 |
| Difference between schedules | €30 more fees minus €10 more expenses | B + C contributes €20 more |

The direct expenses have already been deducted from each contribution. Subtracting the chosen schedule's €50 again would count the same cash expense twice. Likewise, once you compare the afternoon's complete alternative schedules, do not add a second charge for the **same forgone afternoon's earnings**. A separate expense not yet included would need adding; a second label for an included sacrifice would not.

Nor should you add €400 + €310 + €310 as the cost of choosing B + C. Those are competing schedules, not three afternoons you could otherwise have worked. Inside A + D, summing the two compatible jobs is valid; across mutually exclusive schedules, summing invents capacity you do not have.

### When to use it

Use a consistent scale and accounting boundary when alternatives have different fees and expenses. Cash contribution supports this narrow comparison. It does not establish personal satisfaction or long-run business value, and it should not be relabelled as take-home income.

## Test the hourly-rate shortcut

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="The report has the largest cash contribution per hour. Does choosing it first prove the best total schedule?"
  options={[
    { text: "Yes, a higher hourly figure always settles a whole-job decision." },
    { text: "No, the remaining time may fit the other indivisible jobs badly.", correct: true },
    { text: "Yes, provided some unused time remains after accepting it." }
  ]}
  explanation="A rate compares a job with its own duration. It does not check which complete combinations fit the rest of the available time."
/>

An **hourly contribution** divides a job's contribution by its duration. A **greedy rule** takes the apparently best next item before checking the complete combination. The numbers show exactly why the rule “take the highest hourly contribution first” fails in this case.

| Job | Contribution divided by time | Hourly contribution |
|---|---|---|
| A | €300 ÷ 4 | €75/hour |
| B | €210 ÷ 3 | €70/hour |
| C | €210 ÷ 3 | €70/hour |
| D | €100 ÷ 2 | €50/hour |

Choose A first and two hours remain. Only D fits, giving **€300 + €100 = €400**. Choose B and C instead and both fit exactly, giving **€210 + €210 = €420**. The lower-rate pair earns **€20 more** because its pieces fit the time budget better. Both schedules use six hours, so the difference is not caused by leaving time idle.

This example disproves the claim that the shortcut always works for indivisible jobs. It does not prove hourly figures are useless. They can help investigate alternatives, and different assumptions about divisible work would create a different problem. Here the complete bundle comparison settles the choice.

### When to use it

Check the shortcut whenever accepting a job leaves a remainder too small for other work, or whenever an apparently weaker pair fits well. Similar packing issues arise with equipment bookings and project windows. Verify their own constraints rather than transferring the editor's numbers.

## Recalculate when the constraint changes

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="An extra hour becomes available before any job is accepted. What is the relevant comparison?"
  options={[
    { text: "The best complete schedule under each of the two time limits.", correct: true },
    { text: "The original schedule plus the highest hourly figure on the list." },
    { text: "The average fee from every job regardless of whether it now fits." }
  ]}
  explanation="New time may make a different bundle available. Its value depends on that changed menu, not on mechanically multiplying an individual job's rate."
/>

A **binding constraint** is met exactly by the chosen solution. The original best schedule uses all six available hours. Changing the time budget can change both the chosen bundle and the best forgone alternative. Re-enumerate the feasible schedules rather than cling to a choice made for a different afternoon.

| Time available | Best schedule or tied schedules | Maximum cash contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 5 hours | B + D or C + D | €310 |
| 6 hours | B + C | €420 |
| 7 hours | A + B or A + C | €510 |
| 8 hours | B + C + D | €520 |

With seven hours, A + B fits: **4 + 3 = 7 hours**, contributing **€300 + €210 = €510**. A + C ties it. Neither three-job bundle fits in seven hours, so €510 is the maximum. Moving from six to seven hours creates a **€510 − €420 = €90** improvement in the best available cash result.

The following hour has a different value. With eight hours, B + C + D fits and contributes **€210 + €210 + €100 = €520**, only **€10** above the seven-hour maximum. The report plus brochure plus poster still needs nine hours, so that tempting combination remains unavailable. Two extra hours beyond the original budget improve the maximum by **€100**, not twice €90.

These differences describe this menu before acceptance. They are not a universal hourly wage. If the extra time costs money, reduces valued rest or introduces a late-delivery risk, compare those consequences with the improvement. If a job is already contractually accepted, treat that commitment as a new constraint; do not cancel it on the fiction that the original menu is still open.

### When to use it

Recalculate after a changed deadline, job duration, fee or commitment. If durations are uncertain, allow for that uncertainty before accepting a tightly packed schedule. Our exact figures demonstrate the comparison; they do not guarantee that real editing work will finish to the minute.
