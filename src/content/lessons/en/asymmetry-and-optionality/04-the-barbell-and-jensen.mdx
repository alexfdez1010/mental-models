A large reserve and a small experiment can limit a loss. That budget allocation, called a **barbell strategy**, does not by itself establish a convex response or a worthwhile plan.

We will separate three questions: what shape does the function have, which averages does Jensen compare, and which feasible alternative serves a stated objective? Every number here is constructed. Credits are exercise tokens, not money or investment advice.

## Name the input and the response

A **response function** assigns an outcome to each input. Its **domain** is the set of allowed inputs. We write `x` for the input and `f(x)` for the response. An input might measure intensity; a response might measure points, costs or terminal balance. Meaning matters: a higher cost is usually worse.

For `f(x) = x²`, the domain is the real line. With equally likely inputs −10 and 10, both responses are 100 points. The mean input is 0 and its response is 0. We abbreviate an input unit as **u** and a response point as **pt**.

| Input (u) | Probability | Response (pt) | Contribution to mean response (pt) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| −10 | 1/2 | 100 | 50 |
| 10 | 1/2 | 100 | 50 |
| Total | 1 | — | 100 |

We have not yet compared two available plans. We have applied one function to two states and to their mean input. If the points measured damage, celebrating the hundred points would be an unusual decision.

### When this distinction helps

Before discussing risk or curvature, write down the input, domain, response, units and objective. Favorable outcomes in two states do not define a function between them or beyond the interval.

## The chord and the two averages

A function is **convex** on an interval if, between any two points in that interval, its graph lies no higher than the **chord** joining their responses. For a weight `w` between 0 and 1:

`f((1 − w)a + wb) ≤ (1 − w)f(a) + wf(b)`.

The left side averages inputs and applies the function once. The right side applies the function first and then averages responses. This condition must hold for all points and weights in the domain, not only the pair chosen for a picture.

**Jensen's inequality** extends the comparison to a finite distribution. If states have probabilities `pᵢ ≥ 0` totaling 1, and their inputs lie in the interval of convexity, then:

`f(E[X]) ≤ E[f(X)]`.

`E` means a probability-weighted average: `E[X] = Σ pᵢxᵢ`. In this finite case every response is a finite number. We do not need independence between “states”: they are mutually exclusive alternatives, not signals whose probabilities are being multiplied. The statement and conditions follow [MIT's convexity notes, pages two to three and twelve](https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-079-introduction-to-convex-optimization-fall-2009/2ae23d35685ff402473b36011138149a_MIT6_079F09_lec03.pdf).

For the square, the response at the mean is `f(0) = 0`, whereas the mean response is `100`. Define the **Jensen gap** as `E[f(X)] − f(E[X])`: here, 100 pt. It is a response difference, not a return rate or a probability.

### When to use Jensen

Use it to compare a mean response with the response at the mean of **the same distribution and the same function**. On its own it does not rank all distributions, say that greater variance always improves the result, or make the mean input an available action.

## Equality and a counterexample with a floor

Consider `h(x) = 20 max(0, x − 1/2)` for `x ≥ 0`. The response is zero up to the threshold and then grows along a straight line. It is convex: the maximum of those two affine functions is convex. An **affine** function has the form `ax + b`; it is both convex and concave.

| Function and input law | Mean input (u) | Response at mean (pt) | Mean response (pt) | Gap (pt) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `h`, inputs 0 and 2, half each | 1 | 10 | 15 | 5 |
| `h`, inputs 1 and 2, half each | 1.5 | 20 | 20 | 0 |
| `a(x) = 10x`, inputs 0 and 2, half each | 1 | 10 | 10 | 0 |
| `r(x) = 10√x`, inputs 0 and 4, weights 3/4 and 1/4 | 1 | 10 | 5 | −5 |

In the first row, `h(0) = 0` and `h(2) = 30`: their mean is 15. In the second, `h(1) = 10` and `h(2) = 30`: their mean is 20, equal to `h(1.5)`. There is uncertainty and convexity, but no strict gain: the inputs stay on an affine segment. For a strictly convex function and finite, nonconstant inputs with positive probability, the comparison is strict.

The last function is **concave** on its nonnegative domain: the chord lies below the curve and Jensen reverses. It has a zero floor and no ceiling on that domain, yet it is concave. Its responses are 0 and 20, so `3/4 × 0 + 1/4 × 20 = 5`, versus `r(1) = 10`. Floors and ceilings do not classify curvature.

## Audit a reserve and an experiment

Now there is a decision. A team has **100 credits (cr)** for one period. It can hold them and finish with 100 cr, or choose this reserve-and-experiment plan:

- Reserve 85 cr, held unchanged until the end under the model assumptions.
- Pay 10 cr for the experiment at the start. This is not automatically repaid.
- Pay a further 5 cr fixed fee at the start, in every state.

There is no interest, inflation, debt, later payment, reserve loss or other obligation. The team does not choose the external input `X`. Its three mutually exclusive and exhaustive states are 0, 1 and 2 u, with probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4. These are stipulated probabilities, not measured frequencies.

The experiment's gross response uses the same rule `h(x) = 20 max(0, x − 1/2)`, now measured in credits. It is a credit receipt additional to the reserve; it does not include a separate repayment of the outlay. Terminal balance is therefore `g(x) = 85 + h(x)`. Both initial payments have already been deducted in leaving the 85 reserve.

| Input (u) | Probability | Gross response (cr) | Terminal balance (cr) | Contribution to mean balance (cr) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 1/4 | 0 | 85 | 21.25 |
| 1 | 1/2 | 10 | 95 | 47.5 |
| 2 | 1/4 | 30 | 115 | 28.75 |
| Total | 1 | — | — | 97.5 |

The mean input is `1/4 × 0 + 1/2 × 1 + 1/4 × 2 = 1`. The mean gross response is `12.5 cr`, so the mean balance is `85 + 12.5 = 97.5 cr`. Summing the last column gives the same answer.

Do not confuse this law with the first chart's two-endpoint profile. There `h` had a mean response of 15. Here the endpoints carry only half the probability: `1/2 × 15 + 1/2 × h(1) = 7.5 + 5 = 12.5`.

Jensen still holds: `E[g(X)] = 97.5 ≥ g(E[X]) = g(1) = 95`. The gap is 2.5 cr. But `g(1)` is a mathematical benchmark; we have not assumed that `X = 1` can be chosen.

The available alternative is holding 100 cr. If the stated objective is to maximize **expected terminal balance** between these two plans, holding wins: `100 > 97.5`. This is not dominance in every state: the experiment leaves 115 cr in the upper state. A known floor and a positive gap do not settle the choice.

Use it to evaluate a reserve, an outlay and an uncertain response. Compare balances with balances, at the same date and against a feasible alternative. Subtracting the initial fifteen credits again from terminal balance would charge the cost twice.

## Change a cost without changing curvature

Keep the input law and the 10 cr outlay fixed. Change only the fixed fee to `F`, paid at the start. The reserve becomes `90 − F`, and terminal balance is `g_F(x) = 90 − F + h(x)`. All four fees in the table leave a nonnegative reserve.

| Fixed fee F (cr) | Reserve (cr) | Balance at mean input (cr) | Expected balance (cr) | Jensen gap (cr) | Better expected balance: experiment or hold |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 0 | 90 | 100 | 102.5 | 2.5 | Experiment |
| 2.5 | 87.5 | 97.5 | 100 | 2.5 | Exact tie |
| 5 | 85 | 95 | 97.5 | 2.5 | Hold |
| 15 | 75 | 85 | 87.5 | 2.5 | Hold |

Both balance columns fall by exactly one credit for each extra credit of fee. Expected balance is `102.5 − F`; the benchmark at the mean input is `100 − F`. Subtracting cancels the common fee and leaves the same 2.5 gap. The function remains convex even though the choice changes at `F = 2.5`.

### What to review before changing the plan

A cost might be paid always, only when an option is exercised, or after an outcome. Here it is always paid and reduces the reserve at the start. Changing that timing or condition requires a new account. A true inequality does not pay an invoice.

## Check what the floor depends on

In the original model the minimum balance is 85 cr because the gross response is never negative and the reserve is held intact. That floor follows from the assumptions; the name “barbell” does not guarantee it.

Consider two **separate** modifications. If the 85 reserve loses twenty percent and there is no other obligation, minimum balance becomes `85 × 0.8 = 68 cr`. If the reserve stays intact but an additional 7 cr obligation appears, the minimum is `85 − 7 = 78 cr`. We have not combined the modifications. Nor have we defined their effects on holding: these calculations revise the floor, not the ranking of modified plans.

### When to review a loss limit

Reopen the account if the reserve guarantee, liability beyond the outlay, or cost of closing the experiment changes. A small budget share only limits loss to that share when the contracts and obligations allow it.
