Five people have chosen A. The auditor asks for their original records and finds **A A B B B**. Nobody changed a record. Three people chose A after privately seeing B.

To understand that trail, we need the decision rule, the information each person could see and a separate objective for the auditor. Counting raised hands will not supply those details.

## Fix the observer and the decision

This is a **constructed information-cascade model**, followed by an audit. There are two possible states, A and B. One state is selected and stays fixed throughout the five decisions. A **private signal** is a noisy observation of that state; it is not knowledge of the state.

Everyone knows these rules. Participants calculate correctly, and their reward for a correct choice does not depend on later choices. There is no desire to conform or to influence the auditor. **Conditional independence** means that, once a state is fixed, learning one draw does not change the distribution of another. The signals can still be related before conditioning: they concern the same hidden state.

Think of draws with replacement from an urn containing two matching labels and one opposing label. After each private draw, its letter is recorded faithfully. A record contains **only A or B**. It never reveals the true urn or an extra identifying mark. Our later microcase index is bookkeeping, not information available to the auditor.

Before seeing any observations, the auditor chooses one of two access plans:

- **Action log**: read the five public choices, at no fee.
- **Letter records**: read the five original private letters, paying a fixed fee in every case.

The records already exist. Buying access changes what the auditor can read; it changes neither the five signals nor the participants’ behaviour. After reading, the auditor chooses the more probable state. There is no later option to buy selected records after inspecting the action log.

This model follows the signals-versus-actions distinction in Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch. We use the own-signal tie convention discussed by Anderson and Holt. Their alternative random tie convention would change numerical probabilities; a tie rule belongs in the data, not in the small print. [BHW, section II A](https://doi.org/10.1086/261849), [Anderson–Holt, section I and note 10](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/Econ264/papers/Holt%20Anderson%20AER%201997.pdf).

### When to use this boundary

Use it before comparing two audit plans. If access changes incentives, records can be altered, the state changes or signals have another dependence structure, those are different models. Anonymity alone does not supply the assumptions above.

## Trace the five decisions

The actual private letters in this example are **A A B B B**. Start with prior **odds** A:B of 1:1. Odds compare the probabilities of the two states. A private A has likelihood ratio 2 in favour of A, because it is twice as likely under A as under B. A B has ratio 1/2. Multiply odds by a new signal’s likelihood ratio, then convert odds a:b to probability a/(a+b).

The first person sees A: odds 2:1, so they announce A. The second sees A too: 4:1, so they announce A. Under the declared tie rule, these two actions reveal two A signals.

The third sees B. Their odds are now 4 × 1/2 = 2:1, so A remains more probable. Had they seen A instead, they would also have announced A. Holding the history fixed and changing their private signal does not change their action: **that action reveals no additional private signal, conditional on this history and known rule**.

The fourth person recognises this. They retain public odds **4:1**, not 8:1, before adding their own signal. Their B produces 2:1, so they announce A too. The fifth faces the same information structure.

The last three actions leave the public posterior unchanged. They do not make the initial evidence disappear either. After all five A announcements, an outside observer using this model still assigns A probability **4/5**. A cascade can transmit some early information while preventing later information from entering the public log.

### A later start

If the first two signals are A B, their actions reveal those signals and the public odds return to 1:1. The next pair can then start a cascade. “A lead of two” refers to the imbalance in revealed signals here; it does not mean every cascade starts with the first two people. Once a cascade starts under these fixed assumptions, later actions do not add further signal evidence.

## Ask which observation you are conditioning on

Three people can hold different posteriors without anyone making an arithmetic error. They may be conditioning on different observations. To make the denominators inspectable, imagine an exact **probability catalogue**: two equally likely states and three equally likely hidden draw positions for each of five draws. It has 2 × 3⁵ = **486 equiprobable microcases**. These are stipulated possibilities, not a promise about a batch of 486 experiments.

Among microcases producing five public A actions, **108** have state A and **27** have state B: 108/(108+27) = **4/5**. For the fifth participant, the observation is earlier actions A A A A **plus their own B**. The counts become **36** and **18**, so the posterior for A is **2/3**. The participant does not count their future action as an extra observation.

When the auditor reads all five records A A B B B, only **4** catalogue microcases have state A and **8** have state B. The posterior for A is **1/3**, so the auditor chooses B. In odds form, the same calculation is 2² × (1/2)³ = 1/2: odds 1:2 for A:B.

Knowing all five records and the declared rule also determines all five actions. If the auditor keeps the public log too, they must not multiply those actions in as extra evidence: they are functions of letters already known.

The microcase index is never in a record. It is a way to count the probability mass of identical observed letters. Reading five letters does not reveal which hidden draw positions produced them.

### The same action log under another procedure

Now consider a **separate diagnostic model**. Each person announces their own letter without seeing any earlier announcement. The prior and signal law remain the same. If this procedure produces A A A A A, all five actions reveal private A signals: odds 2⁵:1, giving **32/33** for A.

The same visible log can therefore have a different evidential meaning under different known procedures. If the procedure itself is unknown and we specify no probability model for it, there is no unique posterior to report. A restaurant queue or a vote count alone cannot identify which model generated it. This comparison changes the participants’ observation procedure; it is not one of the two audit-access alternatives above.

## Compare access plans over the complete catalogue

One revealing trace establishes a possibility. To price an access plan chosen **before** observations, we need every possible trace and its probability. Keep the original sequential participant procedure fixed for both plans.

There are **32 letter sequences** of length five. Along with the two states they form 64 coarse profiles, but those profiles are not equally likely. Conditional on state A, a sequence containing k A letters has probability $2^k/3^5$. For A A B B B, that is **4/243**, not 1/32. State B gives the mirrored weights.

The full conditional table below is the model’s accounting sheet. The weight column counts hidden draw positions out of **243**, conditional on A. The last two columns are the auditor’s best classification under each access. With the action log, this classification happens to equal the fifth action for every reachable history in this five-person model; it is a consequence checked from the history likelihoods, not a general rule to follow the last person.

Multiply each row’s weight by whether its classification is correct, then add. Under A, the action-log column is correct in **188/243** of the probability mass; the record column is correct in **192/243**. Swapping every A and B gives the same accuracies under state B. With the equal prior, the full catalogue therefore gives **376/486** correct classifications versus **384/486**.

You can reproduce the action-log total without summing 32 rows. Conditional on the true state, the probability that a pair disagrees is $u=2q(1-q)$, where q is signal accuracy. The final classification is correct if the first pair starts a correct cascade, if a disagreeing first pair is followed by a correct second pair, or if both pairs disagree and the fifth signal is correct:

$$q^2+u q^2+u^2 q.$$

With q = 2/3, u = 4/9, this is 4/9 + 16/81 + 32/243 = **188/243**. Complete records lead to the majority of five letters: the correct-state probabilities for exactly three, four or five matching signals sum to **192/243**. This majority rule depends on the equal prior, equal quality, conditional independence and symmetric reward already declared; it is not a prescription for every committee.

### What the comparison establishes

Record access raises expected accuracy here by **4/243**, approximately **1.65 percentage points**. It does not guarantee a correct classification in any individual case. The catalogue is a probability model, not observed performance or an estimate with a sampling interval.

## Price the improvement before looking

The auditor earns **243 points if correct and zero if wrong**. Expected gross reward equals that prize multiplied by the probability of a correct classification. Thus action-log access gives **188 points**, while record access gives **192 points before its fee**.

The **gross value of record access relative to the action-log plan** is 192 − 188 = **4 points**. The fixed fee is paid once in every case, including a case where both plans choose the same state or the record-based classification is wrong. Do not subtract it once per record or only from favourable outcomes.

### When this price does not apply

Four points is an **ex ante** value for the specified menu and reward. Buying access after observing a particular history, reading only selected records, changing the reward or allowing unreliable records requires a new comparison. The most one should pay is not determined by the headcount of the group.

## Transfer to a different signal law and reward

The transfer changes two inputs to the **gross reward comparison**: signal accuracy becomes **3/4** and the correct-classification prize becomes **512 points**. The prior, five-person order, conditional independence, own-signal tie rule, records, access timing and zero reward for a wrong classification remain fixed. The fee is now **8 points**.

Before opening the solution, work out which state the records A A B B B favour. Then compare the two access plans over the whole new law. You may use the two-pair formula above; do not reuse the old 4-point value after changing both its probability and reward inputs.

$$\frac9{16}+\frac{27}{128}+\frac{27}{256}=\frac{225}{256}=\frac{450}{512}.$$

## Carry the audit to a new group

The transferable procedure starts with observable information. List what each participant sees, what rule maps that information to an action, what the auditor sees and when the access decision is made. Then compute likelihoods for those observations, rather than treating each visible choice as an independent private signal.

For a real group, those rules and likelihoods need evidence. Records of who saw earlier responses, independent source checks and tests of how choices respond to private evidence can help distinguish explanations. Five matching choices alone establish five matching choices. They do not tell us the incentive structure, accuracy, independence or honesty of the original observations.

Within this model, access to the original letters makes later hidden information available. It still leaves uncertainty about the state. Whether that access is worth buying depends on the reward, fee and timing. Revise the comparison if the signals share a source, their accuracy varies, records can be altered or participants respond to the audit itself.
