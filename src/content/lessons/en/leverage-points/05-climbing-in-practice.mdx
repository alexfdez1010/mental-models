## Step 1 — Find the loops

You can't push a system intelligently until you can *see* it, and "seeing" a system means one specific thing: mapping the **stocks** it accumulates and the **feedback loops** that drive its behaviour. This is the skill the prerequisite feedback-loops course handed you, and it's step one because everything above it depends on it. Skip it and you're not climbing a ladder — you're flailing at whatever intervention happened to come up in the meeting.

Concretely, for the misbehaviour you want to change, ask: What is the **stock** at the centre of it — the thing filling or draining (the water table, the traffic on the road, the hours lost to meetings, the trust in a wiki)? What **reinforcing loops** amplify it (the ones that turn a small trend into a runaway)? What **balancing loops** are supposed to hold it in check (and are they even present)? Where are the **delays** that make the whole thing overshoot and oscillate?

A proposed price or rule needs a specific mechanism. Mapping stocks, flows and feedback can expose that mechanism and its assumptions. The drawing is a hypothesis about the relationships; it does not establish an effect without the quantities and evidence needed for the comparison.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The one habit that does most of the work">
Before you propose a single fix, sketch the loop. Name the stock, trace the reinforcing loop that's running away, and look hard for the balancing loop that *should* be pulling it back — because a missing balancing response is one candidate explanation to investigate. A diagram can help identify what information would support or challenge it.
</Callout>

## Step 3 — Generate alternatives and compare implementations

Use the ordering to widen the search beyond the first familiar adjustment. Ask what a parameter change, a feedback response, a new information channel, a rule or an implemented goal could do. Include the baseline instead of assuming every change is an improvement.

1. Which **loop or flow** would the proposal change, and in which direction?
2. Who receives any new **information**, and which decision can respond?
3. Which **rule or incentive** changes, and what response is assumed?
4. How does a new **goal** alter operating decisions rather than wording alone?

Then compare the feasible alternatives under an explicit outcome and horizon. Preparation, approval, ongoing resources and reversibility can restrict the menu. A higher place in the guide does not automatically win; nor does a difficult approval process establish a larger effect.

## Pitfall 1 — Right point, wrong direction

Finding a plausible intervention point still leaves the direction question. Meadows warns about pushing an important relationship the wrong way. Treat that as a prompt to inspect the mechanism, not a measured claim that most people reliably find a strong lever and then misuse it.

The classic shape: a system is in trouble *because* of a runaway reinforcing loop — an economy overshooting its resource limits, a basin draining, a company burning cash to chase growth. Someone correctly identifies the growth loop as the high-leverage spot. And then, because their whole worldview says growth is good, they push it *harder* — more stimulus, more subsidies, "we'll grow our way out." They found the fire and fed it. The right-direction move was to *weaken* the reinforcing loop or *strengthen* a balancing one, not to crank the runaway. Correct spot, catastrophic direction.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The tell of a backwards push">
Before you push a high rung, run the direction check. Are you **weakening** a runaway loop or **feeding** it? **Closing** a missing feedback link or **burying** the signal in noise? Steering toward the **right** goal or doubling down on the wrong one? Potential harm depends on the actual intervention and its consequences. A parameter near a critical boundary can be damaging too; the rung alone does not set a harm ceiling.
</Callout>

## Pitfall 2 — Mistaking activity for progress

The second trap is to treat activity as evidence that an outcome improved. Meetings, fee changes, publicity and dashboards can all have effects; their quantity alone does not tell us whether the stated objective improved or why it did not.

Compare actions with the mechanism they were intended to implement. A dashboard that nobody uses may leave operations unchanged. A capacity adjustment can produce a measurable improvement. Agreement, effort and opposition are separate observations, not a scale of leverage.

## Pitfall 3 — Wrong information isn't more information

An information proposal needs the relevant signal, a recipient who can use it and a response link. Its cost and effect cannot be read from the information-flow label. This is where the rung gets abused. Someone hears "information is high-leverage" and builds a dashboard of forty metrics, most of them vanity numbers, and buries the one signal that actually mattered under a pile of noise. That's not a high-leverage information flow; it's a high-leverage *pipe pumping garbage*, and it can be worse than no dashboard at all, because now the real signal is hidden *and* everyone feels informed.

The proposed aquifer meter would show a consequence users might otherwise miss. Whether that changes extraction depends on accuracy, users’ options, incentives and responses. More readings alone do not establish that the required feedback loop has closed.

## Pitfall 4 — Big levers cut both ways

A change can help one outcome and harm another, and it may be difficult to reverse. That possibility applies to parameters as well as to rules or goals. Specify the affected quantities, implementation and boundaries before assigning either a benefit or a harm. A rung does not guarantee catastrophe, safety or reversibility.

Where a pilot can be informative and reversible, compare its outcomes with a suitable alternative and watch for displaced effects. A well-implemented capacity change can outperform a poorly implemented rule, just as another rule can outperform that capacity change under different arrivals. Choose through the stated comparison, and keep a way to revise the implementation.

Objections can expose missing assumptions or conflicting aims; they do not measure the effect. A small change is not necessarily safe, and a structural change is not necessarily worth its cost.
