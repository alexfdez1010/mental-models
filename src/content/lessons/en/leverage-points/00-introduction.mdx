A team has tried changing a target, adding a dashboard and rewriting a rule. The outcome it cares about has not improved. That result is a reason to investigate. It does not tell us, by itself, which mechanism failed or which grander-sounding proposal will work.

A **leverage point** is a place where a change can have a substantial effect through a system’s relationships. Donella Meadows’s [essay on places to intervene](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/) organises candidates from parameters through feedback, information, rules and goals to paradigms. It also qualifies that ordering and gives parameter exceptions. Treat it as a guide for asking better questions about a system.

## Start with a comparison you can specify

A physical lever is a useful opening analogy: where you apply a force affects its mechanical effect. The analogy has a limit. A service, ecosystem or organisation does not inherit a known lever-arm length merely because we call an intervention “a goal change.” Its relationships and implementation need evidence.

Three distinctions run through this course:

- **A mechanism versus its name.** A new goal must change decisions or resources to change the modeled operation. A dashboard needs someone who can act on its information.
- **A feasible plan versus a better outcome.** Preparation requirements can exclude a plan. Among feasible plans, the chosen outcome and horizon determine the comparison.
- **A model result versus evidence about the world.** Exact arithmetic is conditional on its assumptions. A real application needs evidence that those assumptions fit.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Before choosing a rung">
Name the outcome, horizon, baseline and actual implemented change. Then compare alternatives and check what happens at thresholds, limits and changed inputs. Difficulty or opposition alone does not supply that calculation.
</Callout>

### The course route

Five teaching lessons explore mechanisms and their limits; the final exam comes last.

1. **[Compare interventions in a working model](/leverage-points/01-the-counterintuition)** — calculate a four-day queue. A capacity setting and a feedback rule trade places when arrivals change. Separate setup hours, outcomes, null announcements and direction.
2. **The shallow end** — parameters, buffers, physical structure and delays. Ask what each proposed change does under the relevant system boundary; a low position in the guide does not prove a small effect in every case.
3. **Rewiring the loops** — balancing and reinforcing feedback, and information available to decision makers. A signal needs a response mechanism; its effect and cost require checking.
4. **Rules, goals and paradigms** — incentives, self-organisation and the assumptions behind a system. Distinguish a proposed operating change from a change of wording.
5. **[Comparing in practice](/leverage-points/05-climbing-in-practice)** — generate candidate mechanisms, state the assumptions they need, and investigate direction, implementation and reversibility before choosing.

This advanced course builds on **stocks and flows**, which distinguish accumulated quantities from rates, and **feedback loops**, where a system state influences an action that affects the system again. Revisit those prerequisites if the queue calculation is unfamiliar.

### How to use the comparisons

Commit to a prediction before opening a worked solution. Then reconstruct the update, including its units and timing. When an intervention wins, ask which changed input could reverse that result. This method tests your reasoning on the page; it does not promise that a real system will follow an invented example.
