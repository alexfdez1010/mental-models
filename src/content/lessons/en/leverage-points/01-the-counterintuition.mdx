A service desk has eight unfinished requests. One proposal changes a capacity number. Another installs a feedback rule. A third announces a new goal. Which deserves the preparation budget?

The labels suggest different mechanisms. To compare their effects, we need to know what each proposal actually changes, what counts as success and when we measure it. This lesson supplies those missing details in a constructed model. You can check every result without taking a ladder’s word for it.

## Define the outcome and the available plans

A **leverage point** is a place where an intervention can have a substantial effect through the system’s relationships. The claim needs a boundary: an effect on which outcome, compared with what alternative, over which period? A useful intervention need not solve every problem or last forever.

Here the **stock** is the number of unfinished requests. The objective is to minimise that stock **at the end of day 4** among the offered plans whose setup fits the preparation budget. Smaller is better. This objective does not reward a short queue on an earlier day separately.

The following inputs are stipulated, not observations of a real desk. Start with 8 requests. Exactly 10 new requests arrive each day. First choose that day’s capacity from the start-of-day stock, then add the arrivals, then complete as many requests as capacity permits. Nothing abandons, duplicates or reopens. A service slot completes one request.

### When this comparison is useful

Use an explicit contract like this when proposals are described at different levels of abstraction. “Change the goal” is incomplete until it says what decisions or resources will change. A proposed rule also needs its threshold, timing and response capacity.

## Work the capacity change through all four days

Let $B_t$ be unfinished requests at the start of a day, $A$ arrivals during that day, and $C_t$ service slots available during that day. Both $A$ and $C_t$ count requests over this one-day interval: capacity 12 requests/day supplies 12 slots in the update. Actual completions are the smaller of available requests and those slots:

$$D_t=\min(B_t+A,C_t),\qquad B_{t+1}=B_t+A-D_t=\max(0,B_t+A-C_t).$$

For P on day 1: 8+10=18 requests are available; capacity 12 completes 12;18−12=6 remain. On day 2, 6+10−12=4 remain. Repeat the same update. For N, arrivals and capacity are both 10, so the old stock of 8 never shrinks.

## Check what the feedback rule actually does

F responds to the stock: it observes unfinished requests and selects capacity accordingly. That closes a **feedback link** from a system state to a later action. Its exact rule matters more than that description.

On day 1, the start stock 8 exceeds 4, so F uses 14: 8+10−14=4 remain. On day 2 the start stock is **exactly 4**. The condition is *greater than 4*, not greater than or equal to 4. Capacity therefore falls to 10, which equals arrivals. The stock stays 4 on each remaining day.

## Separate feasibility, null effects and harmful direction

The setup budget first determines which plans are feasible. Then the outcome ranks those plans. With budget 1, only N and G fit; both leave 8 requests. There are **two equally good feasible plans**. N uses less setup, but no secondary preference for cheaper setup has been declared. With budget 2, P becomes feasible and wins at 0. With budget 4, F joins the menu, but P still wins at 0.

G’s announcement costs preparation time yet changes no operational input. It follows the baseline path. This demonstrates the null effect of **this announcement-only plan**. It does not show that an implemented goal change is ineffective; such a change might alter capacity, rules or incentives, which would need specifying.

Direction and the boundary matter even within constant-capacity plans:

### What resistance can tell us

Objections may reveal implementation obstacles, competing objectives or costs missing from the model. They deserve investigation. However, the amount of disagreement supplies no value for capacity, arrivals or the response rule. We have no resistance variable or measured relationship connecting opposition to outcomes. Easy approval alone cannot invalidate P; controversy alone cannot establish that G will work.

## Use Meadows’s ordering as a guide to candidates

Donella Meadows proposed an ordering of places to intervene, from parameters through stocks and flows, feedback, information, rules and goals to paradigms. Her essay also gives parameter exceptions and closes with qualifications about the ordering. It supports looking beyond familiar adjustments; it does not supply fixed effect sizes or an identity between difficulty and impact. [Meadows, parameter section and conclusion](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/).

Our model illustrates why those limits matter. P changes a capacity setting; F changes the mapping from stock to capacity; G only announces an aim. To evaluate another goal proposal, ask how it changes the operating system. To evaluate another information proposal, identify who receives the signal and which decision responds. Classification helps organise the questions; it does not answer the calculation.

Government Office for Science guidance similarly develops intervention hypotheses, assumptions and monitoring questions. A map can help specify a mechanism, while evidence is still needed to use its quantities for a real service. [Systems thinking toolkit, tools 8–11](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/systems-thinking-for-civil-servants/toolkit).

### When to recompute

Recompute when the horizon, arrivals, capacity availability, response timing, implementation costs or outcome changes. The example gives no forecasts after day 4, no quality measure and no optimal policy outside the offered menu. Transferring the method means rebuilding these quantities, not carrying the winning label into a new system.
