You have one sheet, six information blocks and room for three blocks on each face in the approved layout. “How do I fit all six on the front?” sounds like the natural question. The brief, however, may ask something else: give each person all six blocks legibly.

This lesson follows a constructed case from that first question through checking what comes out of the printer. Numbers and records are invented so every step can be audited. They are not observations of a real print shop or evidence that a creative technique always works.

## Read the brief before choosing a solution

A club is preparing information for **twelve people**. A block is one required section of content. Each person must receive **all six blocks on paper**, in the **approved legible layout**. That layout holds three blocks on each face. People may turn the sheet over: the blocks do not have to be read simultaneously.

There are at most **twelve sheets** and a budget of **thirty-six exercise credits**, abbreviated **cr**. Each sheet costs one credit and **each attempt to print a face** costs another, including an attempt that leaves it blank. The budget covers the entire run, including checks and repeats.

A **problem representation** is how you express the problem: which goal you name, which facts you include and which restrictions you take for granted. A **constraint** is a requirement a solution must respect in this brief. An **assumption** is something you take to be true; it may be justified, need checking, or not belong to the brief at all.

“Each person needs six legible blocks” preserves the goal. “All six must fit on the front” adds a restriction. Noticing the difference opens a question: could the other face help? It does not yet establish that the run will meet its budget or that every reverse face will print correctly.

### When to write down the requirements

Do it when a request already contains a solution, such as “fit more text here.” Separate what must be achieved from the proposed form. If a condition really is mandatory, changing it means agreeing a different brief; calling it an assumption is not enough.

## Use the names as two working emphases

In Edward de Bono’s teaching contrast, **vertical thinking** develops and checks a line of reasoning; **lateral thinking** deliberately seeks other starting points or representations. His [official teaching materials](https://www.debono.com/training) describe the latter as a structured approach to thinking differently.

Here the names guide questions; they do not classify people or partition all thought into two separate mechanisms. Careful analysis can also backtrack, change a premise or discover the reverse face. We do not need to assume that logic cannot generate alternatives.

In the club case, “How many blocks fit on this front?” develops the initial representation. “Does the brief require only one face?” examines that representation. “What do twelve sheets with both faces printed cost?” checks a newly generated candidate. The same person can do all three and repeat them when new data arrive.

### When to change the question

Consider another framing if candidates miss the goal or you cannot identify the source of a restriction. If the missing step is checking a sum or inspecting a printed face, start with that check. A stall is a clue to investigate; it neither identifies a brain mechanism nor requires an automatic switch of mode.

## Generate candidates and check every requirement

A **candidate** is an alternative still needing checks. We compare only the four in the table; we do not claim they exhaust every possible design.

One face in the approved layout gives each person three blocks: three are missing. Compressing six onto that face would provide all the content but violates the approved layout by stipulation. Two one-sided sheets give six blocks but require twenty-four sheets.

The two-sided candidate puts three blocks on the front and three on the reverse: `3 + 3 = 6`. For twelve people, that is twelve sheets and `12 × 2 = 24` attempts. If every attempt produces the planned output, cost is `12 + 24 = 36 cr`.

Now define a **conditional completion scenario**: the eight remaining packets succeed, and one additional attempt completes the blank reverse on the same sheet. We stipulate this to calculate an account; we do not forecast it from three successes out of four or guarantee a real repeat will work.

The first four already consumed four sheets and eight attempts: `4 + 8 = 12 cr`. The eight remaining packets would consume eight sheets and sixteen attempts: `8 + 16 = 24 cr`. The repeat would use no new sheet and one more attempt: `1 cr`.

The total would be `12 + 24 + 1 = 37 cr`, using the same twelve sheets. It exceeds the budget by one credit. The initial account did not forget the price per face; it assumed two attempts per packet would be enough. The log requires revisiting that execution assumption.

None of the four candidates has been validated to finish within this brief under this scenario: the first three already failed a requirement, and the fourth would exceed the budget when completed as stipulated. That does not prove no other design exists. You can seek more candidates or obtain authorization to change resources or requirements; you cannot declare compliance without doing so.

### When a check changes the next step

If a face lacks content, check what was printed and how to complete the packet. If a cost changes, recalculate. A new representation does not replace those tasks. Repeating a real run would need evidence about the process and resources for checks; here we audited an invented log only.

## Transfer the method to a different requirement

Another brief is for **eight people**. Each needs **four blocks**, and the approved layout holds **two per face**. There are **eight sheets** and **twenty-four credits**. A sheet and each attempt to print a face still cost one credit each.

Now one condition changes the comparison: all four blocks must be **visible at once**, and only one face of each sheet is visible at any one time. Turning it over later is insufficient. This condition is written into the new brief.

Consider the same catalogue: one approved face; one compressed face outside the layout; two one-sided sheets; and one two-sided sheet. Before opening the solution, calculate content, simultaneous visibility, paper and cost. Which candidates meet every condition?

The reverse face has not become generally useless: the requirement changed. Using it here would require agreement to let blocks be viewed sequentially; that would be an authorized change of brief. You could also investigate designs outside the catalogue. We have not proved global impossibility.

### When to reuse a framing

Reuse the questions, not the earlier verdict. Check which goal or constraint changed. A solution correct under “receive everything” can fail under “see everything at once.”

## Decide which work is missing

The first representation may be useful or need revision. Ask what blocks progress: a missing fact, an unchecked account, candidates that miss the goal, or a convention treated as a requirement? These questions guide the next step without diagnosing a personality or a brain channel.

In the first brief, questioning “only the front” opened an option. Later, inspecting reverse faces exposed an execution mismatch. In the transfer, rereading “at once” prevented copying an answer that missed the goal. Work returns from data to assumptions when needed; it does not require a one-direction relay.

### When to stop changing the framing

If a proposal meets the requirements and its assumptions are sufficiently checked for the particular decision, careful execution may be next. Novelty alone adds no compliance. If a check changes what you believed, revise what needs revising rather than defending a label.
