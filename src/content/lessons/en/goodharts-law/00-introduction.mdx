Use an invented factory illustration. A factory is rewarded for the **weight** of nails, so it makes a few giant, useless nails. Change the target to the **number** of nails and suppose it makes tiny, useless tacks instead. In both versions, it meets the target while missing the goal: useful nails. This illustrates a possible incentive problem; it is not historical evidence or a prediction about every factory.

The familiar warning is **Goodhart's Law**: *when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.* Read that as a reason to test a proxy under optimisation, not a theorem that every target must fail. The factory's number and purpose separate because its available shortcuts pay. Whether they pay is part of the explanation.

## What this course gives you

Goodhart's Law is one of those models that, once you see it, you cannot unsee. It's hiding inside every KPI, every league table, every "we hit our targets", every algorithm optimising for engagement. By the end you'll be able to name the mechanism precisely, recognise it in a dozen disguises, and — crucially — know what to actually *do* instead of throwing your hands up. Here's the map:

1. **Proxy vs goal** — compare genuine improvement with score inflation, including the actions and costs that make each worthwhile.
2. **How the seams split** — the mechanism of decoupling under pressure: Campbell's Law and the four flavours of Goodhart.
3. **A hundred costumes** — the flagship disasters, from cobra bounties to clickbait to the 2008 risk models, all wearing the same skeleton.
4. **Why it's a special case** — how *incentives*, *feedback loops* and the *principal–agent problem* can combine in strategic metric gaming.
5. **What to do about it** — safeguards, their costs, and the ways quoting "Goodhart!" becomes its own lazy excuse.
6. **[Audit a target rule](/goodharts-law/06-audit-a-target-rule)** — solve a complete response model, compare rules and distinguish gaming from a changed population.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The one-sentence version">
When a metric stands in for a goal, targeting it can change the behaviour or population that made it useful. Check the actual goal after those changes; a rising score alone cannot establish success.
</Callout>

## A quick taste: the honest number that goes bad

Picture a school where, quietly, good teaching and good test scores rise together. Nobody's gaming anything, so the scores are an *honest* read on learning — a teacher with better scores really does teach better. The number is trustworthy.

Now suppose those scores decide teacher pay and funding, and staff respond by excluding weak pupils from the test rather than improving teaching. Scores rise while learning stays unchanged. In this constructed case the changed behaviour weakens the score as evidence of learning. Pay alone does not prove that response happened; you would need evidence about teaching, participation and learning.

## How to use this course

Every lesson opens with a quick guess, teaches the idea through a concrete case, and checks that it stuck. Don't skip the guesses — trying to answer *before* you know is one of the most reliable ways to remember. The next lesson compares productive work and score inflation in one declared action menu, retaining the exact ties as rewards change. Exercises and a finite workshop let you check the assumptions.

When you've finished the six teaching lessons, a graded final exam pulls it all together. It's one-way — once you submit an answer it's locked — so treat the practice questions along the way as exactly that: practice.

<Callout variant="warning" title="One thing to unlearn as you go">
"We hit our numbers" feels like success and often *is*. But this course will leave you unable to hear that phrase without a second question forming automatically: *did the thing the number stood for improve too — or did someone just get very good at moving the number?* That reflex is the whole skill.
</Callout>
