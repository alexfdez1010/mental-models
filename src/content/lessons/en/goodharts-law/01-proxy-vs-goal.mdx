The dashboard awards ten points. Eight broken items are still waiting for repair. The scorekeeper is delighted; the queue has had a less exciting afternoon.

A reward can encourage useful work. It can also make a shortcut worthwhile. To tell which happens, separate **what the organisation wants**, **what it scores** and **what a person can do to earn that score**. This lesson calculates those distinctions in a complete, invented case.

## Name the goal and the score separately

A **goal** is the result being sought. A **proxy** is an observed stand-in used to judge or reward that result. A score can be convenient to collect while leaving important parts of the goal unmeasured.

Think of a workshop’s progress board. Ticking a box can record a completed repair; a box can also be ticked through a procedure that leaves the item broken. Whether that second route exists is a fact to investigate, rather than a property of all progress boards.

Our fictional workshop has eight broken items. Its goal quantity **G** is the number that pass an independent working test after the shift. Its administrative score **M** awards form points, on a scale from zero to ten. Repairs and form points are different units. A score of six is not a claim that exactly six items work.

The independent test lets the learner audit the result after the shift. It does not supply an extra reward to the worker. All values below are stipulated teaching inputs, not observations of an actual organisation.

| Quantity | What it records | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| G | Items passing the working test | Working repairs, from zero to eight |
| M | Points awarded by the administrative form | Form points, from zero to ten |
| C | The worker’s cost of an action | Fictional utility credits, abbreviated cr |

A common mistake is to let a familiar name settle the distinction: a column called “quality” can still score something narrower than the desired result. Write the operational definition of each column before interpreting a change.

### When to make this distinction

Use it before treating a rising number as evidence of success. Ask which outcome matters, how the number is recorded and how the outcome can be checked independently. A useful proxy need not have the same unit or numerical value as the goal.

## Specify what the worker can choose

The worker takes **exactly one** of three legal actions. Actions cannot be combined. There is one shift, no borrowing, no later reward and no uncertainty about these stipulated outcomes.

| Action | Goal G, working repairs | Score M, form points | Cost C, cr |
|---|---|---|---|
| Idle | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Repair | 8 | 6 | 2 |
| Relabel | 0 | 10 | 5 |

Repair completes all eight repairs and earns six form points under this rubric. Relabel changes the presentation enough to earn ten points while completing no repair. Relabelling is permitted in this constructed menu; the result does not depend on declaring the worker dishonest.

The organisation publicly offers a reward weight **w**, between zero and one **credit per form point**. Each weight considered is a separate offered rule. The worker sees the rule and chooses an action that maximises its own net utility:

$$U=wM-C.$$

An extra credit has the same utility value in every outcome. The three net utilities are therefore:

$$U_I=0,\qquad U_R=6w-2,\qquad U_L=10w-5.$$

The subscripts name Idle, Repair and Relabel. A **best response** is an action attaining the largest net utility among the available actions. Keep every maximising action at a tie. A tie between two inferior actions does not make either one optimal.

The reward weight is offered from outside this calculation. We solve the worker’s response to it. Choosing an optimal contract for the organisation would need an organisational objective and the costs of implementing the alternatives.

### When to use an action menu

Use one when a claim about incentives skips from a rule to an assumed response. Include useful work, goal-avoiding routes and feasible outside options. The menu here is complete by assumption; a real investigation has to look for omitted actions and changing costs.

## Work through three offered rewards

At **w = 1/2 cr per point**, Repair earns a reward of 6 × 1/2 = 3 cr, then pays its cost of 2 cr: net utility **1 cr**. Relabel earns 10 × 1/2 = 5 cr and costs 5 cr: net utility **0 cr**. Idle also gives 0. Repair uniquely maximises utility, so the goal result is **eight working repairs**.

### When a comparison is informative

Compare the predicted or observed goal result as well as the score. A response calculation depends on available actions and payoffs; it supplies a conditional prediction for that model. To use it at work, check whether the menu and costs describe what people can actually do.

## Locate the exact changes and retain ties

Three trial weights do not locate every boundary. Compare the utility formulas directly.

Repair beats Idle when **6w − 2 > 0**, or **w > 1/3**. Relabel beats Repair when **(10w − 5) − (6w − 2) = 4w − 3 > 0**, or **w > 3/4**.

We must also compare Relabel with Idle. Relabel’s utility crosses zero at **w = 1/2**. That crossing does not change the best response: Repair already exceeds both there. For w below one third, both active actions are below zero. Between one third and three quarters, Repair exceeds Idle and Relabel. Above three quarters, Relabel exceeds Repair and is positive. These inequalities cover the whole declared interval.

### When the boundary matters

Retain ties when a threshold determines a predicted response. A rounded decimal can hide an equality, and a display that selects one action does not prove that the action is uniquely optimal. Distinguish a complete set of allowed responses from one selected example.

## Separate reward alignment from information

In the high-reward case, a larger score accompanies fewer working repairs. The **ranking** “more M means more G” fails in this menu. That does not mean M has lost every use.

Here all three actions have different scores. If the declared menu is known and the score is recorded accurately, **M = 0 identifies Idle, M = 6 identifies Repair and M = 10 identifies Relabel**. An auditor could use that lookup to recover G. Paying a reward proportional to M and interpreting M through a known lookup are different procedures.

A **correlation** describes how recorded quantities vary together across a specified collection or distribution. This action menu supplies no population weights or sample frequencies, so it supplies no numerical correlation coefficient. Nor would an observed association by itself establish that changing the score causes a repair.

The distinction resembles a thermometer display connected to a bonus scheme: reading a number and rewarding increases in it are separate operations. Whether a real score remains informative depends on the recording process, available actions and evidence connecting it with the goal.

### When this distinction is useful

Use it when “the metric is broken” could mean several things: the reward favours the wrong action, an ordering is unreliable, a record cannot identify the underlying outcome, or a statistical relation has changed. Name the claim and test the evidence it requires.

## Change one cost and audit again

Keep the actions, G, M and reward interval. Change only the cost of Relabel from **5 cr to 7 cr**. Its new utility is **10w − 7**; Repair remains **6w − 2**.

Try the changed menu at **w = 1 cr per point** before opening the calculation.

### When to recalculate

Recalculate when a safeguard changes costs, observations, actions or the reward. A proposed audit may be expensive, imperfect or avoidable. Its name supplies no numerical effect. This transfer demonstrates sensitivity to a cost assumption, rather than recommending an optimal reward or a universal defence.

## Reconstruct a metric audit

Start with the desired outcome and its unit. Define the recorded score and how it is produced. List available actions and costs, including doing nothing where feasible. Compute best responses under each offered rule, retain ties, and evaluate the goal after the response. Check what the recorded score can still identify and what further evidence a causal or statistical claim needs.

### Sources and scope

Manheim and Garrabrant’s [categorisation of Goodhart effects](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.04585v4) distinguishes several mechanisms and explains why an intervention can change a metric without necessarily worsening the underlying goal. The workshop, action menu, credits and thresholds here are constructed. The calculations follow from the stated utilities; they are not empirical estimates of workers’ behaviour.
