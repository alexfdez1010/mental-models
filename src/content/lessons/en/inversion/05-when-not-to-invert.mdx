You are planning a workshop for 20 people. You check the booking, test the
projector, and confirm that everyone can enter the building. Those checks reduce
specific risks to the workshop. They do not yet tell you what to teach
or whether your activity will help people practise it.

Inversion helps you inspect a plan for failure. Its value depends on how you
connect that inspection to the goal, the evidence, and the next decision.

## A safeguard does not choose the goal

Separate a **necessary condition** from a **sufficient condition**. A necessary
condition is something the plan needs; a sufficient condition would establish
the result. For this in-person workshop, being able to enter the room is necessary.
It is insufficient to establish that the participants will learn the skill.

Inversion can also suggest new alternatives. Finding that an activity cannot run
without the projector may lead you to prepare a paper-based alternative. The limit
is that identifying this safeguard does not compare every possible activity or
prove which one teaches best.

### When to use this distinction

Use inversion to check an activity you have proposed. Use forward thinking to
state the skill, design alternatives, and decide what successful practice would
look like. Move between the two as the plan develops.

## A large prize needs a probability

Suppose one experiment costs 20 euros. If it works, it returns 200 euros before
that cost; if it fails, it returns nothing. The loss is limited to 20 euros only
if there are no further commitments or hidden costs.

To compare the potential reward with the cost, you also need an estimate of the
chance of success. Let **p** be that probability: 2% means p = 0.02. The
**expected net result** is the probability-weighted average of the outcomes,
not a forecast of what one attempt will pay:

**Expected net result = p × 200 − 20 euros**

| Chance of success | Calculation | Expected net result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2% | 0.02 × 200 − 20 | −16 euros |
| 20% | 0.20 × 200 − 20 | +20 euros |

The possible prize and the maximum loss are identical in both rows. The
probability changes the comparison. At 20%, one attempt can still lose the full
20 euros; a positive average does not promise a win. Repeated attempts also add
costs: five failures cost 5 × 20 = 100 euros.

### When to lead with exploration

If you need new alternatives, generate them before filtering out every uncertain
one. Then use inversion to look for uncounted costs and unrecoverable losses.
A survivable downside can make an experiment feasible; the chance of success,
value of what you learn, and competing uses of your resources still matter.

## Set a stopping rule for the review

A risk review needs a decision rule. Otherwise every newly imagined problem
can reopen it, even when it changes nothing about the plan.

For the workshop, suppose the team agrees to confirm room access, test the
activity with two people, and assign someone to check the equipment before
opening. Once those checks are complete, the next step is a small rehearsal.
If the rehearsal exposes an inaccessible room or an activity that nobody can
complete, reopen the relevant part of the plan. If a repeated concern adds no new information
and would not change the next decision or a safeguard, record it and return to
the next step.

Prioritise risks by their possible severity, likelihood, reversibility, and the
cost and effectiveness of the response. A rare severe outcome can deserve a
separate limit; an uncertain probability is not evidence that it is negligible.
Do not treat a short failure list as proof that the plan is safe.

### A pitfall to watch

A stopping rule is not permission to ignore a new warning. It says which evidence
would change the decision and which checks are enough for the next reversible
step. High-consequence decisions may need specialist standards and review.

## Choose the next question

| What you need now | Useful question |
| --- | --- |
| Generate an activity | What could help participants practise this skill? |
| Check a proposed activity | What would prevent participants from completing it? |
| Compare experiments | What are the costs, possible benefits, and probabilities? |
| Turn a risk into action | Who checks what, and when? |
| Decide whether to continue | Have the agreed checks passed, and has new evidence changed the plan? |

Inversion and forward thinking can support the same experiment. Finding a failure
mode may suggest a design; generating a design may expose a new failure mode.
Use each question to advance the decision rather than assigning yourself a fixed
identity as a cautious or a bold thinker.
