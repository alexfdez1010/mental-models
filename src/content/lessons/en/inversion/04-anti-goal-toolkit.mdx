A job offer promises more interesting work, but also a longer commute. Before deciding, you need both a positive aim and a boundary: what do you want the job to provide, and which cost are you unwilling to accept?

An **anti-goal** makes an unwanted outcome explicit. This lesson turns that boundary into questions, checks and a decision, while keeping external events separate from the choices you control.

## Anti-goals: deciding what you refuse to do

A goal states what you want: “work on projects that develop my skills”. An **anti-goal** states an outcome you want to avoid: “a working week that routinely takes more than 45 hours including travel”. A usable boundary adds a definition of routinely, an observation and a response.

For example: count scheduled work and travel each week; review after four weeks; if the agreed limit is repeatedly exceeded, discuss scope or working arrangements before accepting more work. The appropriate limit is a personal decision, not a universal rule.

An anti-goal differs from a **safeguard**. The anti-goal names the unwanted result; a safeguard acts against a cause of it. “No required weekend availability” can be checked in a proposed contract. “No client ever emails at midnight” depends on other people and is a poor description of what you can enforce.

<Callout variant="info" title="Goal vs. anti-goal">
Separate **what you want to avoid**, **what you can control** and **what you will check**. You can decide which availability requirement to accept; you cannot guarantee when another person sends a message.
</Callout>

A boundary can help compare offers before you are committed. It should not hide a trade-off: refusing weekend availability may exclude some roles you otherwise like. Make exceptions explicit, including what counts as an emergency and who decides. A boundary is useful when you can explain its purpose and act when it is crossed.

## Inverting your own decisions

Before accepting the job, ask: “Six months later, the role does not meet my needs. What did I fail to check?” That directs attention to information you can seek before committing.

Do not turn the question into “everything bad that happens must be my fault”. Some conditions are external, and some remain uncertain even after a careful decision. You may be able to monitor, negotiate or prepare a fallback without controlling the cause itself.

1. **Name the positive aim and decision.** Which offer are you comparing, and what do you want it to provide?
2. **Imagine a relevant mismatch.** Specify what would make the role unacceptable under your criteria.
3. **Identify evidence to seek.** A bad feeling is a reason to ask a question, not a conclusion about a person.
4. **Choose a response.** Check a condition, negotiate, change the plan, accept a residual risk or decline.

<Reveal label="Worked decision: a job with a longer commute" buttonLabel="Compare the boundary and the options">

**Aim:** Accept a role with useful learning opportunities while keeping scheduled work and travel within a 45-hour week.

**Offer:** 40 hours of work plus 10 hours of weekly travel: **50 hours**, which exceeds the chosen boundary by **5 hours**.

**Failure path:** Accept the role based on its title and pay without checking whether the travel requirement can change.

**Evidence to seek:** Confirm the working hours, travel days and any remote arrangement with the employer. Ask about typical demands rather than assuming the advertised schedule covers every week.

**Possible safeguard:** Negotiate two remote days. If each saves two travel hours, the total becomes **50 − 4 = 46 hours**. That still exceeds 45; the safeguard does not yet meet the boundary.

**Decision:** Seek another arrangement, compare another offer, or deliberately revise the boundary after weighing the benefits. Do not quietly call 46 “within 45”. A clear anti-goal makes the trade-off visible; it does not decide what matters most to you.

</Reveal>

## Now stack the two directions

Apply both directions to the same decision rather than using the avoid-list as a substitute for choosing.

| Step | Question | Job example |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | What result do I want? | Work that develops relevant skills |
| Invert | What would make it unsuitable? | Regularly exceeding my work-and-travel limit |
| Safeguard | What can I check or change? | Confirm hours and negotiate travel days |
| Advance | Which option best serves the goal within my constraints? | Compare the revised offer with alternatives |

The safeguard can suggest a new option, such as a different working arrangement. You still have to assess whether that option meets the aim and its conditions. If every available offer crosses the boundary, acknowledge the conflict and reconsider the options or the boundary explicitly.

## When review stops changing the decision

A decision review loses usefulness when it repeats the same feared outcomes without producing new evidence, a changed action or a decision. That pattern is different from a bounded attempt to inspect a plan. This is a distinction about how the exercise is used, not a diagnosis of someone's mental health.

Set a scope and a review time appropriate to the stakes. At the end, record the important unresolved questions, actions and accepted uncertainties. Proceed, revise, seek missing information or stop according to that record. Reopen the review if new evidence could change the choice; do not dismiss a real new warning merely because an earlier checklist was completed.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Invert, then close the loop">
End a review with **a decision, actions or a specific information need**. Give the next review a trigger. Repeating a scenario without new evidence adds little, while a material new fact deserves attention.
</Callout>
