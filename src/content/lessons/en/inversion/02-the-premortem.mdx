The release plan has dates, owners and a demonstration that works on one laptop. Before committing to it, the team imagines a different scene: it is a week after launch and users cannot finish their first task. What might explain that result?

A **premortem** applies inversion while you can still change a plan. It produces possible causes to investigate, rather than a prediction that the project will fail.

## What a pre-mortem is

A **premortem**, a planning technique associated with psychologist Gary Klein, asks a team to imagine that a planned project has missed its objective and work backwards to plausible causes. Choose a concrete result and date: “It is a week after launch; new users cannot complete setup. What happened?”

Treat failure as given **within the exercise**. Outside the exercise, its causes remain hypotheses. The team may discover an untested assumption, confirm a known dependency or find that a feared scenario has little support. Those outcomes should lead to different responses.

<Callout variant="info" title="Pre-mortem vs. post-mortem">
A **postmortem** reviews an actual event to learn from what happened. A **premortem** explores an imagined failure early enough to change a plan. Both can improve later work; neither makes every cause preventable.
</Callout>

## What the failure prompt changes

In a hopeful planning meeting, raising a concern can feel like opposing the project. Assigning everyone the task of explaining an imagined failure changes what counts as a useful contribution. The engineer worried about the schedule no longer has to be the only person introducing a negative possibility.

This can help surface overlooked assumptions, but it does not automatically remove **optimism bias** or **groupthink**. A manager who dismisses uncomfortable answers can still silence the room. Start with independent written answers, allow concerns about the plan itself, and ask for the evidence behind each cause before deciding what to do.

## How to run one — the five steps

1. **Specify the plan and imagined result.** Use an objective and a review date that everyone understands; “a disaster someday” is too vague.
2. **Write independently first.** Ask each person for plausible causes before anyone hears the manager's answer. This limits one route by which the first opinion can shape the rest.
3. **Collect causes before debating them.** Separate a cause (“the import was never checked”) from an outcome (“users lost records”). Keep dissenting explanations visible.
4. **Investigate and prioritise.** Consider evidence, likelihood, severity, reversibility and the cost of a response. Mark uncertainty instead of assigning invented precision.
5. **Assign actions and review triggers.** Name an owner, a deadline and the observation that will show whether the safeguard works. Record accepted risks and when to reconsider them.

<Reveal label="Worked premortem: launching a mobile app" buttonLabel="Show causes, checks and actions">

**Plan:** Launch in three months. A first-time user should be able to create an account and complete the first task.

**Imagined result:** One week after launch, many new users cannot finish that task.

| Possible cause | Evidence needed | Action before launch | Owner and check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Setup instructions use unexplained terms | Observe first-time users attempting setup | Revise the terms and test the revised flow | Product owner checks completion and remaining points of confusion |
| An external service rejects launch traffic | Confirm limits and test expected load | Reduce the load or prepare a fallback | Engineering owner runs the load test before approval |
| Import drops records on an older device | Compare records before and after import | Fix the import and verify recovery | Test owner checks supported devices and recovery results |

These actions reduce particular risks. They do not establish product demand or remove every launch failure.

**A numerical priority check:** Suppose two monetary losses are estimated at a 20% chance of €5,000 and a 2% chance of €50,000. Both have an expected loss of **€1,000**: 0.20 × 5,000 and 0.02 × 50,000. The second is rarer but could be harder to survive. Expected loss alone does not choose the safeguard; check the loss limit, evidence for the probabilities and the cost of mitigation. These are estimates for the example, not measured launch rates.

</Reveal>

## The same flip, scaled up

The core operation has not changed; it now applies to dependencies across a project.

| Inversion step | Premortem application |
|---|---|
| Define the goal | State the result the project should deliver |
| Consider the opposite | Imagine that result was missed at a specified date |
| Trace failure paths | Gather and investigate possible causes |
| Choose safeguards | Assign actions against the important causes |
| Verify and continue | Check completion, accept residual risks and revisit triggers |

## Prioritise without treating every risk equally

A list of forty possible failures is a set of candidates, not forty equally urgent assignments. Prioritisation should distinguish minor inconvenience, a recoverable loss and a failure that makes the whole project unacceptable.

Do not equate “rare” with “safe to ignore”. A low-probability irreversible loss can deserve a response, while a frequent typo may not. Some requirements are conditions for proceeding at all. If probabilities are unknown, ask what observation, pilot or limit would make a decision possible rather than inventing a number.

Set a time for the review, identify actions and residual risks, then decide whether to proceed, revise or stop. Reopen the review when a defined trigger occurs or material new evidence appears. Repeating the same list without changing evidence or action adds little.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Choose the response using the consequences">
Prioritise by **likelihood, severity, reversibility and response cost**. A serious rare risk may outrank a common inconvenience. “No action now” should mean a reasoned acceptance or a monitoring plan, rather than forgetting the risk.
</Callout>
