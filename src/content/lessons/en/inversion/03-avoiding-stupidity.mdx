A dispatch team sends 100 orders a week. Eight usually need rework because an address field is missing. The team could spend its next hour designing a faster packing method or checking the information that repeatedly causes those returns.

Charlie Munger's advice to avoid costly mistakes suggests a useful question: **how much could we gain by removing a recurring error we already understand?** The answer depends on the size of the loss, the effectiveness of the safeguard and the opportunity cost of the effort. It is not a rule that caution wins in every field.

## The "loser's game" insight

The **loser's game** analogy describes a contest in which avoidable errors account for much of the result. Imagine a tennis match where most points end with an unforced error. Keeping the ball in play may help more than attempting a difficult winner that often misses.

A **winner's game** draws attention to situations where creating a superior result matters more. These are lenses for a particular task, not permanent labels for whole professions. A researcher needs both reliable measurements and an original question; a business needs both accurate dispatch and a product people want.

Look at the errors and opportunities in front of you. If the dispatch team's losses repeatedly come from missing information, preventing that error is a candidate worth evaluating. The tennis analogy does not establish how much it will save or whether another improvement would save more.

<Callout variant="info" title="Examine what determines this task">
Ask **what currently determines the result**. When recurring avoidable errors dominate, prevention can be valuable. When the missing ingredient is a useful idea or a working product, an avoid-list will not supply it by itself. Many tasks need both.
</Callout>

## Compare prevention with the alternatives

A known recurring error has a practical advantage: you can often inspect its cause, estimate its cost and check whether a safeguard changes it. That makes it easier to evaluate than a vague aspiration such as “be exceptional”. A concrete new improvement can be evaluated too.

Return to the dispatch example. Each of the eight errors takes **15 minutes** to correct, so the weekly rework is **8 × 15 = 120 minutes**. Suppose a proposed check takes **20 minutes a week** and would prevent five of the eight errors. It would avoid **5 × 15 = 75 minutes** of rework, for a net saving of **75 − 20 = 55 minutes**. The three remaining errors would still need attention.

This is a conditional calculation. Before adopting the check, investigate whether it really prevents those five errors and whether it creates delays elsewhere. Compare the 55-minute saving with the best alternative use of the same effort. Inversion identifies a candidate; the comparison decides whether it is worthwhile.

## Apply the failure question to team management

Apply the same reasoning to team management. “Support the team” can remain abstract until you ask what might prevent people from doing useful work. Conflicting priorities, missing decision authority and credit that ignores contribution are concrete candidates.

<Reveal label="Worked transfer: make a study session useful" buttonLabel="Show the failure paths and checks">

**Goal:** At the end of a study session, solve a new problem using the method just studied.

**Possible failure:** You can recognise a worked solution but cannot choose the first step on a new problem.

**Cause to investigate:** Every practice item displayed its solution before you tried it. You never checked whether you could begin independently.

**Safeguard:** Include one new problem without its solution visible. Write or select the first step, then use the explanation to compare your reasoning. If the step is wrong, identify the missing concept and try another item after reviewing it.

**Limit:** One correct item is evidence about that item, not proof that you can solve every problem or retain the method indefinitely. The forward work still includes learning the method and choosing suitable practice.

## Protect the ability to recover

Some errors consume more than rework time. Losing the only copy of a dataset can remove months of work; an inaccurate backup label can make the supposed protection unusable. Prevention matters especially when recovery is difficult or the loss is irreversible.

This connects inversion with **margin of safety**, a buffer against an estimate being wrong, and **compounding**, in which earlier results affect later ones. Protecting continuity can preserve the chance to improve. It does not guarantee that improvement will occur, and a backup is useful only if you can recover the needed data from it.

## The boundary: don't confuse 'not losing' with 'winning'

A dispatch process can be accurate and still ship a product nobody wants. A study session can avoid the identified mistakes and still omit an essential concept. Successful prevention supports the desired result without establishing it.

Use a short avoid-list when the causal path is understood and the protection is worth its cost. Keep room for positive work and revise the list when conditions change. An error that was common last year may no longer be the best use of attention.

## Where this goes next

Next, apply these distinctions to a personal decision: define what you want, identify an unwanted outcome and choose a boundary or check you can actually use. A boundary can guide your own action without giving you control over every external event.
