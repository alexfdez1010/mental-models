You are preparing an online workshop. The goal is clear: participants should leave able to use one new tool. Yet “make it a good workshop” does not tell you what to check first.

Try the opposite question: **what would make it unusable?** Send the wrong link, assume knowledge participants do not have, or use every minute for explanation and leave none for practice. Each answer points to a check you can perform before the session starts. This is the planning use of **inversion**.

## The flip, in one breath

**Inversion** approaches a problem through its opposite. In this course, we use it to ask how an unwanted outcome could occur, then work backwards to the actions or conditions that could produce it. “How would I guarantee failure?” is a deliberately strong brainstorming prompt; it does not mean that every item on your list is certain to cause failure.

The useful result is a **failure path** and a **safeguard**. A failure path explains how a particular action could undermine the goal. A safeguard is a concrete check or change aimed at that path. Merely saying “avoid failure” supplies neither.

Before the example, try choosing a question that could reveal a practical check.

## A tiny example you can feel

Suppose the workshop lasts **30 minutes** and the exercise requires **8 minutes**. Your draft allocates 27 minutes to the explanation and 3 minutes to practice. The failure path is explicit: participants cannot complete an eight-minute task in the three minutes available.

Invert the plan into a safeguard. Reserve the eight minutes first, leaving **30 − 8 = 22 minutes** for explanation, instructions and transitions. Rehearse against that limit and prepare a shorter example if necessary. You can check the timing before participants arrive.

This does not establish that everyone will learn the tool. The exercise might still be unclear or require knowledge you have not taught. It does show exactly which problem the safeguard addresses and how you would check it.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The one-sentence version">
Inversion = **identify a way the goal could fail, then design and check a safeguard.** Keep the link between the failure and the action visible; “be more careful” is too vague to verify.
</Callout>

## Why this is a *mental model*, not a party trick

A **mental model** is a simplified, reusable way to reason. Inversion transfers because the same question can reveal different failure paths in a meeting, a product launch or a study plan. The mechanism in the workshop was a time mismatch; in a meeting it might be ending without naming who will do the agreed work.

Its usefulness depends on the problem. A vague success goal may become easier to examine when you ask how it could fail, but a well-specified forward plan can be concrete too. Neither direction automatically produces the better answer.

Use inversion to inspect a plan, then return to the positive goal. A workshop that avoids broken links and timing errors still needs an explanation, a suitable exercise and feedback.

## The map of the course

1. **Forward and backward thinking** — follow a release meeting from its goal through a failure path to a safeguard and a check.
2. **The premortem** — gather possible causes of a missed project objective, distinguish hypotheses from evidence and prioritise responses.
3. **Avoid costly errors** — calculate the possible value of preventing recurring rework and compare it with other improvements.
4. **The anti-goal toolkit** — make a decision boundary observable and test whether a proposed job arrangement meets it.
5. **The limits of inversion** — distinguish necessary from sufficient conditions and evaluate a small experiment with explicit probabilities and costs.
6. **[Premortem workshop](/inversion/06-premortem-workshop)** — turn failure hypotheses into capacity checks, preparation priorities and launch conditions.

### How to use this course

Choose an answer before checking an exercise. Then read the explanation, including why a tempting alternative does not fit. If you miss one, identify the step you skipped: defining the goal, explaining the failure path or connecting the safeguard to its cause.
