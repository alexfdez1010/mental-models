A meeting has 20 minutes to decide whether a proposed release is ready. “Run a good meeting” gives little direction. “What would leave us unable to decide?” points towards missing test results, absent decision-makers or an unclear acceptance criterion.

Both directions can produce concrete actions. This lesson shows what the backward question adds, then follows one failure path all the way to a checkable safeguard.

## Two directions, two different views

**Forward thinking** reasons towards a desired result: “What evidence do we need to approve the release?” **Backward thinking**, used as inversion here, starts with the unwanted result: “The meeting ended without a decision; what prevented it?”

| | Forward thinking | Backward thinking |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | The outcome we want | An unwanted outcome |
| Useful question | What would achieve the goal? | What action or condition could defeat it? |
| Meeting example | Bring the test results and acceptance criteria | Missing results leave the decision unsupported |
| What to check | Will the proposed action help? | Does the safeguard interrupt the failure path? |
| Possible blind spot | An overlooked obstacle | A positive opportunity the avoid-list does not create |

The advantage is a change of perspective. A failure prompt can expose a dependency that an upbeat plan skipped. It can also produce implausible stories, so specificity is a starting point for checking, not proof.

<Callout variant="info" title="From a warning to a cause you can check">
A failure path becomes useful when it names **a cause, a consequence and something you can change or monitor**. “The meeting fails” names only an outcome; “the test report arrives after the meeting” identifies a dependency you can investigate.
</Callout>

## How to actually perform the flip

Use the same goal in all five steps so the exercise does not drift into a list of unrelated worries.

1. **Define the goal and its criterion.** “At the end of 20 minutes, approve or defer the release using the agreed test criterion.”
2. **Describe the unwanted result.** “The meeting ends without enough evidence to decide.”
3. **Trace a plausible cause.** “The report is not ready because the test run is scheduled too late.” Check the schedule rather than treating the story as established.
4. **Choose an action that interrupts that cause.** Move the run earlier, reduce the scope of the release or defer the decision. “Do not be late” gives no owner or change.
5. **Name a check and a response.** Confirm the report is available the day before. If not, defer approval rather than pretending the risk has disappeared.

<Reveal label="Worked example: invert a release meeting" buttonLabel="Show the worked case">

**Goal:** Decide readiness in a 20-minute meeting on Friday. Approval requires a completed test report and sign-off from the release owner.

**Imagined failure:** Friday's meeting ends with no defensible decision.

| Possible cause | Evidence to check | Safeguard | Residual uncertainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| The report arrives late | Test schedule and report preparation time | Finish the test run on Thursday; assign a report owner | A failed test run may still delay it |
| The decision-maker cannot attend | Release owner's availability | Arrange a delegate authorised to decide | The delegate may need extra context |
| Participants disagree on “ready” | Written acceptance criterion | Agree the criterion before reviewing results | A borderline result may require judgment |

**Return to the forward plan:** Send the evidence in advance, spend the meeting on unresolved questions, record the decision and who acts on it. The safeguards support that plan; they do not replace it.

</Reveal>

## Combine both directions

The meeting's backward analysis found missing evidence, an unavailable decision-maker and an ambiguous criterion. It did not choose what should be released or establish that the product serves a user need. Those questions still require a forward plan and supporting evidence.

The pairing also works in the other direction. A forward plan that already names the report, owner and criterion may need only a short inversion check. There is no benefit in repeating a long failure exercise when it adds no decision-relevant information.

## Check the failure path before trusting it

A vivid failure story can feel convincing even when it is unsupported. “Users will leave because setup is confusing” is a hypothesis to investigate, not a fact made true by inversion. Observe people attempting setup, examine where they stop and consider alternative causes.

Inversion can invite concerns that an optimistic plan overlooked. It cannot make you unbiased or ensure the most memorable risk is the most important one. **Trust the evidence and the causal connection, rather than the direction of the question.**
