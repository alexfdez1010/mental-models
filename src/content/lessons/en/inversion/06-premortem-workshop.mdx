The posters are printed. The room is booked. The volunteer who understands the registration system is arriving after everyone else. A beautiful poster will have trouble checking people in.

In the earlier premortem lesson, you learned to imagine failure before committing to a plan. Here you will carry one review through to a launch decision. The case is a public photography workshop; its timetable, costs and rehearsal results are teaching data, rather than a report of an actual event.

## Specify the result that must survive

A **premortem** asks people to explain an imagined failure of a plan. Gary Klein describes the method as beginning with the proposed plan and inviting the team to generate threats to it. The imagined outcome supplies the question; it does not supply evidence that any particular cause will occur. [Klein's description](https://www.gary-klein.com/premortem)

Think of the review as a rehearsal with the curtain deliberately stuck. You want to find the snag while you can still reach it. Here is the plan everyone receives before suggesting causes.

| Planning fact | Workshop plan |
|---|---|
| Participants | 48 registered beginners |
| Reception opens | 09:45 |
| Mandatory opening briefing | 10:00 |
| First photography exercise | 10:10 |
| Result to protect | Registered participants can reach the room, complete reception and receive the briefing before the exercise |
| Preparation remaining | 6 volunteer-hours before the event |

The prompt is: **“It is 10:10 on workshop day. Some registered participants cannot begin because they missed the briefing. What happened?”** Keep the positive result beside it. Cancelling the workshop would prevent a late start, but it would also prevent the workshop.

### When to use it

Use this level of detail when a plan has dependencies you can still change. For a casual conversation, the full timetable may be excessive. For this launch, it makes a concern testable: does the reception plan actually leave time for the briefing?

## Turn complaints into causes you can investigate

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A volunteer writes 'reception will be chaos'. What would make this concern useful?"
  options={[
    { text: "Count how many colleagues also find the sentence convincing." },
    { text: "Name the reception step that fails and the evidence that would test it.", correct: true },
    { text: "Rewrite it more confidently so the team treats it as established." }
  ]}
  explanation="A concern becomes actionable when it identifies a mechanism and a check. Agreement can help collect concerns, but it does not establish a cause."
/>

A **cause** is a condition or action that helps produce an outcome. “People miss the briefing” names the result we want to explain. “The only reception desk cannot process the queue before the briefing” proposes a mechanism. “The volunteers are hopeless” supplies a verdict about people while leaving the mechanism hidden.

The AHRQ premortem tool separates independent written concerns, a shared discussion, interventions and follow-up. We adapt that sequence here: collect concerns before debating them, then preserve the difference between a proposed cause and an observation. [AHRQ's premortem procedure](https://www.ahrq.gov/hai/tools/mvp/modules/cusp/premortem-tool.html)

| Original concern | Investigable cause | What would check it? |
|---|---|---|
| Reception will be chaos | A single desk cannot finish reception for all registered participants before the briefing | Time a rehearsal using the actual reception steps |
| Nobody will find the room | The booking email names the old room after a room change | Compare the email, signs and confirmed booking |
| The briefing will fail | The presenter has no usable copy if the projector fails | Try the planned fallback without the projector |

**Worked distinction:** You compare the email with the venue confirmation and find different room names. That mismatch is an observation. “Some participants will go to the wrong room” is still a prospective consequence: signs or a volunteer might redirect them. Correct the mismatch and check the whole route, rather than claiming you have already observed a missed briefing.

### When to use it

This translation is useful whenever a concern is broad, personal or emotionally persuasive. Do not demand a perfect explanation before allowing someone to speak. Record the concern first, then help the team turn it into a checkable statement.

## Follow a failure path to its useful intervention

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="The room name is wrong in an email. Which proposed response acts closest to that cause?"

    { text: "Improve the photography exercise once participants reach their seats." },
    { text: "Ask the presenter to deliver the same briefing with more enthusiasm." }
  ]}
  explanation="Correcting directions and testing the route address the mismatch. The other changes may affect teaching, but they leave this route to failure in place."

A **failure path** is a chain linking a condition to a missed result. Draw it before choosing a response. Otherwise, a team can spend its remaining preparation time improving something adjacent to the problem.

In our room-change path, an outdated email sends someone to the old room; the resulting detour delays reception; the participant misses the briefing and cannot begin independently. Each link is a possibility to check. A helpful steward could break the chain, so the diagram represents a proposed mechanism rather than a prediction.

A **preventive safeguard** acts before the unwanted event: send corrected directions and check that signs match. A **fallback** helps if the event still happens: place a steward at the old room and provide a catch-up briefing. Both need an owner and a check. An unsent correction and an unstaffed fallback are entries on a list.

For this event, the organiser confirms the room with the venue, sends a correction and asks a volunteer unfamiliar with the building to follow the instructions. The volunteer reaches the right room. That observation supports the route under those test conditions; it does not show that every participant will read the message.

### When to use it

Trace paths when a proposed action sounds useful but its connection to the failure is unclear. Stop at enough detail to choose and test a response. Listing every possible footstep does not improve this decision.

## Calculate what the reception plan can handle

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A reception rehearsal shows that the queue outlasts the preparation window. What should the team examine first?"
  options={[
    { text: "Whether the available desks can complete the required reception work.", correct: true },
    { text: "Whether the event poster makes the opening exercise sound exciting." },
    { text: "Whether everyone agrees that a queue would look unprofessional." }
  ]}
  explanation="The capacity calculation connects the observed task time to the deadline. Appearance and agreement do not change how long the required reception work takes."
/>

**Capacity** is how much work a process can complete in a stated period. For the first calculation, suppose all 48 participants are ready at 09:45, every reception takes 30 seconds, and desks operate independently without interruption. These are simplifying assumptions for a planning test.

| Calculation | One desk | Two desks |
|---|---|---|
| Work required | 48 × 30 = 1,440 desk-seconds | The same 1,440 desk-seconds |
| Elapsed reception time | 1,440 ÷ 1 = 1,440 seconds = 24 minutes | 1,440 ÷ 2 = 720 seconds = 12 minutes |
| Finish from 09:45 | 10:09 | 09:57 |
| Compared with briefing at 10:00 | 9 minutes late | 3 minutes spare |

The one-desk plan cannot satisfy this test even with perfect attendance at opening. Two independent desks pass this calculation. To turn the calculation into an intervention, train a second volunteer, give each desk access to the required records and rehearse the split queue. If both volunteers must ask the same person to approve every entry, they are not independent desks.

The spare three minutes are a **buffer**: unused time available to absorb some variation. They are not a guarantee. Late arrivals, difficult bookings or a printer interruption can consume that time. A rehearsal with varied reception cases checks more than timing an easy case once.

The organiser has six volunteer-hours left and these indivisible preparation options:

| Option | Time required | Evidence and contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Train and rehearse the second reception desk | 3 hours | Addresses the demonstrated timing shortfall |
| Confirm, correct and test the arrival route | 2 hours | Addresses the observed room-name mismatch |
| Prepare and test the briefing fallback | 1 hour | Provides an alternative if the projector fails or someone misses the main briefing |
| Refine the advanced closing slides | 2 hours | Improves an optional segment; no failure of the opening exercise has been linked to it |

For the stated objective and evidence, choose reception plus directions: **3 + 2 = 5 hours**, leaving **1 hour** for the briefing fallback. The three responses use **3 + 2 + 1 = 6 hours**. Adding slide polish would bring the total to **8 hours**, exceeding the budget by **2 hours**. Choosing these responses is justified by the failures they address, not simply by fitting the budget. If a required access arrangement were missing, this shortlist would need revision before deciding.

### When to use it

Calculate capacity when a failure path includes a queue, deadline or scarce resource. Keep units visible and check the assumptions that make parallel work possible. Numbers are most useful here when someone can reproduce them and challenge their inputs.

## Decide what would make you proceed or revise

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="The team has named both safeguards. What evidence is still needed before treating them as ready?"
  options={[
    { text: "A written owner for each action, whether or not the action has been tried." },
    { text: "Completed checks against the conditions used to approve the launch.", correct: true },
    { text: "A longer list of failure stories, whether or not the plan changes." }
  ]}
  explanation="Ownership enables follow-through, but an assignment is not evidence that the safeguard works. A launch decision needs the results of the agreed checks."
/>

A **decision condition** states what must be true to proceed under the current plan. A **review trigger** states which later observation will reopen the decision. A **residual risk** is a relevant risk that remains after the chosen response.

The organiser assigns the remaining hour to preparing and testing a short briefing that works without the projector and can also serve late arrivals. If that work needs longer, the preparation allocation must change; the time budget does not expand because the action sounds sensible.

These are the organiser's conditions for this case, not universal thresholds for public events:

| Item | Owner and check | Decision or later response |
|---|---|---|
| Reception ready | Reception lead rehearses both desks with the required booking records before the event | Proceed with this reception plan only if it finishes before the briefing and the records are correct |
| Directions consistent | Venue contact confirms the room; another volunteer follows the corrected route | Correct and repeat the route check if the room or instructions change |
| Briefing fallback ready | Presenter tests the short briefing without the projector and names someone who can deliver it | Revise the plan if the fallback cannot explain the opening task within the preparation available |
| Some people may arrive late | Reception directs them to the named person for the tested catch-up briefing | Use it for late arrivals; do not treat correct directions as a cure for all lateness |
| Reception workload changes | Organiser watches registrations and any change to reception steps | Recalculate and rehearse again if either invalidates the tested workload |

Suppose the rehearsal finishes before the briefing but the second desk uses an incomplete list. The timing result passes; the record check fails. The response is to fix the records and repeat the relevant check, rather than averaging a pass and a failure into a reassuring score.

### When to use it

Use decision conditions before the commitment and triggers while the plan is running. A premortem has reached a useful stopping point when important hypotheses have a response, checks have results, and someone can explain what remains uncertain. It does not establish that participants will enjoy the photography exercise; that needs evidence about the teaching itself.
