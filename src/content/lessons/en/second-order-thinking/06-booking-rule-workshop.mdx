A library's study rooms are fully booked and often empty. The manager proposes
a rule: confirm your reservation online by the previous afternoon or it becomes
available to someone else. The booking calendar will certainly change. Whether
more people get useful study time is a different question.

Here you will take a plausible consequence tree and turn it into a testable
pilot: a limited trial used to learn before extending a change. The goal is to
find which reactions matter enough to alter the decision, then collect evidence
about them.

## Trace the helpful route and the competing route

A theatre can have empty seats and a sold-out sign at the same time. Changing
the sign does not seat an audience. Likewise, a **causal pathway** is the sequence
of links by which a rule is expected to produce its outcome. A **theory of
change** records those links and the assumptions they depend on. These tools
come from programme evaluation; the
[Magenta Book's Test and Learn guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book/test-and-learn-html)
describes both.

The library's helpful route is: confirmation requirement → unconfirmed hours
released → other readers book them → those readers attend. A competing route
is: confirmation requirement → some intended users miss the message → their
reservations disappear → they lose study time. A reaction can start immediately;
it is the causal link, not a delay on the clock, that places it downstream.
The released reservation is a direct output of the rule; its effect on study
time is mediated by readers rebooking and attending.

The **system boundary** determines which effects you include. Counting only the
calendar includes released hours but excludes the reader whose booking vanished
and the staff member handling the complaint. The
[Government Office for Science toolkit](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/systems-thinking-for-civil-servants/toolkit)
recommends considering both boundaries and the perspectives of people in the
system.

### When to use it

Draw competing routes when a proposal's headline result is one step short of
the outcome you want. Include a plausible beneficial response and a plausible
costly response. Listing only disasters is as selective as listing only gains.

## Count the gain after accounting for displaced use

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Replacement readers use some released hours. Why is that total insufficient to calculate the rule's net benefit?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Some original readers may lose hours they would have used', correct: true },
    { text: 'Every replacement reservation should count as a guaranteed gain' },
    { text: 'Cancelled reservations can never have belonged to intended users' },
  ]}
  explanation="New use must be weighed against use displaced by the rule. Non-confirmation does not tell you that the original reader would have stayed away."
/>

Think of moving water between buckets: the amount poured into the new bucket
does not tell you how much water the system gained. **Net additional use** is
use created by the change minus use that the change removes. The following
planning scenario supplies both quantities so that you can check the arithmetic.
In a real pilot, the no-rule alternative would need to be estimated.

The library offers 100 one-hour room slots per week. Before the rule, all are
reserved, 60 are used, and 40 are not. Suppose the confirmation rule produces
these responses. Count occupied slots at this library during that week, and
assume attendance in the other bookings is unchanged. This counts room use,
rather than numbers of readers or staff working time.

| Planning assumption | Hours per week |
|---|---|
| Original reservations cancelled for non-confirmation | 20 |
| Of those cancelled, hours the original readers would have used | 8 |
| Of those cancelled, hours that would have gone unused | 12 |
| Cancelled hours booked by replacement readers | 15 |
| Of those replacement bookings, hours actually used | 12 |
| Of those replacement bookings, hours not used | 3 |
| Released hours with no replacement booking | 5 |

The unchanged original bookings contribute $60 - 8 = 52$ used hours. Replacement
readers contribute 12, so total use becomes $52 + 12 = 64$ hours. The net gain is
$64 - 60 = 4$ hours, equivalently $12 - 8 = 4$. Releasing twenty hours did not
create twenty hours of study.

The capacity also balances: 64 hours are used; 28 unchanged bookings still go
unused; 3 replacement bookings go unused; and 5 released slots remain unbooked.
Together, $64 + 28 + 3 + 5 = 100$ hours. No hour has been counted twice.

For this narrow outcome, the rule helps if replacement attendance exceeds
displaced original attendance. In symbols, $\Delta U = R - L$, where $R$ is
replacement use and $L$ is lost original use, in the same units and period. With
eight lost hours, eight replacement hours break even; fewer reverse the gain.
This is a conditional accounting rule, not a prediction that either response
will happen.

### When to use it

Use this calculation when someone reports gross gains: new attendees, new sales,
faster completions, or recovered capacity. Check what existing activity was
displaced. Even a positive total does not settle who gains, who loses, or whether
the staff effort is worth it.

## Test the explanation as well as the total

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Room use rises after the pilot starts, during the approach to exam season. What has that observation established by itself?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Use changed; the rule may not explain all of that change', correct: true },
    { text: 'The rule caused the entire increase in occupied room time' },
    { text: 'Exam season makes any comparison or measurement pointless' },
  ]}
  explanation="Monitoring establishes what changed. To estimate the rule's effect, investigate what would have happened without it and other changes occurring at the same time."
/>

A plant growing after you water it does not show how much of the growth came
from the water rather than the arriving spring. A **counterfactual** is the
outcome that would have happened without the intervention. We cannot observe
both versions of the same room-week, so an evaluation needs a credible way to
estimate that alternative. The
[Magenta Book's impact-evaluation guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book/quality-in-policy-impact-evaluation-qpie-html)
distinguishes observed change from change attributable to a policy.

The planning assumptions above are not pilot findings. In this separate pilot
example, the earlier assumption of unchanged use in other bookings is no longer
given. Suppose the manager observes these totals:

| Location | Used hours before | Used hours during the pilot | Change in hours |
|---|---|---|---|
| Library testing the rule | 60 | 64 | +4 |
| Comparison library keeping its old rule | 50 | 56 | +6 |

If, for this illustration, both libraries would otherwise have gained the same
six hours, the pilot library's no-rule outcome would be $60 + 6 = 66$ hours.
The comparison-adjusted estimate is then $64 - 66 = -2$ hours, or
$(64 - 60) - (56 - 50) = -2$. The observed increase of four hours can coexist
with an estimated negative effect of the rule.

That conclusion depends on the comparison assumption. Different opening hours,
exam timetables, building work, or readers switching between libraries can make
the comparator misleading. The comparison needs consistent measurement, a site
unaffected by the pilot and no other change that would give the libraries
different no-rule trends. One before-and-after pair at each library cannot
demonstrate those conditions, and this tiny example does not establish a
reliable effect size. A stronger design might allocate comparable locations
to a pilot or comparison condition, with enough locations and time to assess
variation.

Totals also cannot reveal which branch of the tree occurred. Pair them with
evidence about the mechanism:

| Question | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|
| Does a release arrive in time to be useful? | Release, rebooking, and arrival timestamps |
| Do replacement bookings become attendance? | Booked hours linked to occupied hours |
| Are intended users losing access? | Cancellation reasons, help requests, and follow-up with affected readers |
| Are gains concentrated among fast online bookers? | Use and failed bookings by booking channel and relevant user circumstances |
| Is another change driving demand? | Opening hours, local exam dates, and comparable-site trends |

Readers who cannot reliably confirm online may react differently from readers
who check messages all day. Ask those readers and the staff supporting them;
absence from the booking log does not show that they no longer need a room.

### When to use it

Use monitoring to find operational failures promptly. Use an appropriate
evaluation design when deciding whether the rule caused enough benefit to
extend it. A weak comparison cannot be repaired by drawing a more elaborate
consequence tree.

## Revise the rule at the link that fails

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Readers report that online-only confirmation cancels bookings they intended to use. Which revision addresses that particular failure?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Offer a workable assisted confirmation route and test its costs', correct: true },
    { text: 'Send more identical online messages and count them as success' },
    { text: 'Shorten the deadline without investigating missed confirmations' },
  ]}
  explanation="An assisted route targets the access barrier. It still needs testing: staff capacity and whether people can use the route affect whether the revision works."
/>

Repairing a bridge means finding the failing joint, not repainting the whole
structure. The same applies to a rule. If late releases are the problem, test an
earlier release or a waiting-list process. If confirmation access is the problem,
test telephone or desk assistance. If replacement bookings become repeated
no-shows, investigate those bookings rather than assuming every cancellation is
a recovered benefit.

Each repair has further consequences. Follow one more branch:

Agree revision criteria before the pilot, with the people running and using the
service. In this example, the manager records these decision rules:

| Evidence during the trial | Action |
|---|---|
| Confirmation messages fail to arrive or cancellations cannot be corrected | Suspend automatic cancellations and restore workable access |
| Intended readers repeatedly lose access because the confirmation route is unusable | Revise that route before extending the rule |
| Rebooking rises but occupied hours do not | Investigate the release-to-attendance link |
| Use improves under a credible comparison, access concerns are addressed, and staff workload is manageable | Consider a larger trial, with the same monitoring |

Observe complete booking-and-attendance cycles, not just launch-day clicks.
Repeat observation after readers have had time to adapt: some may learn to
cancel promptly; others may collect backup bookings or abandon the system.
These are possibilities to test. They do not make a reversal inevitable.

### When to use it

Stop expanding the tree when the next useful step is an observation that could
change the decision. You need not forecast every future reaction. You do need
an owner for the evidence, a review point, and a way to change course when an
important assumption fails.

### Sources and provenance

The library figures and pilot tables are constructed teaching examples, not
findings about an actual library. The planning table specifies alternative
outcomes for arithmetic; a real evaluation would need evidence to estimate them.

- [Government Office for Science — systems thinking toolkit](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/systems-thinking-for-civil-servants/toolkit) supports the use of system boundaries, stakeholder perspectives, causal mapping, and monitoring questions.
- [HM Treasury and Evaluation Task Force — Test and Learn](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book/test-and-learn-html) explains causal pathways, assumptions, small-scale testing, and adaptation before wider implementation.
- [HM Treasury and Evaluation Task Force — quality in policy impact evaluation](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book/quality-in-policy-impact-evaluation-qpie-html) distinguishes outcome monitoring from attribution and explains the assumptions required by comparison designs.
