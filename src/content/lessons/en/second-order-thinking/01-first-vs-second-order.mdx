A stone disturbs the water; a wave carries that disturbance to a floating leaf.
The picture is useful because it makes a connecting process visible. It becomes
misleading if you learn only “first means now, second means later.” A response
can happen almost immediately, and a direct change can be scheduled for next
month.

This lesson makes the distinction depend on the **causal links** you state.
You will identify the connecting change, separate causal order from intention,
and compare a direct saving with the work a response adds back.

## State the decision and the map

<MCQ client:visible pretest

  ]}
  explanation="Order describes a place in a causal map. A delay or an intention does not, by itself, add an intermediate causal link."
/>

In the stone analogy, first specify the map: stone enters water → water is
disturbed → a wave moves the leaf. The disturbance is the direct effect shown;
the leaf's movement is carried through the water. We are deliberately omitting
many physical details, so the drawing's depth is not a universal label attached
to the leaf.

A **first-order effect** is a direct effect of the chosen action in the stated
causal map. A **second-order effect** is reached through an intermediate effect
or response in that map. Following another link gives a **third-order effect**.
These practical labels organise the explanation. If you change the action or
the level of detail, you may change the numbering.

A **mediator** is an intermediate change through which an effect operates. It
answers “what carries the influence?” A useful explanation names that change,
rather than calling everything beyond the decision “the world's reaction.”
The [Government Analysis Function's theory-of-change guide](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/the-analysis-function-theory-of-change-toolkit/)
uses explicit causal links, assumptions, and evidence to explain how actions
lead to outcomes.

### When to use it

State the action before labelling an effect. “Sales fell” has no meaningful
causal order on its own: it could be the outcome you are explaining, a mediator
in a staffing decision, or an observation with several competing causes.

## Work through the café's causal chain

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A café changes its menu, the payable price falls, and customers buy additional cups because the price is lower. Which change mediates the menu's effect on purchases in this map?"
  options={[
    { text: 'The change in the price customers must pay', correct: true },
    { text: 'The number of days since the menu was printed' },
    { text: 'The owner’s wish to sell more cups of coffee' },
  ]}
  explanation="The stated chain runs through the payable price. Time and intention provide context, but neither supplies that connecting change."
/>

Consider a planning case. A café replaces its menu so that a cup costing five
currency units now costs four. The change in what a customer pays is a direct
effect of applying the new menu. Holding the number of cups fixed, receipts fall
by one unit per cup. That calculation says nothing yet about whether people buy
more cups.

Now add an explicit response: customers notice the lower price and order more.
In this map, the payable price mediates the menu's effect on purchasing. The
response is **intended** by the owner and could occur during the **same visit**.
It is still mediated.

For the competitor-loss branch, suppose some customers switch from the
neighbouring café to this one. The neighbour notices the lost custom and changes
its menu. That adds a further link; extra purchases alone would not establish
customer losses next door. If instead the neighbour reacts directly to seeing
the new posted price, draw a shorter competing branch. Calling every
competitor response “third-order” without naming its cause would hide the
relationship you need to investigate.

### When to use it

Draw the link that would distinguish competing explanations. To investigate the
competitor case, look at when it changed its menu and what prompted the change.
Timing can help test a proposed mechanism; timing alone does not establish one.

## Separate order from timing, intention, and value

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A team deliberately makes instructions clearer so readers understand them and ask fewer questions immediately. Can that be a beneficial mediated effect?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Yes; improved understanding carries the effect to question-asking', correct: true },
    { text: 'No; an intended effect must always be direct rather than mediated' },
    { text: 'No; a mediated effect must be delayed and harmful to the team' },
  ]}
  explanation="Understanding is the intermediate change in the proposed chain. Being fast, intended, and useful does not remove that link."
/>

A postal route tells you which sorting office a parcel passes through. It does
not tell you whether the parcel is welcome or how long it waits. Likewise,
causal order, timing, intention, and value answer different questions:

| Question | What it describes | Counterexample to the shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Through which links does it occur? | Causal structure | A direct price change can begin next week |
| When does it occur? | Timing | A purchasing response can happen in the same visit |
| Did someone want it? | Intention | Clear instructions can deliberately improve understanding and reduce questions |
| Does it help the chosen objective? | Value | A mediated response can reinforce, weaken, or reverse a benefit |

A direct effect can also be unwanted: replacing the café's menu could remove a
decaf option by mistake. It remains a direct consequence of that replacement in
this map, even though nobody wanted it. Conversely, a delayed benefit can be
intended. Neither intention nor delay assigns a causal order.

### When to use it

Keep these questions separate when discussing a policy's “unintended
consequences.” The phrase asks about intention, not necessarily about causal
depth. Also name whose objective you are evaluating: a lower price can help a
customer and reduce receipts per cup for the café.

## Calculate a sign-flip in consistent units

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="An automatic reply saves typing time but makes some customers send follow-up questions. What is needed to decide whether it saves team work overall?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Compare saved typing work with the additional follow-up work', correct: true },
    { text: 'Count the replies sent without considering the customer response' },
    { text: 'Treat follow-up work as irrelevant because it was not intended' },
  ]}
  explanation="Both the direct saving and the mediated cost belong in a comparison of team work. The sign depends on their relative sizes."
/>

Here is a second planning case, with a complete numerical comparison. A support
team replaces individually written replies with a standard template. It handles
forty requests per day and saves one and a half minutes of typing per request.
The direct saving is $40 \times 1.5 = 60$ minutes of team labour per day.

Suppose the template leaves the next step unclear for ten customers. Each sends
a follow-up that takes three minutes to read and triage plus five to resolve,
eight minutes altogether. **Customer confusion is the
mediator** between the template and the extra work. The response could happen
within minutes, even though it was not the intended result.

Assume these ten daily follow-ups would not occur with the individually written
replies, and all other work stays the same.

| Change in work | Minutes of team labour per day |
|---|---|
| Typing work removed | −60 |
| Added reading and triage | $10 \times 3 = 30$ |
| Added resolution work | $10 \times 5 = 50$ |
| Net change | $-60 + 30 + 50 = 20$ |

Total work increases by twenty minutes. That is a **sign-flip** for the objective
of reducing team labour: a direct saving becomes a net cost under the stated
response. We have compared the same outcome, units, and period, rather than
subtracting customer satisfaction from staff minutes.

If only five customers needed those follow-ups, the added work would be
$5 \times 8 = 40$ minutes, leaving $60 - 40 = 20$ minutes saved. A downstream
cost would weaken the benefit without reversing it. A response can also help:
if clearer instructions reduce follow-ups, the mediated saving reinforces the
direct one. The direction must be checked, not assumed.

### When to use it

Look for sign-flips when a proposal is justified by a local saving or improvement.
Keep the response size conditional until you have evidence. In this case,
seven follow-ups cost $7 \times 8 = 56$ minutes and preserve a small saving;
eight cost $8 \times 8 = 64$ and reverse it.

<MCQ client:visible
  question="What does the smaller follow-up scenario demonstrate?"
  options={[
    { text: 'A mediated cost can reduce a saving without reversing its sign', correct: true },
    { text: 'Any mediated cost makes the whole intervention a failed policy' },
    { text: 'An intended saving means the additional work should be ignored' },
  ]}
  explanation="The same mechanism can produce a smaller benefit or a net cost at different response sizes. A sign-flip is an outcome of the comparison, not the definition of second-order thinking."
/>

## Read Howard Marks's second-level thinking carefully

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="An investor calls a company excellent. In Howard Marks's account, what question does that statement leave open?"
  options={[
    { text: 'What expectations the current price reflects, and what could differ', correct: true },
    { text: 'Whether excellence guarantees a good return at any possible price' },
    { text: 'Whether disagreeing with other investors guarantees an advantage' },
  ]}
  explanation="Quality alone does not settle the judgment. Expectations and price also matter, and a different view can be mistaken."
/>

In [I Beg to Differ](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memo/i-beg-to-differ)
and [It's Not Easy](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2015-09-09-its-not-easy.pdf),
Howard Marks describes **second-level thinking** as considering expectations,
price, and competing outcomes beyond a superficial opinion.

Compare “the company has a strong product” with “what performance does the price
assume, and what evidence challenges that expectation?” The latter question
does not establish a buy or sell conclusion. An excellent company is not
automatically overpriced. Marks's broader “level” of reasoning also differs
from a numbered depth in our causal tree.

### When to use it

Use it when a familiar fact is being treated as a complete judgment. Keep
uncertain expectations visible.

## Combine the method with inversion and a stopping rule

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A reversible change could fail because users misunderstand the instructions. What is a useful next step after naming that mechanism?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Test the instructions and observe whether misunderstanding creates extra work', correct: true },
    { text: 'Add more hypothetical branches until every remote consequence is listed' },
    { text: 'Declare the change harmful because one possible failure has been imagined' },
  ]}
  explanation="A proposed mechanism should guide a proportionate test. More imagined depth does not establish its likelihood or cost."
/>

**Inversion** asks how a plan could fail. Second-order thinking helps supply one
possible answer: an intermediate change causes a response the plan did not count.
In the support case, the mechanism is confusion leading to follow-up work. A
revision should target that link, for example by clarifying the next step, then
checking whether the additional work actually falls.

Also ask how a response could help. Otherwise, inversion can turn into a list of
reasons to reject every proposal. If clearer replies both save typing and reduce
follow-ups, that favourable mediated effect belongs in the comparison too.

A practical stopping rule is to trace far enough to identify consequences or
uncertainties that could change the decision. Then investigate the important
links. The [Government Office for Science toolkit](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/systems-thinking-for-civil-servants/toolkit)
recommends checking whether extra detail adds to the understanding needed for
the chosen purpose, and monitoring how an intervention performs.

### When to use it

Give a consequential or hard-to-reverse choice more scrutiny than a minor choice
that can be corrected cheaply. For either, distinguish a planning hypothesis
from an observed causal effect. A diagram cannot rule out a workload change,
a staffing change, or another explanation for the measured outcome.
