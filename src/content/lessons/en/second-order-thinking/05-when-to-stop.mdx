A choir coordinator wants shorter rehearsal notes. Editing would take less time, but singers might need more explanations. A colleague adds another chain: more questions → frustration → people stop volunteering. The map is getting longer. Which link deserves another hour of work before choosing a format?

The earlier lessons taught you to follow the response to an intervention. This lesson adds a stopping discipline: identify what could change the action, its safeguards or the decision to wait; then ask what evidence could resolve it at an acceptable cost. A low probability, an opposing effect and an undo button each need interpretation before they can justify stopping.

Consequences can branch, merge and feed back. In a toy tree where every node has three different children, four levels contain **3 + 9 + 27 + 81 = 120 nodes** below the root. That illustrates a cost of enumeration; it is not a claim that every real system has this structure or that every finite forecast is impossible. Merging repeated mechanisms can also make a map smaller.

Before expanding a branch, name the **decision**, the **outcome**, the people affected and the period being compared. In the choir case, saving the coordinator's time is not the same outcome as saving all volunteers' time. A shorter document could shift work from one person to several others.

Separate a link you have evidence for from a link you are proposing. Records of clarification work, the reasons people ask questions and a credible comparison with detailed notes would help test the proposed mechanism. More questions after a format change alone would not establish its cause: rehearsal difficulty, membership and other changes may differ too. An observed queue or an elegant arrow is a reason to investigate, not proof of the connecting mechanism.

### When to use selective tracing

Use it when a map grows faster than the time available to make the decision. Prioritise consequential assumptions with weak evidence, including an affected group the current map excludes. The [Magenta Book's Test and Learn guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book/test-and-learn-html) recommends targeting critical assumptions, proportionate methods and unintended effects rather than testing every assumption equally.

## Calculate the event you actually mean

<MCQ

  explanation="A probability calculation concerns specified events and their relationships. General confidence labels and the number of arrows do not supply that information."

A **conditional probability** asks how likely an event is given something already specified. For one required route, the chain rule is:

**P(A and B and C) = P(A) × P(B given A) × P(C given A and B)**

Each conditioning event must have positive probability. If a required event is impossible, the whole route is impossible. [Chris Piech's Stanford probability reader](https://chrispiech.github.io/probabilityForComputerScientists/en/part1/prob_and/) derives this rule. Multiplying the separate, unconditional probabilities instead needs a justification such as mutual independence; the [reader's independence chapter](https://chrispiech.github.io/probabilityForComputerScientists/en/part1/independence/) explains that distinction.

For a frequency illustration, stipulate **100 hypothetical choir members** and this route: a message reaches them → they read it → they follow its instruction. These are invented counts for the arithmetic, not observations about a real choir.

| Required event | Count in this illustration | Conditional fraction |
|---|---|---|
| A: the message reaches the member | 80 of 100 | P(A) = 0.80 |
| B: it is read, among those reached | 60 of the 80 | P(B given A) = 0.75 |
| C: its instruction is followed, among those reached and reading | 54 of the 60 | P(C given A and B) = 0.90 |

Select a member uniformly from this imagined group. The probability of the **entire specified route** is **0.80 × 0.75 × 0.90 = 0.54**, or **54%**. Its complement, **at least one required step not occurring**, is **1 − 0.54 = 46%**. This does not mean there is a 46% chance of every step failing, of harm occurring or of the whole causal model being false.

Now allow a second route: some members get an explanation at the desk. Stipulate that **30** complete the instruction through that route, including **18** who also completed the message route. Completion through **at least one** route is **54 + 30 − 18 = 66 members**, or **66%**. Adding 54 and 30 without subtracting the overlap would count those 18 people twice. This is the [inclusion–exclusion rule](https://chrispiech.github.io/probabilityForComputerScientists/en/part1/prob_or/) applied to the two routes.

### Depth does not set a confidence cutoff

If five required events each had a **conditional** probability of 80% given all earlier events, their joint probability would be **0.80⁵ = 32.768%**. With 99% for each conditional probability, it would be **0.99⁵ ≈ 95.10%**. Neither calculation supplies the missing evidence for those probabilities. A general claim to be “80% confident in each explanation” does not establish the same model. **False precision** appears when unsupported inputs are presented as measured accuracy.

The first number is also not a reason to discard the route as noise. A possible loss below 50% probability may still matter greatly; a likely but tiny effect may not change the choice. The question is what event was calculated, what evidence supports it, and what its consequences are. There is no universal fifty-percent stopping rule.

Return to the format decision. For a separate **hypothetical one-rehearsal budget**, abbreviated notes save **40 staff-minutes of editing**. They may create **20** or **80 additional staff-minutes of clarification**. All figures concern total staff work compared with keeping detailed notes; they are not elapsed waiting time. Quality and access must remain acceptable. The route counts above do not establish these work figures or their probabilities.

| Clarification case | Editing time saved | Additional clarification | Net staff-minutes saved |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low extra work | 40 | 20 | 40 − 20 = +20 |
| High extra work | 40 | 80 | 40 − 80 = −40 |

In the low-work case, the opposing response **partly offsets** the saving. In the high-work case it **reverses the net sign**. Exactly 40 extra minutes would cancel the benefit, giving zero rather than a reversal.

Finding the high-work possibility is a reason to examine its likelihood, cost and possible safeguards. It is not an instruction to stop after the first sign reversal, nor proof that all short notes are a bad idea. A clearer summary, a different distribution method or retaining particular details would be new options to test. Any new option must enter the comparison explicitly.

If clarification can credibly be bounded between **20 and 30 minutes**, the editing saving remains **10 to 20 minutes** before other costs. A further branch matters if it could consume that margin, affect access or change a safeguard. A few speculative arrows do not replace checking that bound.

## Let the next check earn its cost

<MCQ

A **decision policy** specifies what action to choose for each possible result. For this calculation, restrict the choices to **use the unmodified short notes or keep detailed notes for the next rehearsal**. Suppose low clarification work has probability **75%** and high clarification work **25%**. These are assumptions of the planning model. An **expected value** is the probability-weighted average of its possible outcomes, not a guarantee for this rehearsal.

Without more information, short notes have expected savings **0.75 × 20 + 0.25 × (−40) = 5 minutes**. Detailed notes give zero, so the best current action saves an expected **5**. The possible high-work case matters despite being less likely than half.

Imagine a targeted review that reports favourable for **80% of low-work cases** and unfavourable for **80% of high-work cases**. These performance assumptions need validation before real use. The following expected frequencies explain its value without requiring you to assume that a favourable report is infallible:

| Review result | Low-work cases out of a hypothetical 100 | High-work cases | Best action after this result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Favourable | 75 × 0.80 = 60 | 25 × 0.20 = 5 | Short notes: 60 × 20 − 5 × 40 = +1000 across this branch |
| Unfavourable | 75 × 0.20 = 15 | 25 × 0.80 = 20 | Detailed notes: zero, rather than 15 × 20 − 20 × 40 = −500 |

Across the hypothetical hundred cases, the result-guided policy therefore saves **1000 ÷ 100 = 10 minutes on average**, before review effort. Its **additional expected decision value** is **10 − 5 = 5 minutes**. A review requiring **3 staff-minutes** has net improvement **5 − 3 = 2**, and complete expected net savings **10 − 3 = 7**. If the same review required **6 minutes**, its net improvement would be **−1**: that check would lose to deciding without it.

Even a perfect report could only save **0.75 × 20 = 15 minutes** on average. Its improvement over the current best action is **15 − 5 = 10**. Thus a twelve-minute check cannot earn its cost for this narrowly defined decision, even if perfect. This bound changes with a different decision horizon, repeated future uses or another objective.

The same logic applies to more tracing: ask what an additional calculation, observation or comparison could change. **Analysis paralysis** is the failure mode where further tracing substitutes for deciding without a credible prospect of improving the decision. Count costs from **now onward**; work already spent on the map does not make the next hour worthwhile. Include delay when it changes what can be done. A review arriving after the notes must be distributed cannot guide that distribution, although it may inform a later rehearsal. Do not deduct the same lost work once in adjusted payoffs and again as a separate delay cost.

### A proportionate stopping decision

Stop the current investigation when required harm checks are complete and the next available analysis cannot usefully change the choice or safeguards, or when its expected additional benefit does not justify its full cost. This is a judgement about a specified next step, not proof that all future research is worthless. A stronger study, a bundle of evidence or new harm information can justify reopening the decision.

## Check what reversing the decision would actually undo

Jeff Bezos's [2015 shareholder letter](https://ir.aboutamazon.com/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF) distinguishes consequential, difficult-to-reverse decisions from reversible ones and argues for lighter processes for the latter. The useful question for this lesson is how much can actually be recovered. The ability to change a setting is one part of that assessment.

For a **separate live-trial budget**, keep the forty-minute editing saving and suppose the high clarification case occurs. Setup uses **6 staff-minutes**, monitoring **4**, restarting the old approval workflow **8**, and replacing copies already distributed **12**. These tasks are separate from the **80** clarification minutes. The budget covers the trial rehearsal and its cleanup; these are assumptions to check, not typical trial costs.

| Trial work compared with keeping detailed notes | Added staff-minutes |
|---|---|
| Clarification | 80 |
| Setup | 6 |
| Monitoring | 4 |
| Restart the old approval workflow | 8 |
| Replace distributed copies | 12 |
| Total added work | 110 |
| Less editing work saved | −40 |
| Net additional work | 70 |

Returning to the old file does not erase the **70 additional staff-minutes**. It may also leave habits, obsolete copies or missed participation outside this numerical budget. Check those effects before assuming the harm is bounded. A trial that teaches you something after the rehearsal cannot improve an already completed rehearsal; any benefit to later decisions needs its own horizon and comparison.

Before a bounded trial, name an owner, the groups exposed, the work and access outcomes to monitor, a comparison and a review date. Agree what evidence would trigger stopping or modification, and how existing copies and outstanding questions will be handled. The [Test and Learn guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book/test-and-learn-html) recommends setting decision points and criteria before interpreting the findings. A deadline can make targeted checks urgent; it does not make known downstream harm irrelevant.
