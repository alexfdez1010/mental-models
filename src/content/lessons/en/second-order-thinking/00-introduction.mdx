In **1935**, cane toads were introduced to Queensland to control agricultural
pests. They proved ineffective at that job, spread, and became a pest themselves.
Their toxins can kill native predators that try to eat them. The intended
benefit was a hope, not a successful first step later undone. These details are
recorded by the [Queensland Government](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/farms-fishing-forestry/agriculture/biosecurity/animals/invasive/other/cane-toad)
and the [Australian Government](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/biosecurity-trade/invasive-species/feral-animals-australia/cane-toads).

The useful question travels well beyond ecology: after an intervention, what
changes, and what happens **because of those changes**? That is the starting
point of **second-order thinking**.

## Count causal links, not hours

  explanation="The link matters: reward changes what people think will pay, which changes sharing. A response can be immediate and intended while still operating through an intermediate step."
/>

Think of a row of dominoes. Naming the tile you push is not enough; you need to
see which tile carries the push onward. **First-order thinking** considers the
direct effects of a decision in the causal map you are using. **Second-order
thinking** follows an effect through an intermediate change and asks what it
produces next: “and then what?” Further links create further orders.

An **intermediate change**, or **mediator**, is something through which an effect
operates. For example, a rule changes who gets credit; that changes the incentive
to share notes; the change in sharing affects handovers. Naming the mediator
makes the explanation more useful than saying “there might be consequences.”

The labels depend on the level of detail in your map. They are a thinking aid,
not a universal numbering system for every physical event. Always state the
links you mean.

| Tempting shortcut | Better distinction |
|---|---|
| Direct means immediate | A direct change may start next month because the decision is scheduled for then |
| Downstream means unintended | A manager can deliberately use recognition to encourage later cooperation |
| Downstream means harmful | A response can reinforce a benefit, weaken it, or reverse it |

### When to use it

Use this question when people, incentives, or connected processes can respond to
a decision. Ask “through what change?” rather than assigning an order from the
calendar or from whether you like the result.

## Work through a decision before trusting the saving

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A team removes a handover check to save work. Which observation would most directly test a plausible downstream cost?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Extra handling caused by unclear ownership after the check disappears', correct: true },
    { text: 'The check minutes removed, with all other work assumed unchanged' },
    { text: 'The manager’s stated intention to make the process more efficient' },
  ]}
  explanation="The direct saving is only part of the comparison. If removing the check changes ownership clarity, extra handling is one possible consequence to investigate."
/>

For a worked planning case, consider a team processing twenty requests per day.
Each request has a two-minute handover check that confirms who owns it. The
manager removes that check. Holding the other work fixed, the direct saving is
$20 \times 2 = 40$ minutes of team work per day.

Now specify the response rather than merely predicting “something bad.” Without
the check, some requests have unclear ownership. In this case, four requests are
handled twice, adding ten minutes each, and two other requests require six
minutes of clarification each. These are separate pieces of extra work.

| Work changed by the decision | Minutes of team work per day |
|---|---|
| Removed handover checks | −40 |
| Duplicate handling | $4 \times 10 = 40$ |
| Additional clarification | $2 \times 6 = 12$ |
| Net change | $-40 + 40 + 12 = 12$ |

The team now does twelve **more** minutes of work per day. The **sign-flip** is a
reversal of the initial benefit once the downstream costs are included. It occurs
under these case assumptions; removing a check does not always cause it.

### When to use it

Use the same units, period, and affected group on both sides. Here we counted
team labour minutes, not elapsed waiting time. Pause tracing further links to
compare gains and costs that could change the choice.

## Treat a plausible chain as a hypothesis

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A real team sees more duplicate work after removing a check. Does the sequence establish that unclear ownership caused the increase?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; inspect the mechanism and competing explanations', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; happening afterward establishes the causal link' },
    { text: 'No; downstream effects can never be investigated' },
  ]}
  explanation="The timing is compatible with the proposed chain, but workload, staffing, or measurement may also have changed. A mechanism needs evidence beyond its place in a convincing story."
/>

The numerical case supplies its causal assumptions so that you can work through
them. In a real team, you would check request records, ask what caused repeated
handling, and compare against a credible estimate of what would have happened
with the check retained. A change in request complexity could produce extra work
even without the policy change.

The [Government Analysis Function's theory-of-change guide](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/the-analysis-function-theory-of-change-toolkit/)
separates causal links from assumptions and evidence. The practical distinction
is simple: a tree helps you decide **what to investigate**; drawing it does not
establish which branch actually occurred.

For a transfer case, suppose a manager creates an individual award. Coworkers
interpret it as rewarding solo credit, so they withhold useful handover notes.
The interpretation of the reward is the mediator in this proposed chain. The
withholding is downstream even if it starts today. It is a hypothesis to check,
not an inevitable effect of individual awards.

### When to use it

Reach for second-order thinking before acting on a policy, incentive, or process
change with meaningful consequences. For a cheap, easily corrected choice, a
short check may be enough. More branches are useful only when they reveal a
mechanism or uncertainty that could alter your decision.

## Use the course to practise the method

The route develops the distinction, then gives it more demanding applications:

1. **First-Order vs. Second-Order** — distinguish direct effects from responses carried through further links.
2. **The And-Then-What Chain** — build and explore a branching consequence tree.
3. **Incentives, Feedback and the Cobra Effect** — investigate why people and systems respond to interventions.
4. **Five Cases, Worked in Full** — compare mechanisms across policy, organisations, and shared resources.
5. **When to Stop** — match the depth of analysis to the decision and the evidence available.
6. **Pilot a Rule Before Scaling It** — turn a tree into a test, count displaced use, and investigate alternative explanations.

Answer each exercise before opening its explanation, then compare your causal
links with the ones supplied. The final exam locks each submitted answer during
the run; its score appears at the end.
