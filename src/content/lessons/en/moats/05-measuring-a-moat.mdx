We've defined moats, catalogued the five kinds, separated them from mirages, and watched
them erode. One skill remains, and it's the one that turns all the theory into a usable
tool: **measuring** a moat. Stories are easy to tell and easy to fool yourself with —
every company's annual report claims a durable advantage. So practitioners look past the
story to *evidence*: signs in the numbers and in customer behavior that a moat is really
there. Two useful indicators are **pricing power** and the **persistence of high
returns**. Read them alongside customer alternatives, costs and the history of entry;
neither indicator identifies a moat by itself.

## Investigate pricing power

Start with a question about customer behavior, stated for a particular price change and window:

<Callout variant="tip" title="The pricing-power test">
**Can the business raise its prices meaningfully without losing customers to rivals?** Record the size and timing of the increase, who stayed and what alternatives were available. Retention can support a hypothesis about switching costs, a network or brand preference. It can also reflect temporary shortages or other changes. Lack of a price premium does not rule out an advantage based on lower costs.
</Callout>

A before-and-after observation describes what happened. To explain why, investigate competing accounts: did alternatives become unavailable, did service quality change, or did customer incomes or needs shift? Customer retention alone does not isolate the cause of a price response.

A constructed example: 100 customers face a price rise and 98 remain for the next round. That is 98 retained out of 100 in this window. Without evidence about alternatives and other changes, it is not a measurement of permanent protection. A low-cost seller may instead match competitors' prices and earn more on each sale because its costs are lower. Price and cost evidence answer different questions.

## The financial fingerprint: persistent high returns on capital

A history of return on capital above its cost is relevant evidence about performance across the observed periods. Persistence can make a one-round accident less convincing, but no fixed number of years proves a moat. A long demand upswing, risk, selection of surviving firms and accounting choices can also affect the record.

Ask what happened when conditions changed and rivals attempted entry. Was the proposed obstacle actually present? Would a different explanation predict the same history? The record supports an investigation; it does not by itself measure the cost or time a new rival would face.

## The traps in the numbers

The numbers can lie, and a careful analyst knows the three ways:

**Trap 1 — One good year looks like a moat.** A cyclical upswing, a temporary shortage, or a
one-off hit can produce gorgeous returns that have nothing to do with durability. Persistence across years—including different demand conditions—helps test the good-season
explanation, but does not uniquely identify a moat. Always ask: *has this survived a bad year and rivals' best efforts?*

**Trap 2 — Returns that are high because risk is high.** Sometimes a business earns big returns
simply because it's making a risky bet that's currently paying off, not because anything protects
it. That's not a moat; it's a coin that's landed heads a few times. Check the risk exposure and the conditions behind the observations; stability alone does
not prove a moat, and volatility alone does not disprove one.

**Trap 3 — Accounting that flatters.** Reported profit can be inflated by under-investment (you
look profitable because you're starving the business, including the moat itself), by leaving big
costs off the page, or by booking gains that won't recur. A moat shows up in *durable, cash-real*
returns, not in a flattering single line.

<details>
<summary>A company has earned high returns on capital for ten straight years. Is that conclusive proof of a moat?</summary>
It is evidence about that record, not conclusive identification of a moat. Its strength
depends on the alternatives and the conditions observed. But run
the checks before you conclude: Were those years one long cyclical tailwind that's about to turn
(Trap 1)? What risk exposure and conditions produced these returns (Trap 2)? Are they
cash-real, or flattered by under-investment and accounting (Trap 3)? And critically — *what is the
moat?* If you can name the structure (network, switching costs, scale, intangible, efficient
scale) and test how it affects feasible entry, the track record can support that account. If
you *can't* name the structure, the high returns might be luck wearing a moat's costume. A named mechanism remains a hypothesis to test against alternatives and new evidence.
</details>

## Putting the toolkit together

You now hold the complete moat toolkit. Faced with any business, you can run the full
diagnosis:

| Step | The question | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Find the excess** | Does it earn returns above its cost of capital? | Is there treasure worth protecting? |
| **2. Investigate price response** | Who stayed after a defined increase, and what alternatives existed? | Evidence about retention in that window |
| **3. Check persistence** | Which demand and entry conditions did the history include? | Which explanations remain compatible with it |
| **4. Test a mechanism** | What cost, delay or access constraint does a rival face? | A conditional account to compare with alternatives |
| **5. Test for mirages** | Does a label substitute for evidence about entry? | Where the inference is unsupported |
| **6. Watch the water** | What observation would weaken the proposed mechanism? | A reason to revise the hypothesis |

Run those six and you can present an accountable claim: here is the observed return, here is the proposed obstacle, here are its conditions and alternatives, and here is what would change the conclusion. A persuasive name for the obstacle is not a substitute for that evidence.

## Recap

1. **Pricing power is an indicator:** specify the change, window, customers and alternatives. Retention does not by itself identify the cause; low costs may matter without a premium.
2. **Persistence is a record to explain:** a longer history tests some short-lived explanations without proving a unique mechanism or permanent protection.
3. **Beware the traps:** a single good year, returns that are high only because risk is high, and
   accounting that flatters. Check the record, risk exposure and accounting basis.
4. **Test the mechanism:** describe feasible entry, compare explanations and specify evidence that would weaken the claim.
5. **The full diagnosis** runs six steps: find the excess, test pricing power, check persistence,
   name the structure, reject mirages, and watch whether the moat is eroding or widening.
