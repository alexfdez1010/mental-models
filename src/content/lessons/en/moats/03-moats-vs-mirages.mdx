A shop has 60% of its market and a healthy operating balance. A rival can reproduce the product. Does the rival have an attractive entry project? You still need the bill and the calendar. A photo of a full castle does not measure the difficulty of getting there.

Here **mirage** means an inference that outruns its evidence: treating a popular product, being first, a good team or a large share as proof of durable protection. Those observations may matter. They do not answer the entry question by themselves.

The incumbent’s revenue is 120 × 12 = **1440 cr** per round. Its operating costs are 120 × 8 + 120 = **1080 cr**. Its **operating balance**, revenue minus these operating costs, is **360 cr**. Share is 120/200 = **60%**; balance divided by revenue is 360/1440 = **25%**. That last fraction is an operating margin, **not ROIC**: no invested-capital denominator or cost of capital has been supplied.

These are **pre-entry** observations. They are not an allocation of the market after a rival arrives. In particular, we will not assume both firms still sell 120 units in an unchanged 200-unit market. The rival’s later sales are a separate conditional premise, not a joint market-clearing forecast.

### When to use this distinction

Use it when a percentage is being asked to do more than it measures. A share is a sales ratio; an explanation of its persistence needs evidence about customers, costs and competitors.

## Put the rival’s proposal on a calendar

We compare only two choices: **do not enter**, with balance 0, and the **specified entry project**. The objective is the largest complete, undiscounted balance within this menu and window. A tie retains both choices; no tie-breaking rule is assumed.

The installation is available and financing is feasible by assumption. Sales and cost quotes are also stipulated: charging 11 cr does **not** establish that 120 units will sell. The first d rounds have no operations and no additional costs beyond K. Active rounds run from d + 1 through T. If T is at most d, there are no active rounds, but choosing the project still pays K.

Let a be the number of active rounds and S the complete balance:

$$
a=\max(T-d,0),\qquad S=a[q(p-c)-F]-K
$$

q(p − c) is the contribution after variable costs in one active round; subtract F to obtain that round’s operating balance. Multiply by a, then subtract the once-only setup. This is an accounting identity **under the declared conditions**, not an estimated demand model.

### Boundary of the account

We omit the incumbent’s response, other entry plans and prices, taxes, discounting, financing costs, residual value and uncertainty. There are no unlisted operating costs during preparation. A real proposal would need those boundaries checked. A positive S here establishes only a comparison with 0 inside this model; it does not predict actual entry, assess social welfare or classify a legal entry barrier.

## Complete the bill before choosing

With three active rounds, each yields 120 × (11 − 8) − 120 = **240 cr**. The full calculation is 3 × 240 − 600 = **+120 cr**. An equivalent revenue-and-cost account is:

- Revenue: 3 × 120 × 11 = **3960 cr**
- Variable costs: 3 × 120 × 8 = **2880 cr**
- Fixed operating costs: 3 × 120 = **360 cr**
- Setup: **600 cr** once
- Complete costs: 2880 + 360 + 600 = **3840 cr**
- Balance: 3960 − 3840 = **120 cr**

## Change one premise and inspect the result

The same incumbent observations fit both setup quotes. With K = 600 the specified entry account is positive; with K = 900 it is negative. Thus the observations alone do not identify whether this project beats staying out.

For the longer window, 4 × 240 − 900 = **60 cr**. For the tie, 3 × 240 − 720 = **0**: both choices maximize the stated objective. With lower sales, an active round yields 100 × 3 − 120 = **180 cr**, so 3 × 180 − 600 = **−60 cr**. A lower selling price alone cannot guarantee the original sales premise.

A temporary lead can therefore be valuable. It need not become permanent or impossible to copy to cover its complete costs in a given window. Conversely, “someone could make the product” does not show that a specified entry project is attractive after setup and preparation.

### When to revise

Use sensitivity to locate consequential missing information: a verified setup quote, preparation schedule or sales commitment could change the comparison. Do not replace a missing premise with a confident label such as “wide moat.” A cost that makes this project unattractive is not automatically an economic or legal barrier under every definition.

## Turn the four labels into investigations

A head start is like opening the stall before the next trader. It can produce sales during that interval, even if it creates no permanent obstacle. A “brilliant team” may possess routines that are costly to reproduce, but the adjective does not measure them. The useful task is to specify what a rival must actually do.

Share can affect costs through scale, while costs can affect sales and share. Customer access may influence both. A ratio alone identifies none of these causal paths. Likewise, a rival may compete through a niche, a substitute or interconnection rather than a perfect clone.

### Sources and scope

McAfee, Mialon and Williams compare competing definitions and emphasize timing and interactions in [What Is a Barrier to Entry?](https://doi.org/10.1257/0002828041302235). Their definitions do not turn every setup cost into an economic or antitrust barrier. The [2023 DOJ/FTC Merger Guidelines, section 3.2](https://www.justice.gov/atr/merger-guidelines/rebuttal-evidence) examine entry under expected post-entry conditions; [section 2.6](https://www.justice.gov/atr/merger-guidelines/applying-merger-guidelines/guideline-6) discusses access constraints and partial alternatives. Those are sources for the investigative distinctions, not a legal assessment of our fictional shop. All quantities, project options and accounting boundaries in this lesson are constructed.
