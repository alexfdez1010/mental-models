The last lesson introduced the pressure that profitable opportunities can attract. Here we separate a **benchmark**—a simplified model used for comparison—from a prediction about an actual business. We will preserve the financial distinction between return on capital and its cost, then ask which assumptions make entry attractive and what evidence is missing.

## Return on capital vs. the cost of capital

Start with the two numbers the whole model turns on. A business takes in **capital** —
money from owners and lenders — and uses it to earn a **return**. The **return on
invested capital** (ROIC) is just that return as a percentage: earn $15 a year on $100
of capital and your ROIC is 15%. Against it sits the **cost of capital**: what that
money *costs* you, which is really what your investors could earn somewhere else at
similar risk. If investors could get 8% elsewhere, your cost of capital is about 8% —
that's the bar you must clear just to justify existing.

The gap between them is the entire story:

- **ROIC > cost of capital.** You're earning more than your capital costs. This surplus
  is the **excess return** — economic value created, the treasure in the castle. It is
  also a flashing sign that says *attack me*.
- **ROIC = cost of capital.** You're earning exactly the going rate. The business is
  fine, the lights stay on, but there's no surplus — nothing for a rival to gain by
  copying you, and nothing for a moat to protect. Economists call this "normal returns,"
  and it is the **long-run equilibrium benchmark** under perfect competition.
- **ROIC < cost of capital.** You're destroying value — earning less than your capital
  could make elsewhere. Do this long enough and the capital leaves.

<Callout variant="info" title="When the benchmark reaches normal returns">
In the perfect-competition benchmark, many price-taking firms sell identical products, relevant information is available, and firms can enter and exit without restrictions. Economic profit encourages entry; additional market supply lowers price. The long-run equilibrium has zero economic profit, including the opportunity cost of capital. This does not mean zero accounting profit, instant adjustment or an inevitable path for every market. In an actual entry decision, compare the rival's costs and expected post-entry conditions, not just the incumbent's current return.
</Callout>

## Competitive pressure in the benchmark

A ball rolling down a hill is a useful analogy for competitive pressure: a reward can attract attempts to capture it. Its limit matters. Gravity has a measured law; market entry depends on technology, finance, customers and rivals' responses. There is no universal calendar on which every advantage reaches zero.

Ask: **which feasible attack would change this business's position, and what would it cost?** The following illustration is narrower still: a stipulated index responds to two controls. It is a way to inspect a recurrence, not evidence about how a network, firm or market actually develops.

## Investigate a feasible attack

The useful test is conditional: **what prevents a specified rival from competing effectively within a specified window?**

<Callout variant="tip" title="Questions for an entry hypothesis">
Describe the rival's feasible route, setup costs, operating costs, access to customers and preparation time. Then ask what evidence supports each condition. A product clone may leave customer relationships untouched; a different product may bypass them. An impossible “copy the whole company tomorrow” premise cannot identify an actual obstacle.
</Callout>

For example, copying a ride-hailing interface tells you that the visible design is reproducible. It does not tell you whether the rival can attract enough drivers and riders, interoperate with another network, or serve a useful niche. Each is a separate hypothesis. A network can constrain entry without being impossible to reproduce forever.

<details>
<summary>If an advantage can be competed away, why does anyone bother innovating at all?</summary>
Because the *temporary* excess return is still worth a great deal — and because the race
to earn it is exactly what drives progress. An innovator earns fat margins for the
months or years before rivals catch up; that prize is the incentive that funds the
innovation in the first place (straight from your **Incentives** course). Society gets
the better product *and* the eventual low price once competition arrives. The moat lens
doesn't say innovation is pointless. A temporary lead can earn a positive complete
project balance even if a rival later catches up. Innovation can also develop a network,
routines or another advantage whose cost and time of reproduction need investigating.
Lesson 3 works through the temporary case without requiring permanent protection.
</details>

## Why "we'll just run faster" isn't a moat

“We execute better” names a result or ambition, not the mechanism that sustains it. Rivals may hire, train or organize differently, but neither instant replication nor inevitable failure follows. Investigate the routines, learning time, employee retention and resources involved. Continuous effort can maintain a valuable capability; needing effort does not logically disqualify it.

Buffett's 2007 shareholder letter describes his preference for an enduring business advantage rather than dependence on a single exceptional leader. That is an investment criterion, not a theorem that good management is reproducible or that protected firms can safely neglect it. The castle analogy cannot do that work.

## Recap

1. Every business lives between two numbers: **return on capital** (what it earns) and
   **cost of capital** (what its money could earn elsewhere). The gap is the **excess
   return** — the treasure, and the target.
2. **Specify the benchmark.** Free entry and price-taking are assumptions under which long-run economic profit is zero; they are not a universal forecast.
3. **Name the obstacle.** Investigate customer access, costs, timing and the rival's alternatives.
4. **Separate illustration from evidence.** The index follows its stipulated recurrence; its category is not a business diagnosis.
5. **Investigate execution.** A team or a claim of effort does not by itself establish—or refute—durable protection.

Next we examine the **five moats** as possible mechanisms, each with conditions that
can strengthen or weaken its ability to constrain a rival.
