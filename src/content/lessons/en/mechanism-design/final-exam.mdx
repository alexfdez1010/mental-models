This is the whole course in one sitting. Every idea you've met shows up here as a question: the **great flip** from *analysing* a fixed game to *designing* the rules; **incentive compatibility**, where honesty is a best response, and its strongest form, **strategyproofness**; the **second-price auction**, where bidding your true value is a dominant strategy; the **revelation principle**, which lets you study only truthful mechanisms; the toolbox in the wild — Pigouvian taxes, cap-and-trade, and the matching markets that place kidneys and school seats without a price; and the **impossibility results** that prove where even the best rules must give something up. Hold the through-line in your head: mechanism design never asks people to be better than they are — it asks the *rules* to be smarter, so that self-interest walks itself to the outcome you wanted. Take a breath. There is no going back once you commit.

## Data for the allocation-rule questions

These are invented credits of transferable utility, abbreviated cr. Two teams have private values among 0, 3 and 7. Each simultaneously reports exit or a number in that set. Exit cancels the project. Construction costs 8; if cancelled, all payments and costs are zero. A built project gives team i utility v_i minus its payment. Each team can pay up to 5. The organizer is a committed source of financing, not a third strategic participant.

<Callout variant="success" title="Key takeaways">
You now hold the whole model. **Mechanism design** is game theory in reverse: instead of predicting the equilibrium of fixed rules, you fix the outcome you want and **engineer the rules** so self-interested play produces it — you control only the rules, never people's preferences or choices. The central prize is **incentive compatibility** (honesty is a best response) and its strongest form, **strategyproofness** (honesty is a *dominant* strategy); "I cut, you choose" is the clean example, where a selfish cutter is forced to split 50/50. The showpiece is the **second-price (Vickrey) auction** — highest bidder wins but pays the runner-up's bid, so bidding your **true value is a dominant strategy** because the price never depends on your own bid (and eBay's proxy bidding is exactly this). The **revelation principle** says any outcome reachable by any mechanism is reachable by a **truthful** one, so designers can study only honest rules — a tool for proving possibility and impossibility, not a prescription. The toolbox is everywhere: **Pigouvian taxes** and deposit-refunds internalise externalities, **cap-and-trade** reveals hidden abatement costs through a permit market, and **matching markets** (deferred acceptance, kidney exchange, school choice) place people without any price. And stay honest about the limits: **impossibility results** (Myerson–Satterthwaite, Gibbard–Satterthwaite, Arrow) prove you can't have efficiency, honesty, and budget balance all at once; **Goodhart's law** means mechanisms reward the letter not the spirit; **participation** constraints let players walk away; and every design assumes people actually reach the equilibrium — so **simplicity** is a real virtue. Never ask "how do I make these people behave?" Ask the designer's question: *what rule makes the behaviour I want each player's own best response?*
</Callout>
