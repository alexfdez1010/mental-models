A print-room detector flags a label. A second report also flags it. Two confirmations sound more convincing than one, until you discover that the second report simply imports the detector's first result. A new document is not necessarily a new observation.

The course has already warned against double-counting. This workshop goes further: you will reconstruct three report-generating processes, calculate the information left after the first result and audit which process fits the records. Dependence does not require throwing away every later report. It requires using the likelihood that matches what you already know.

## Define the hypothesis and update once

This is a **constructed probability model**, not a report of printer performance. The hypothesis **H** is “the label's alignment falls outside a predeclared tolerance.” Its alternative **not H** is “the alignment meets that tolerance.” The state stays fixed while reports are generated. A plus sign means a report flags the label; a minus sign means it does not.

The prior probability of H is **10%**. Report A flags **80% of labels under H**, and **20% under not H**. These are conditional rates within the two states, not an unspecified overall “accuracy.” Their **likelihood ratio (LR)** is **0.80 ÷ 0.20 = 4**.

Represent the probabilities by **1000 equally weighted model cases**: 100 have H and 900 have not H. The cases are a stipulated probability catalogue. They are neither an observed validation sample nor a promise about the next 1000 actual labels.

| State | Model cases | Report A flags | Report A does not flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | 100 | 80 | 20 |
| Not H | 900 | 180 | 720 |
| Total | 1000 | 260 | 740 |

After A flags, the relevant set contains **80 + 180 = 260 cases**. The posterior is **80/260 = 4/13 ≈ 30.77%**. In odds form:

**Prior odds H : not H = 1 : 9**

**After A flags: 1 : 9 × 4 = 4 : 9**, giving **4/(4 + 9) = 4/13**.

## Specify what the second report contains

Consider three separate hypothetical pipelines. In each, both A and B individually flag 80% of H cases and 20% of not-H cases. The prior remains 10%. What changes is their joint behaviour.

### Pipeline C — B copies A

B imports A's exact flag field and has no other input relevant to the hypothesis. The pair must agree:

| A result, B result | H cases, total 100 | Not-H cases, total 900 |
|---|---|---|
| Plus, plus | 80 | 180 |
| Plus, minus | 0 | 0 |
| Minus, plus | 0 | 0 |
| Minus, minus | 20 | 720 |

Two flags retain **80 H cases and 180 not-H cases**, exactly the same subset as the first flag. The posterior stays **80/260 = 4/13 ≈ 30.77%**. Copying the result does not repeat the underlying measurement.

### Pipeline I — measurements are conditionally independent

Here B has the same within-state flag rates, and its result is **conditionally independent of A given H, and also given not H**. This means that once the state is fixed, learning A's result does not change B's specified result probabilities.

| A result, B result | H cases, total 100 | Not-H cases, total 900 |
|---|---|---|
| Plus, plus | 64 | 36 |
| Plus, minus | 16 | 144 |
| Minus, plus | 16 | 144 |
| Minus, minus | 4 | 576 |

For example, the two-flag probabilities are **0.80 × 0.80 = 0.64 under H** and **0.20 × 0.20 = 0.04 under not H**. That produces **100 × 0.64 = 64** and **900 × 0.04 = 36** weighted cases. After two flags, the posterior is **64/(64 + 36) = 64%**.

Conditional independence is a model assumption, not a synonym for “two documents.” [Berkeley's account of naive Bayes](https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/textbook/ml/naive-bayes.html) makes this conditioning on the hypothesis class explicit when it factors a joint likelihood into individual terms.

### Pipeline P — dependence leaves some additional information

In the third model, B sometimes reuses A. For each label, an unobserved routing choice copies A with probability **3/8** and otherwise obtains the conditionally independent measurement from Pipeline I with probability **5/8**. This choice is independent of the true state and A’s result. We know the routing probabilities, but not which route generated B for the current label.

For example, after A flags, B flags under H with probability **3/8 + (5/8 × 0.80) = 7/8**. Under not H, that probability is **3/8 + (5/8 × 0.20) = 1/2**. The full table of state and report outcomes is therefore:

| A result, B result | H cases, total 100 | Not-H cases, total 900 |
|---|---|---|
| Plus, plus | 70 | 90 |
| Plus, minus | 10 | 90 |
| Minus, plus | 10 | 90 |
| Minus, minus | 10 | 630 |

Check B's individual rates: **(70 + 10)/100 = 80% under H**, and **(90 + 90)/900 = 20% under not H**. A has the same margins. Nevertheless, two flags now retain **70 H and 90 not-H cases**, so the posterior is **70/160 = 7/16 = 43.75%**. It is neither the copy result nor the independent-measurement result.

Each table is a separate, complete probability catalogue for the state and report outcomes; Pipeline P averages over the unobserved routing choice. Its columns sum to the same state totals. None promises those exact frequencies in a realised sample. The overlap table defines one possible dependent process; it is not a universal formula for all reports that share inputs.

## Update using the information that remains after the first report

The general chaining rule comes from conditional probability, not from an assumption that every clue is independent. For the observed pair A-plus and B-plus:

**P(A-plus and B-plus given H) = P(A-plus given H) × P(B-plus given H and A-plus)**

Apply the same identity under not H, then divide the two expressions. [Stanford's conditional-probability lecture](https://web.stanford.edu/class/archive/cs/cs109/cs109.1202/lectures/04_cond_bayes.pdf) develops this general product rule before applying Bayes.

The result is:

**Final odds = prior odds × LR for A-plus × conditional LR for B-plus after A-plus**

where the last factor is:

**P(B-plus given H and A-plus) ÷ P(B-plus given not H and A-plus)**

The multiplication remains valid for dependent reports when the conditional likelihoods are the right ones. Conditional independence in both states is a sufficient way to replace that last factor with B's marginal likelihood ratio. The essential check is whether the proposed factor equals the correct history-conditioned ratio; independence is not the only possible way an equality could happen.

Here are the actual second factors:

| Pipeline | B-plus given H and A-plus | B-plus given not H and A-plus | Second conditional LR | Final odds H : not H |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copy C | 80/80 = 1 | 180/180 = 1 | 1 | 4 : 9 |
| Independent I | 64/80 = 0.80 | 36/180 = 0.20 | 4 | 16 : 9 |
| Partial dependence P | 70/80 = 7/8 | 90/180 = 1/2 | 7/4 = 1.75 | 7 : 9 |

For P, **4 : 9 × 7/4 = 7 : 9**, which converts to **7/(7 + 9) = 7/16**. Equivalently, the joint likelihood ratio is **0.70/0.10 = 7**; multiplying prior odds **1 : 9** by 7 gives the same answer. Joint and sequential calculations must agree when they use the same model.

For Pipeline P, a plus-minus pair **is** possible: its row contains **10 H cases and 90 not-H cases**. Thus **P(H given A-plus and B-minus) = 10/100 = 10%**. Sequentially, B-minus has conditional probabilities **10/80 = 1/8** under H and **90/180 = 1/2** under not H. The ratio is **1/4**, so **4 : 9 × 1/4 = 1 : 9**, returning to the original prior in this particular example.

Agreement does not automatically add a fixed amount, and disagreement does not automatically cancel an earlier report. Their diagnostic strength depends on the specified likelihoods. This model happens to give a full cancellation for that pattern.

For Pipeline C, plus-minus has probability zero under **both** states. It is not evidence with a calculable posterior of zero: the required normalizing probability is itself zero. A contradiction with the model is a reason to check or extend it.

A zero in an observed finite validation sample would be a different kind of information. It would not by itself prove that the underlying probability is exactly zero. Our zeros are stipulated features of an ideal copying model, rather than estimates justified merely by an empty cell.

## Audit the process before choosing its likelihoods

A source log should identify the item, original measurement, image or dataset, report version and transformations used to produce each output. “Another person wrote it” and “another organisation published it” do not specify which observations were new. A repeated method can produce additional information, while a differently named method can reuse the same failure-producing input.

To estimate the required rates from real work, retain linked results for **the same items**, with independently established state labels and a relevant reference population. Check positive and negative first-result histories, not just the cases where both reports agree. Rates estimated on a selected history or another type of label may not transfer to the current question. Finite validation brings sampling uncertainty; our stipulated tables have not measured that uncertainty away.

Also retain what the publication process tells you. In Pipeline C, B is a deterministic copy with no extra result-selection step. If a later investigation reveals an additional measurement or selective publication, that is a changed information set and needs a new model, rather than a blanket instruction to ignore every repeated sentence.

### Conditional independence is not independence in the mixed population

Even Pipeline I's reports are associated before the state is known. Each flag has overall probability **0.10 × 0.80 + 0.90 × 0.20 = 0.26**. Both flags have probability **0.10 × 0.64 + 0.90 × 0.04 = 0.10**, whereas **0.26 × 0.26 = 0.0676**. These differ. Conditional independence within each state therefore does not imply independence after the states are mixed together.

For this audit, the relevant factorisation is within **each hypothesis state**, not independence tested only across the pooled labels. The same hidden state can make both reports tend to agree in the mixed population even when their remaining errors are independent within states.

## Transfer to a binding inspection

A library considers whether a book's binding needs reinforcement, hypothesis H. The prior is **20%**. A first inspection flags **60% of H books** and **20% of not-H books**. After a first flag, the library receives either a copied summary or a new inspection result.

For the new inspection, the following rates are supplied **conditional on the first flag**: a further flag occurs in **60% of H books** and **30% of not-H books** within that history. These are not claims about its rates after a first negative result.

<details>
<summary>Check the transfer by reconstructing the cases</summary>

The new flag retains **120 × 0.60 = 72 H** and **160 × 0.30 = 48 not H**, giving **72/120 = 3/5**. A copy retains the entire first-flag subset instead. The two routes agree with the odds calculation.
