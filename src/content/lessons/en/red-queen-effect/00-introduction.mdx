There's a scene in Lewis Carroll's *Through the Looking-Glass* where Alice finds herself running hand-in-hand with the Red Queen, faster and faster, trees and landmarks streaming past — and yet, when they finally stop, she's standing under the very same tree she started from. Baffled and out of breath, she protests that back home, running that hard would take you *somewhere*. The Queen is unimpressed: "Now, *here*, you see, it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place. If you want to get somewhere else, you must run at least twice as fast as that!"

In 1973, evolutionary biologist **Leigh Van Valen** borrowed the name for a hypothesis about evolution in environments where other organisms also change. The **Red Queen effect** helps identify a possibility: improvement can maintain a position against adapting counterparts. Parallel improvement alone does not establish reciprocal response.

## The one idea to take away

Here's the entire model in a single line, before we spend the course earning it:

<Callout variant="tip" title="The one-sentence version">
**When your rivals adapt too, you may need to keep improving just to hold your position — and even improving as fast as you can may only keep you in the same place.** Getting *better* (absolute improvement) is not the same as getting *ahead* (relative position). In a particular race, improvement can sustain a position without creating a durable lead. Consequences depend on the case.
</Callout>

**Absolute improvement** compares performance with its own past; **relative position** compares it with a current benchmark. Reducing your time does not guarantee overtaking a rival who also reduces theirs. A fixed environment does not make those measures identical either. State whether you mean a difference, ratio or rank, and how it relates to the outcome you care about.

## Watch the race that goes nowhere

Reading about a treadmill is one thing; watching two competitors sprint up it is another. Below is a coevolution race between **you** and a **rival**. Each generation, both of you improve — and crucially, whichever one is *behind* pushes harder to catch up, so your two lines climb in near-lockstep. Press **Auto-run** (or advance a generation at a time) and watch the two capability lines soar upward together while the **relative-advantage meter** below them barely twitches off centre. That's the paradox: enormous absolute progress, near-zero relative change.

Then try the button that teaches the other half of the model. Press **Stop running (opt out)** and watch what happens to a competitor who decides the treadmill isn't worth it.

Notice what this simulation assumes: both parties keep improving, and the one behind receives a stronger push. When you stop adapting your line, the other keeps moving and the gap shifts against you. That illustrates a possibility under these rules, not proof that any pause destroys a real service. Choosing requires costs, benefits, a horizon and a feasible fallback, which the final workshop supplies.

## Why this belongs in a latticework

The Red Queen earns its place because it's really two models wearing one name, and they reinforce each other:

- In **biology**, it's the engine behind **coevolutionary arms races** — predator and prey, host and parasite — and even the leading explanation for the single weirdest fact in evolution: *why sexual reproduction exists at all.* (It's a defence against parasites. We'll get there.)
- In **strategy and economics**, it prompts a question about which part of an improvement a rival could match, when, and with what consequences. Imitation can erode a positional lead while leaving functional benefits for users or firms.

Both applications invite the questions: *what improved, against which benchmark, and how could the other side respond?* The analogy does not replace evidence of response or a benefit-and-cost account.

## The map of the course

Five teaching lessons, then one exam you can't undo. The route:

1. **Running to Stay in Place** — Van Valen's law and the heart of the model: what *coevolution* is, and the absolute-versus-relative distinction illustrated with a speed table, so "the gap stays the same" becomes something you can count.
2. **Coevolutionary Arms Races** — the great biological duels: predator vs. prey, the relentless host–parasite war, the "life–dinner" asymmetry, escalation and its costs — and the startling Red Queen explanation for *why sex exists*.
3. **The Treadmill Trap** — the misconception that fools nearly everyone: mistaking absolute improvement for progress when the baseline is moving. Positional vs. absolute goods, when running is worth it, and when to step off the treadmill.
4. **The Red Queen in Business & Strategy** — operational effectiveness, feature wars, response loops, conditional collective-action traps and options for changing a competitive position.
5. **[Audit a Moving Benchmark](/red-queen-effect/05-audit-a-moving-benchmark)** — a workshop on comparable observations, response evidence, functional and positional benefits, costs and alternatives over the same horizon.

Then a **Final Exam** — graded, one question at a time, one-way: once you answer, it locks. No back button, no retries, 70% to pass.

### How to use this course

One habit does most of the work: every time you see a "we improved!" claim, ask *compared to what, and is the competition improving too?* Treat each exercise the way you should treat the gazelle — guess the outcome *before* you reveal the answer, because the small sting of being wrong is what makes the absolute-versus-relative distinction finally stick. And play with the coevolution race above until the two patterns — climbing lines, motionless meter — feel obvious in your gut. A treadmill you've *watched* go nowhere sticks far better than one you've only read about.
