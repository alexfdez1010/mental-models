Business rivals can respond to one another, making a performance improvement less distinctive. That is a useful analogy to reciprocal adaptation, not evidence that every company coevolves or that every improvement triggers a matching move. This lesson considers possible responses and strategic options; the next workshop turns them into a complete audit.

The practical question is whether to maintain, improve or change the dimension of competition. None wins by definition. Specify the value, cost, response and alternative before choosing.

## The Red Queen, spoken by a CEO

**The analogy.** In biology the gazelle runs because the cheetah runs back. In business the gazelle is your company and the cheetahs are your competitors — except they're smarter than cheetahs and they're watching your every move with spreadsheets. When you cut a price, launch a feature, or open on Sundays, you're not acting on a fixed environment. You're acting on *other agents who adapt right back at you.* That's the whole difference between business and, say, paying off a mortgage. The mortgage doesn't fight back.

**The precise idea.** A **competitive arms race** involves repeated changes in response to a counterpart whose behavior alters the value of your own move. Unlike genetic coevolution, business responses may involve deliberate plans, purchases and contracts. A capability measure, a competitive position and a customer outcome are different quantities: evidence connecting them is needed.

**Worked example — shared productivity.** Imagine two otherwise comparable factories each doubling output per worker with the same technique. Their output ratio remains equal if it began equal, although both produce more per worker. That does not tell us how much of the saving reaches customers, workers or owners, or whether paying for the technique was worthwhile. Those conclusions require demand, price, cost and timing assumptions.

“We have to keep innovating just to stay in business” is a hypothesis about a fallback, not a conclusion demonstrated by flat market share. Check what active maintenance, a smaller upgrade or a different service would actually deliver. Pausing one optional project is not the same as shutting down the business.

<Callout variant="info" title="Before claiming an edge">
Ask which part rivals can match, when, what remains useful, and how the net result compares with a feasible fallback.
</Callout>

### When to use it

Use the lens when someone promises an edge. Ask what rivals can match, how soon, what value remains after matching, and what the improvement costs. A temporary advantage can be worthwhile; a long-lived one can be too expensive.

## Why operational effectiveness is not strategy

This is the marquee section — the single most useful idea a working strategist can carry, and it's pure Red Queen. It comes from **Michael Porter**, whose [publisher summary of the essay](https://store.hbr.org/product/what-is-strategy/96608) distinguishes effectiveness from positioning: *operational effectiveness is not strategy.*

**The two terms, precisely.**

- **Operational effectiveness** means doing the *same activities* as your rivals, but **better, faster, or cheaper** — running the same race well. Buying the best tools, adopting the latest best practices, squeezing waste, hitting higher quality at lower cost. It's about *running the existing race more efficiently.*
- **Strategic positioning** means doing **different activities** from rivals, or the same activities in a deliberately different way, to occupy a **unique position** that's valuable to a particular set of customers — changing the basis of competition without guaranteeing the outcome.

**The Red Queen insight.** Shared best practices can improve many firms while reducing how distinctive an early adopter remains. Imitation can erode a lead without undoing lower resource use, better quality or benefits earned before copying. It does not determine margins or the distribution of gains by itself. Distinctive positioning and effective operation answer different strategic questions; both need evidence and an account of costs.

**Worked example — a shared tool.** In an invented comparison, two stores start with equal checkout times and each halves them using the same available software. Their time ratio remains equal and both serve a given checkout faster. That shared functional gain remains after adoption. Whether the initial lead or the ongoing improvement justifies the software cost is a separate question.

Contrast a hypothetical airline organized around a narrow service with a rival offering many connecting routes and service levels. Matching the narrow service may conflict with the rival’s existing activities, making imitation costly. This is a possible positioning mechanism, not a claim that every low-cost airline follows one fixed recipe or cannot be copied.

<Callout variant="info" title="Execution needs a comparison">
A copyable improvement can have retained value. It does not guarantee a durable lead, and copyability alone does not show whether its cost is justified.
</Callout>

## Feature wars and spending arms races

**The analogy.** Two rival armies dig trenches; one builds a higher wall, so the other builds a higher one, so the first goes higher still. Eventually both have spent fortunes on walls and neither is safer than when the walls were low. Replace "walls" with "features," "perks," or "ad budgets" and you have the everyday Red Queen of competitive markets: a **spending arms race**, where matching escalates costs for everyone with no lasting advantage for anyone.

**The precise idea.** A feature war *can become* a **collective-action trap** if each party prefers to match the other, yet both would prefer mutual restraint to mutual escalation. Those preferences must be established. Matching may also deliver functional benefits or expand demand; costs may be temporary or recurring. Neither a fixed total prize nor a permanent bill follows from the label “feature war.”

**Possible mechanisms to investigate, not measured conclusions about every industry.** The rows below suggest where to collect evidence. Whether costs persist, customers benefit or a niche is feasible must be checked in the actual case.

| Arms-race domain | The escalation | The cost | Who can opt out? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Smartphone specs** | More megapixels, more cameras, faster refresh — each maker matches the last | R&D and bill-of-materials rise; buyers stop noticing | Hard — a niche "simple phone" brand can, by *not* playing |
| **Airline perks / legroom** | One adds more legroom or free bags, rivals match, then someone removes them to cut fares | Either margins fall (perks) or the perk becomes baseline | A carrier that picks a clear position (ultra-cheap *or* premium) |
| **Store hours** | One opens late, rivals match, soon all are open 24/7 | Labour and overhead rise; customers split across the same hours | Local norms or regulation (some places mandate closing) |
| **Free shipping** | One offers it, rivals must match, the threshold drops toward zero | Shipping cost eats margin industry-wide | Almost nobody, once it's expected |
| **Ad / marketing spend** | Each raises spend to be heard over rivals' raised spend | Budgets balloon; relative share barely moves | Only by changing channel or position entirely |
| **Talent / salary wars** | Each raises offers to poach scarce talent | Comp inflates across the sector | A firm competing on mission or location, not pay |

Read these as hypotheses about costly matching. Free shipping, for example, could transfer value to customers, change demand, or be profitable for some firms. A collective-action trap requires the relevant payoff comparisons, not merely the observation that several firms offer the same perk.

### When to use it

Use the matching question before committing resources: what changes if we act and the rival responds, compared with a feasible fallback? Consider a different dimension too, but price the transition and allow rivals to enter it later. No label guarantees an escape or makes further improvement irrational.

## Other response loops

Tools and policies can change the behavior of a relevant counterpart. The constructed sequence and candidate mechanisms below help frame what evidence to collect.

**A constructed filtering loop.** A message filter blocks a known unwanted pattern. A sender changes the pattern to get through; the filter team updates its rules. If the sender then changes again because of those rules, there is a reciprocal response loop. It may continue, stop paying, or be interrupted by changing access rules. This invented sequence illustrates what response evidence would need to establish.

**Candidate response mechanisms to investigate.**

| Arms race | The loop | The escalation & cost | Who can opt out? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spam vs. spam filters** | Spammers send → filters learn to block → spammers mutate wording → filters update | Repeated engineering on both sides; users pay in false positives | Closed/whitelist-only systems, at the cost of openness |
| **Fraud vs. fraud detection** | Fraudsters find a hole → detection closes it → fraudsters find a new hole | Rising security spend; friction for honest users | Depends on exposure and access rules |
| **SEO vs. search-algorithm updates** | Sites optimise for the ranking → engine changes the algorithm → sites re-optimise | Constant rework; "best practices" expire each update | Sites that earn genuine authority, less so |
| **Cybersecurity: offense vs. defense** | Attackers find an exploit → defenders patch → attackers find the next | Potential recurring work on both sides | Depends on architecture and exposure |

These examples point to possible counter-response, not a theorem of endless escalation. Some measures remain useful despite adaptation; some responses are constrained or stop paying. The abstract of [Dawkins and Krebs](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1979.0081) explicitly considers endings as well as cycles. Biological evidence cannot establish the payoff or inevitability of an organizational race.

<Callout variant="info" title="Response is a possibility to investigate">
An adapting counterpart may limit the duration of an advantage. A race can also end, stall or change; the label does not establish an endless obligation to spend.
</Callout>

### When to use it

Use the lens to plan a response window and a review, rather than assuming a countermeasure will remain effective forever or become useless immediately. Continued adaptation, a stable arrangement and an ending are all possibilities to investigate.

## The game theory of the treadmill

**The analogy.** In one possible game, picture each player holding a single lever marked "escalate." If you pull it and your rival doesn't, you win. If your rival pulls it and you don't, you lose. If you both pull it, you're back to even — but both poorer for the cost. And if *neither* of you pulls it, you're back to even *and* both richer. So the outcome everyone would prefer is "nobody escalates" — yet each player, reasoning alone, pulls the lever anyway, because not pulling it while your rival might is the worst outcome of all. Everybody pulls. Everybody loses. Welcome to the treadmill, drawn as a game.

**The precise structure, when it applies.** A **prisoner’s dilemma** requires a particular payoff ordering: each player prefers escalation regardless of what the other does, yet each prefers mutual restraint to mutual escalation. A race with those preferences can sustain individually tempting but collectively disliked escalation. Not every adapting rivalry has that ordering; the next workshop does not specify both parties’ payoffs and therefore cannot establish it.

**Constructed example — an unstable advertising pause.** Suppose two event organizers each prefer advertising if the other does not, prefer matching an advertised rival to being the only one quiet, but both prefer mutual restraint to mutual spending. Those stipulated preferences have the dilemma structure. A unilateral pause is tempting to exploit. This example is an invented ordering of preferences, not evidence about a historical advertising ban.

**Why restraint can be fragile.** In the stipulated dilemma, each party benefits from departing from mutual restraint if the other remains restrained. Monitoring, enforcement or a different incentive structure may change that comparison. Do not assume every standard or agreement has the same incentives or makes every affected person better off.

### When to use it

Use a payoff comparison before diagnosing a dilemma. An agreement’s stability depends on incentives, monitoring, enforcement and the opportunities to change behavior. A standard can alter the game, but its desirability and effect must also be assessed; mutual restraint is not automatically beneficial to customers or other affected people.

## How to escape: changing the game

Changing the game is one possible response alongside maintaining or improving within it. The following moves can make an advantage harder to match, but each has costs and limits.

**(a) Build a defensible advantage.** A **moat** is a barrier that makes an advantage harder or more costly to match: possible sources include network effects, switching costs, distinctive assets, reputation and scale. A copied feature may leave such a barrier intact. Whether it matters depends on customers’ alternatives, its maintenance costs and how it changes over time; none of these labels guarantees permanence.

**(b) Differentiate.** Serving a different need or niche can reduce direct matching on the current dimension. Test demand, transition costs and whether the apparent opening is accessible to rivals too. A niche remains a competitive position, not an automatically “absolute” good immune to response.

**(c) Identify retained functional value.** Reliability, task completion or resource savings can benefit users even when rivals match them. The positional premium may shrink while that function remains useful. This does not guarantee the benefit exceeds the cost; separate the two components instead of treating the entire product as a purely positional prize.

**(d) Change the rules via standards or regulation.** As the game theory showed, sometimes the only way off a treadmill is to **change the rules for everyone at once** — back an industry standard that ends a pointless feature war, support regulation that bans a mutually-destructive escalation, or set a baseline that removes the lever. This is the collective escape, applied deliberately.

The common question is what each option delivers after feasible responses and costs. Speed, position, retained function and a change of rules can all matter. Evaluate the proposed option against a stated fallback; changing games has no automatic priority.

## Recap

Keep these distinctions available for the applied workshop:

1. Rival responses can erode a positional advantage; parallel improvements alone do not establish reciprocal response.
2. Operational effectiveness and positioning ask different questions. Shared functional gains can remain useful after imitation.
3. A collective-action trap requires the relevant payoff ordering, not just matching behavior.
4. Adaptation does not imply endless escalation or guaranteed collapse after any pause.
5. A moat, niche or common rule can change a comparison, but each needs evidence and costs.
6. Compare net values, a feasible fallback, response timing and the same horizon before choosing.
