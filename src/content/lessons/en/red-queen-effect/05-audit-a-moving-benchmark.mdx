A team brings a faster benchmark to a review meeting. Someone declares victory. Someone else notices a rival got faster too and declares the whole project wasted. Neither conclusion follows yet. The stopwatch has done its job; now the meeting needs to do its own.

You will audit one invented service, separate the measurements from the explanation, and choose between an upgrade and active maintenance under stated assumptions. **Every organization, measurement, score, credit, cost and probability in this workshop is constructed for teaching.** None is evidence about a real market. The method is the transferable part.

## Choose what the gap measures

A **benchmark** is a specified test used as a reference. Here both services perform the same standardized job, on the same test equipment, with unchanged quality requirements and the same measurement procedure at each checkpoint. Time is in **milliseconds per job**; lower is better. Treat the entries as exact inputs to this exercise, not estimates of noisy real-world means.

| Checkpoint | Service A | Service B | Signed gap A − B | A's rank |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Initial | 1000 | 900 | 100 | Slower |
| First recheck | 800 | 700 | 100 | Slower |
| Second recheck | 650 | 600 | 50 | Slower |

**Absolute improvement** compares a service with its own past: A reduces its time by 1000 − 800 = 200, then 800 − 650 = 150, for a total reduction of **350 milliseconds per job**. B reduces it by 200, then 100, for **300 milliseconds per job** in total.

A **signed additive gap** subtracts the two times at a checkpoint: A − B. Positive means A takes longer; zero means a tie. The gap stays at 100 initially, then narrows to 50. The **rank**, just the ordering, never changes: A is slower in all three rows. A smaller deficit can matter even before a rank changes.

A ratio asks a different question: **how many times B's time does A take?** Divide A by B. Initially 1000/900 = 10/9 ≈ 1.111; at the first recheck 800/700 = 8/7 ≈ 1.143; at the second 650/600 = 13/12 ≈ 1.083. Lower is better on this ratio too. From the initial test to the first recheck, the additive gap is unchanged while the ratio gets worse. Neither calculation is broken: one measures extra milliseconds, the other a multiple of B's time.

### When to use this comparison

Choose the measure that fits the decision before evaluating the result: an extra-time requirement may use the additive gap; a proportional slowdown requirement may use the ratio; a winner-only contest may use rank. Keep all relevant measures visible. A time table alone does not establish price, reliability, customer value or survival. A stopwatch is a measuring tool with a very modest job description.

## Ask whether the other side actually responded

Two rising capabilities can have several histories. Perhaps B copied A. Perhaps both adopted the same independently available tool. Perhaps both had projects planned before either release. The observed rows do not distinguish these explanations.

In biology, **coevolution** requires reciprocal evolutionary response, not merely simultaneous change or a convenient match between traits. Janzen's [definition and cautions](https://doi.org/10.2307/2408229) make that distinction explicit. The [Dawkins–Krebs abstract](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1979.0081) describes how reciprocal counter-adaptation *may* produce escalation and lists several ways a race can end. A business team deliberately changing software is an analogy to that feedback structure; it is not a genetic process established by those papers.

Build a response audit before calling the service trace a race:

| Evidence to collect | What it can help distinguish | Remaining limit |
|---|---|---|
| Plans dated before either release | Already planned work versus a response | A plan can change; inspect execution too |
| B's exposure to A's release and subsequent choices | A plausible route from A's move to B's change | Timing alone does not exclude common causes |
| Comparable workstreams with different exposure, tracked over a declared window | Shared improvements versus exposure-linked changes | Non-random exposure can select different teams |
| A's later choices after B's move | A return response rather than only A influencing B | One observed direction does not establish reciprocity |

**Worked audit.** Suppose both teams' dated plans specified the same new library before either release, and B did not see A's release before implementing it. That weakens the claim that *this* change by B was caused by A's release; it remains consistent with common technology. If B instead records an unplanned switch after seeing A, the copying explanation gains support, but an unmeasured common pressure could still matter.

For a bounded test, declare the feature, exposure, quality measure and review window in advance. Where feasible, vary exposure randomly across comparable internal teams, avoid teams sharing the exposure across assignments, and measure every assigned team on the same schedule. That can test an exposure effect in the pilot under those assumptions; it does not make an external rival random, establish both directions, or guarantee later behavior. If random variation is unavailable, state the observational limitations rather than awarding the timeline a causal badge.

### When the evidence is thin

Use a range of response assumptions for planning and collect information that could change the choice. No response within a short window is not proof of no later response. Conversely, a possible response is not a certainty that every rival will match every gain forever.

## Account for what imitation removes and what remains

We now make a **separate invented planning model**. Its numbers do not come from converting milliseconds into value. A performance index is just a stipulated higher-is-better score. **Utility credits** put the benefits and costs on one common comparison scale; they are not currency or measured customer benefit. We assume credits add, and our decision rule prefers the larger probability-weighted net total. A person with a hard loss limit or a different attitude to uncertainty may need a different rule.

Two feasible actions are available over **one period**. **Maintain** keeps the service operating at index 80; it is active maintenance, not shutting down for free. **Upgrade** raises A to 100 and pays an additional cost of 14 credits once. Ordinary operating costs are already treated consistently in all listed values.

| Rule | Credits |
|---|---:|
| Functional value at A's index 80 | 8 |
| Functional value at A's index 100 | 16 |
| Positional premium if A is ahead of B | 14 |
| Positional premium if tied | 4 |
| Positional premium if behind | 0 |
| Extra upgrade cost, paid once | 14 |

The **functional value** depends here on A's capability; the **positional premium** depends on A's position against B. These are declared rules for this case. They are not a fixed prize pool, and we have not specified B's utility. Nothing here establishes a zero-sum game or a game-theoretic equilibrium, a combination of choices where neither party wants to change its own choice while the other holds theirs fixed.

| A's action | B's response within the period | Functional value | Positional premium | Extra cost | A's net credits |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Maintain at 80 | B remains at 90 | 8 | 0 | 0 | 8 |
| Upgrade to 100 | B remains at 90 | 16 | 14 | 14 | 16 |
| Upgrade to 100 | B matches at 100 | 16 | 4 | 14 | 6 |

Reproduce each row: 8 + 0 − 0 = **8**; 16 + 14 − 14 = **16**; 16 + 4 − 14 = **6**. Matching removes ten credits of positional premium, not the eight-credit functional improvement from 8 to 16. Nevertheless, the upgrade's extra cost can make its *net* value worse than maintaining: 6 is below 8.

### When to use this account

Break a mixed benefit into the parts your rival can erode and the parts that remain. Do not label an entire product either “absolute” or “positional” and throw away the other column. Also compare a concrete fallback: choosing not to buy this upgrade is not the same action as abandoning the service.

## Weight the responses before choosing

A **probability** is a weight between zero and one for a specified outcome. Let **q** be the assumed probability that B matches **within the period, conditional on A choosing Upgrade**. The other branch has probability 1 − q. These two outcomes cover every possibility *in this model*. The three earlier time measurements do not estimate q. Under Maintain we separately stipulate the listed net value of 8; we are not applying Upgrade's response probability to a different action.

The **expected value** is a probability-weighted average. At q = 0.30, imagine the model spread across 100 equivalent hypothetical cases: 70 no-match outcomes at 16 and 30 match outcomes at 6. Divide their total by 100:

$$E[U]=0.70(16)+0.30(6)=11.2+1.8=13.$$

This is a calculation device, not a new sample or a guarantee that any 100 real cases would have those frequencies. In one case the upgrade realizes **16 or 6**, not 13. Maintaining gives 8 by assumption, so the expected advantage of upgrading is **13 − 8 = 5 credits**.

For any allowed q:

$$E[U]=(1-q)16+q6=16-10q,$$
$$\Delta=E[U]-E[M]=(16-10q)-8=8-10q.$$

Here Δ means upgrade minus maintenance on the same scale and horizon. Solve Δ = 0: 8 = 10q, hence **q = 0.80**. Below that threshold Upgrade has higher expected value; above it Maintain does. At equality this rule is indifferent; no preference between ties has been stipulated.

| Assumed q under Upgrade | Expected upgrade | Maintain | Upgrade minus Maintain |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.30 | 13 | 8 | 5 |
| 0.80 | 8 | 8 | 0 |
| 0.90 | 7 | 8 | −1 |

Explore the **incremental branch values**: no match gives 16 − 8 = **8**, and match gives 6 − 8 = **−2**. The cost and fallback are already deducted. Subtracting either again would double-count them.

### When to use the threshold

Suppose your defensible assumptions only place q between 0.30 and 0.60. The advantage ranges from 5 to 2, so Upgrade wins throughout that range under the stated rule. If q might be anywhere from 0.60 to 0.90, the advantage ranges from 2 to −1 and changes sign. Do not hide that uncertainty inside a confident point estimate: identify evidence about response timing, revise the range, negotiate the cost, or defer this optional upgrade while actively maintaining. None of those follows from “our chart goes up” alone.

Now vary only that cost, calling it **C**, while preserving all other one-period rules. Gross upgrade value is 30 without a match and 20 with a match, so:

$$\Delta=(1-q)(30-C)+q(20-C)-8=22-C-10q.$$

| Cost C | Upgrade net if no match | Upgrade net if match | Advantage over Maintain | What follows for any q from zero to one |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| 8 | 22 | 12 | 14 − 10q | Upgrade always has higher expected value |
| 14 | 16 | 6 | 8 − 10q | Choice changes at q = 0.80 |
| 24 | 6 | −4 | −2 − 10q | Maintain always has higher expected value |

At cost 8, even certain matching leaves 12, above Maintain's 8. Copying does **not** make every upgrade worthless. At cost 24, even no matching leaves only 6, below 8. An uncopied lead does **not** justify every price. These are bounded comparisons under fixed values, not instructions to ignore rivals or to spend without limit.

### When to use sensitivity analysis

A **sensitivity analysis** changes a stated input and recomputes the choice. Use it to discover which assumption matters before buying more data. If both response branches already beat the fallback, finer knowledge of q will not change this decision; costs, feasibility or omitted outcomes still might. If different branches favor different actions, response uncertainty can matter.

## Transfer to two periods without changing the comparison halfway

A new proposal has a **one-time cost of 38** and a **two-period horizon**. This replaces the earlier cost and horizon. Credits add across periods, and later credits receive the same weight as earlier credits. The service is upgraded before period one; B cannot match until just before period two. B then either matches or does not match, with conditional probability q or 1 − q under Upgrade. There is no further renewal cost. Maintain produces 8 in each period.

| Action and response | Period one gross | Period two gross | One-time extra cost | Two-period net | Difference versus Maintain |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Maintain | 8 | 8 | 0 | 16 | 0 |
| Upgrade, no match before period two | 30 | 30 | 38 | 22 | 6 |
| Upgrade, match before period two | 30 | 20 | 38 | 12 | −4 |

Rebuild it: Maintain is 8 + 8 = **16**. No match gives 30 + 30 − 38 = **22**; match gives 30 + 20 − 38 = **12**. The incremental branches are therefore **6** and **−4**. Deducting 38 in both periods invents a second payment. Comparing either upgrade total with only one period of maintaining drops half the fallback.

$$E[U]=(1-q)22+q12=22-10q,$$
$$\Delta=(22-10q)-16=6-10q.$$

The new tie is **q = 0.60**. At q = 0.30, Upgrade has expected value 19 versus 16, an advantage of 3. At q = 0.80, it has expected value 14 versus 16, a disadvantage of 2. The probability has to concern matching *by the specified time*. “They can copy eventually” is not a date.

### Check the horizon before approving

With the same cost 38 but only one period of benefit, even no matching would yield 30 − 38 = −8 against Maintain's 8. Two periods can change the result because the benefit repeats and the cost does not. That conclusion depends on actually receiving the second period's benefits: cancellation, new costs, quality failures, later responses or a different value of time require a new table.

## Write a decision someone else can audit

**Worked memo.** “For the one-period proposal with additional cost 14, active maintenance yields 8. Upgrade yields 16 without a match and 6 with a match. On the declared expected-value rule its advantage is 8 − 10q. Our assumed q of 0.30 favors Upgrade by 5, but is not estimated from the latency trace. Before approval we must justify the response window, cost coverage, value rules and feasibility. We will revisit if those inputs change. The three benchmarks show time improvement; they establish neither causal reciprocity nor customer survival.”

An independent case can use the same method without any service benchmark. An invented puzzle event can keep its current activity for **12 satisfaction credits**, or install a new activity whose net value is **18** if a nearby event does not match and **9** if it does. All values concern one weekend and already include costs; q is the conditional chance of matching before that weekend. Derive the advantage before revealing the answer.

<details>
<summary>Check the independent transfer</summary>

The branch advantages are 18 − 12 = 6 and 9 − 12 = −3. Their weighted average is (1 − q)6 + q(−3) = **6 − 9q**. The tie is q = 2/3. At q = 1/3, the advantage is 3; at q = 1, it is −3. An estimate spanning the threshold does not support a robust expected-value preference. These invented satisfaction credits also assume a shared additive scale; if that assumption fails, the numerical rule needs replacing.

## Reconstruct the audit from memory

Use the Red Queen lens to ask how a relevant counterpart could change the payoff to your improvement. Then check whether the proposed response actually follows, which value it could erode, what the remaining benefit costs, and what you could do instead. The lens supplies questions; the case supplies the answer.
