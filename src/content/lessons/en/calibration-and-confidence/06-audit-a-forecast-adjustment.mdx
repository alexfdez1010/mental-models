A workshop's delivery forecasts look too extreme. The team moves its probabilities towards the middle, and the historical Brier score improves. Has it found a better forecasting rule, or a better description of outcomes it already knows?

This workshop follows a complete log through that decision. The jobs, probabilities and outcomes are **constructed teaching data**. The arithmetic is exact for these records; the small batches do not reveal the population's true probabilities.

## Freeze the question and preserve the log

A fabrication workshop forecasts whether each job will **pass the agreed quality check by Friday at 17:00**. The event is recorded as one if it does, and zero otherwise. A cancellation counts as not meeting this event. An unavailable verification record would remain unresolved rather than be silently deleted or automatically coded as a failure. An unresolved job cannot supply a Brier error yet: report its count against the eligible total, and do not present a resolved-only score as a score for all eligible jobs.

Before each week's processing begins, the audit lists the first **20 jobs already scheduled by Monday at 08:00**. It freezes one forecast per job at that cutoff. Job categories are recorded at intake; later updates remain in the history but are not substituted into this primary comparison. Both methods are evaluated against the same outcomes without changing how jobs are processed.

The first week is the **development batch**, used to propose an adjustment. A later week is reserved for evaluation. All outcomes in the supplied batches are resolved.

| Field fixed before outcomes | Why the audit needs it |
|---|---|
| Eligible IDs and intake category | Prevents dropping difficult or cancelled jobs afterwards |
| Event, quality rule and deadline | Makes the outcome comparable across jobs and methods |
| Forecast timestamp and version | Separates issued probabilities from later revisions |
| Outcome source and unresolved status | Makes verification and missing records visible |
| Evaluation rule and benchmark | Prevents choosing a flattering metric after seeing results |

### When to fix the protocol

Do this before scoring or fitting. Counting repeated updates to the same job as independent cases, changing deadlines after delays or selecting only easy-to-resolve jobs changes the question. If an operational forecast changes which jobs receive attention, its evaluation may also need to account for that changed process.

### Define the proposed adjustment

A simple candidate maps the original lower probability to **0.40** and the original higher probability to **0.60**, matching the observed development frequencies. Save these fitted values in a **replay column**. They were chosen after these outcomes and were not the forecasts originally issued.

The candidate's perfect agreement with this small table is a property of the fit. It does not establish exact future calibration. One changed result in a group would move its observed frequency by **one tenth**, or **ten percentage points**.

## Compare scores without confusing the forecast attributes

Use the course's binary **mean Brier score**, the mean of $(p-o)^2$, with lower values better. This convention scores one event probability, rather than summing errors over both binary outcome categories.

For a classification hit rate only, declare **yes at or above 0.50, no below it**. This threshold is a comparison convention here, not a universal decision rule for unequal costs. The original and adjusted rules call no in the lower group and yes in the higher one.

The original score is fully reproducible from the ledger:

| Original group | Squared error for events | Squared error for non-events | Group error sum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower | 4 × (0.20 − 1)² = 2.56 | 6 × (0.20 − 0)² = 0.24 | 2.80 |
| Higher | 6 × (0.80 − 1)² = 0.24 | 4 × (0.80 − 0)² = 2.56 | 2.80 |

Thus **original Brier = (2.80 + 2.80) ÷ 20 = 0.28**. For the fitted replay:

- Lower group error sum: **4 × 0.60² + 6 × 0.40² = 1.44 + 0.96 = 2.40**
- Higher group error sum: **6 × 0.40² + 4 × 0.60² = 0.96 + 1.44 = 2.40**
- Fitted-replay mean Brier: **(2.40 + 2.40) ÷ 20 = 0.24**

A constant **0.50 benchmark** has a squared error of **0.25** on every job. Freeze that benchmark before evaluating the later batch too.

| Development comparison | Mean Brier | Classification hits | What the comparison establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original issued rule | 0.28 | 12 ÷ 20 = 60% | Performance of the probabilities actually recorded |
| Adjusted fitted replay | 0.24 | 12 ÷ 20 = 60% | Better fit on the data used to choose the adjustment |
| Constant benchmark | 0.25 | 10 ÷ 20 = 50% | Reference performance on the same cases and convention |

### Reliability, resolution and uncertainty

The binary Brier decomposition separates **three terms**. For each forecast-value group, let $n_k$ be its count, $p_k$ its constant probability and $f_k$ its observed event fraction. Let $N$ be the full count and $\bar o$ the overall event fraction. Then:

$$
UNC=\bar o(1-\bar o)
$$

$$
RES=\sum_k\frac{n_k}{N}(f_k-\bar o)^2
$$

$$
REL=\sum_k\frac{n_k}{N}(p_k-f_k)^2
$$

$$
BS=UNC-RES+REL
$$

**UNC**, outcome uncertainty, describes the batch's binary outcome variation. **RES**, resolution, measures how the observed event frequencies in the forecast groups differ from the overall frequency. **REL** is a reliability penalty: squared calibration gaps, weighted by the same case counts. A smaller reliability penalty is better; greater resolution reduces the Brier score when the other terms are fixed.

For the original development rule:

- **UNC = 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25**
- **RES = ½ × (0.40 − 0.50)² + ½ × (0.60 − 0.50)² = 0.01**
- **REL = ½ × (0.20 − 0.40)² + ½ × (0.80 − 0.60)² = 0.04**
- **BS = 0.25 − 0.01 + 0.04 = 0.28**

The adjusted replay retains the **same groups and weights**, so its resolution remains **0.01**. Its reliability penalty becomes zero, giving **0.25 − 0.01 = 0.24**. The constant benchmark has one forecast-value group, hence zero resolution; its probability matches this batch's overall frequency, so its reliability penalty is also zero.

This identity is exact here because each group contains a **constant forecast probability**, every outcome is available and the same jobs receive the same weights. Coarsening unequal forecast values into broad bins does not automatically give this exact decomposition of their individual scores. The empirical terms are also not exact population attributes: finite-sample estimation can be biased. [Ferro and Fricker's original analysis](https://empslocal.ex.ac.uk/people/staff/ferro/Publications/ferro-fricker2012preprint.pdf)

**Sharpness** concerns the concentration of the predictive distribution, a property of forecasts alone. For binary events, probabilities nearer the endpoints are sharper. Our original rule is sharper than the fitted replay, yet both have the same empirical resolution because their group membership and outcomes are unchanged. Making a probability more extreme does not by itself create resolution. [Gneiting, Balabdaoui and Raftery on calibration and sharpness](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jrssb.pdf)

Lock the **adjustment map, original rule, benchmark, eligibility, outcome and primary score** before the later week. Use mean Brier as the primary probability score, with hit rate and grouped calibration as diagnostics. The later ledger is complete:

| Intake group | IDs meeting the event | IDs not meeting it | Events | Non-events | Total | Original probability | Frozen adjusted probability | Observed frequency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lower | L01–L02 | L03–L10 | 2 | 8 | 10 | 0.20 | 0.40 | 0.20 |
| Higher | L11–L18 | L19–L20 | 8 | 2 | 10 | 0.80 | 0.60 | 0.80 |

Original group sums are **2 × 0.80² + 8 × 0.20² = 1.60** in the lower group and **8 × 0.20² + 2 × 0.80² = 1.60** in the higher one. Thus **original mean Brier = 3.20 ÷ 20 = 0.16**.

Adjusted group sums are **2 × 0.60² + 8 × 0.40² = 2.00** and **8 × 0.40² + 2 × 0.60² = 2.00**. Thus **adjusted mean Brier = 4.00 ÷ 20 = 0.20**. The benchmark remains **0.25**. Original and adjusted calls both achieve **16 ÷ 20 = 80%** classification hits.

| Later comparison | Mean Brier | Reliability penalty | Resolution | Outcome uncertainty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Original | 0.16 | 0 | 0.09 | 0.25 |
| Frozen adjustment | 0.20 | 0.04 | 0.09 | 0.25 |
| Frozen constant benchmark | 0.25 | 0 | 0 | 0.25 |

For example, later resolution is **½ × (0.20 − 0.50)² + ½ × (0.80 − 0.50)² = 0.09**. The adjusted rule again preserves groups, but its probabilities now miss their observed frequencies. Its paired mean Brier difference, **adjusted minus original**, is **0.20 − 0.16 = +0.04**: worse on this batch.

## Make a review decision without inventing precision

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="The fitted improvement reverses in the later batch. What conclusion is justified now?"
  options={[
    { text: 'The adjustment did worse on these later cases; broader performance remains uncertain.', correct: true },
    { text: 'The original rule now has proven population calibration for every future case.' },
    { text: 'The later batch should be deleted because it disagrees with the development fit.' }
  ]}
  explanation="Keep the later result. It fails to confirm the proposed improvement, while still being a small observed comparison rather than a population guarantee."
/>

The team should **not present the candidate as a validated improvement** on this record. Retain both batches, report the adverse later comparison and keep the original as the reference while collecting a predeclared next evaluation. If it proposes another adjustment, give it a new version and evaluate it on subsequent cases.

This decision does not require declaring either rule permanently correct. The change in grouped frequencies might reflect chance, changed case difficulty or a changed process. Jobs affected by the same disruption are not necessarily independent observations. More recorded rows do not automatically mean equally much independent information.

| Before the next evaluation | What to declare or inspect |
|---|---|
| Candidate and reference versions | Freeze the mapping and comparison before new outcomes |
| Shared eligible cases | Preserve cancellations, failures and unresolved verification records |
| Primary score and diagnostic grouping | Avoid choosing whichever metric happens to favour the candidate |
| Case mix and common disruptions | Check whether later jobs address the same forecasting task |
| Evidence needed for adoption | State the practical improvement required and how uncertainty will be assessed |

A real adoption rule needs a meaningful improvement target and an uncertainty method suitable for the number and dependence of cases. This toy record does not supply a universal sample-size cutoff or a confidence interval. Its grouped frequencies move in coarse steps, even though probabilities can be written with many decimal places.

A separate complete batch has **ten forecasts**, of which **eight events occur and two do not**. Rule Cedar assigns **0.70** to every case; rule Birch assigns **0.50**. Both were logged before the outcomes. Keep the same classification convention as before.

| Rule | Error on events | Error on non-events | Total squared error | Mean Brier | Classification hits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cedar | 8 × 0.30² = 0.72 | 2 × 0.70² = 0.98 | 1.70 | 1.70 ÷ 10 = 0.17 | 8 ÷ 10 = 80% |
| Birch | 8 × 0.50² = 2.00 | 2 × 0.50² = 0.50 | 2.50 | 2.50 ÷ 10 = 0.25 | 8 ÷ 10 = 80% |

Both rules have **zero resolution**, because each has only one forecast-value group. Cedar has the smaller empirical calibration gap and mean Brier in this batch. Birch's constant midpoint is not calibrated to the observed event fraction here. Neither result establishes an exact population event probability from these few cases.
