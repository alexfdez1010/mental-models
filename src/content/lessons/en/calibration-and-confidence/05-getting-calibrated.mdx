You've spent four lessons learning what calibration *is*: confidence that matches
hit rate, read off a reliability curve, undermined by overconfidence and
overprecision, scored by the Brier score, and made honest with ranges instead of
points. This lesson turns those ideas into a practice routine. It answers the only
question that finally matters: **how do you actually become calibrated** — and why
that turns out to be a learnable skill rather than a personality you were born
with (or without).

A constant base-rate forecast can match the overall event frequency without separating cases. The aim is to make useful distinctions while keeping the probabilities consistent with outcomes. That requires distinguishing calibration, resolution and sharpness.

## Calibration is trainable

Here is the genuinely hopeful claim at the heart of this course: **calibration is a
skill you can drill, not a trait you're stuck with.** You can't easily train "be
smarter" or "have better taste." But you *can* train "make your 80% mean 80%,"
because — uniquely among thinking virtues — it's **measurable**. You can see the
gap between your confidence and your accuracy as a number, and anything you can
measure, you can practice closing.

The evidence is strong and comes from the most-measured predictors alive.
**Weather forecasters** are famously well calibrated: when they say "70% chance of
rain," it rains on close to 70% of those days. So are expert bridge players and
seasoned bookmakers. What do these groups share? Not humility, not genius — a
**tight feedback loop**: many predictions, each with an explicit probability,
each promptly scored by reality. Scored feedback makes systematic discrepancies
visible. Whether a particular adjustment improves later forecasts still needs
evaluation; access to feedback does not guarantee success.

That gives us the core training loop, the spine of everything below:

1. **Predict** — commit to an explicit probability *before* the outcome.
2. **Record** — write it down so memory can't quietly edit it later.
3. **Score** — when the outcome lands, grade it (Brier score, reliability curve).
4. **Review** — look at the pattern, especially your confident misses.
5. **Test an adjustment** — specify a candidate change, then evaluate it on later cases.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The whole lesson in one line">
Calibration improves the same way a free throw does: **many reps, honest scoring,
and feedback you actually look at.** The rest of this lesson is just five concrete
ways to build that loop into how you think.
</Callout>

## Practice 1 — Track your predictions

Memory is a liar, and it lies in a specific direction. After you learn an outcome,
your brain quietly rewrites how sure you *were* — "I knew it all along." That's
**hindsight bias**, and it's poison for calibration: if you misremember your fuzzy
60% hunches as confident 90% calls (or your wrong 90% calls as cautious maybes),
you can never see your real track record, so you can never fix it.

The fix is almost insultingly simple: **write your predictions down, with a
probability and a date, before the outcome is known.** Keep a **prediction
journal** (or forecasting log) — a running list the future can't edit. Once it's on
paper, hindsight has nothing to grab. The number you wrote is the number you wrote.

A few entries from a real-looking log:

| Date | Prediction | Confidence | Resolved | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan 3 | Project ships by end of Q1 | 80% | Mar 31 | Missed (shipped Apr 9) |
| Jan 12 | Candidate A accepts our offer | 65% | Jan 20 | Hit |
| Feb 1 | This feature lifts signups | 55% | Mar 1 | Missed |
| Feb 4 | Rent on our lease goes up at renewal | 90% | May 1 | Hit |

A worked read of that log: of the predictions made at "high confidence" (80–90%),
one of two resolved correctly so far. Too few to judge anything yet — and that's
the point of the journal. You don't trust four predictions; you accumulate
*dozens*, then the pattern (your 80%s only come true 65% of the time, say) becomes
undeniable in a way no amount of "I'm usually right" self-talk can fake.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The pitfall: predictions you can't grade">
A prediction journal only works if each entry is **resolvable** — a clear outcome
and a clear date. "The economy will struggle" can never be scored (struggle how?
by when?). "GDP growth is below 1% this calendar year" can. Vague predictions feel
safe precisely because they dodge the grade — which is exactly why they teach you
nothing.
</Callout>

### When to use it

Start a journal for any domain where you make repeated, checkable calls: project
deadlines, hiring, sales forecasts, sports, your own "this'll take an hour"
estimates. The mundane, high-volume predictions are the *best* training data,
because they resolve quickly and often — fast feedback beats important-but-rare.

## Practice 2 — Give ranges and explicit probabilities

Two reflexes do enormous work, and you already met both. From *Thinking in Ranges*:
**record a range alongside a point estimate.** A point estimate ("revenue will be
\$2M") can be tracked and scored, but does not express forecast uncertainty. A
prediction range with declared **90% coverage** ("\$1.4M–\$2.7M") makes a further
check possible: how often do comparable later outcomes fall inside it? Undercoverage
can suggest intervals are too narrow for that task; overcoverage can suggest they
are wider than needed. Finite samples, dependence and changing conditions affect
both interpretations. Neither pattern reveals the forecaster's intention.

From *Scoring Your Beliefs*: **attach an explicit probability** to every yes/no
call, so it becomes scorable. "Probably" is not a forecast — one person's "probably"
is 55%, another's is 90%. A number is unambiguous *and* gradeable. Vague words are
comfortable exactly because they can never be proven wrong; that comfort is the
enemy of getting better.

### When to use it

Default to ranges for any quantity (time, money, counts) and to explicit
percentages for any yes/no event. The friction you feel writing "90% sure it's
between X and Y" instead of a confident single number makes your uncertainty
explicit. Checking later coverage is how you assess whether the range is calibrated.

## Practice 3 — Calibration training and feedback

A journal of unscored predictions is a diary, not a gym. The rep that actually
builds the muscle is **scoring a batch and looking at the result.** This is
**calibration training**: answer a set of questions with explicit probabilities,
grade them, and plot where your confidence and your accuracy diverge.

The drill, concretely:

1. Take a batch of true/false claims (or resolved predictions from your journal).
2. For each, state your probability that it's true.
3. Score the batch with the **Brier score** from *Scoring Your Beliefs* — the mean
   squared distance between your probabilities and what actually happened. Lower is
   better; honest reporting minimizes its expectation under your belief.
4. Plot your **reliability curve** from *What Calibration Means*: group your answers
   by stated confidence, and for each bucket compare "how sure you said you were" to
   "how often you were actually right." Points above the diagonal mean you were
   underconfident in that bucket; points below mean overconfident.
5. Read the gap, specify a candidate adjustment and **evaluate it on later cases**.

A gap in a probability group can suggest a candidate adjustment, but the observed frequency is uncertain. Preserve the original forecasts, specify the adjustment and assess its performance on later cases not used to choose it. Repeatedly changing the same historical probabilities until their curve matches the outcomes measures fit, not independent improvement.

<Callout variant="info" title="You already have the gym">
The calibration lab can help you practise recording probabilities and reading feedback. For an independent assessment, use fresh questions or later forecasts whose outcomes were not used to choose your answers. A better score on remembered claims does not by itself demonstrate improved calibration on new questions.
</Callout>

### When to use it

Choose a review schedule that fits how quickly comparable outcomes become available.
An individual miss can be scored, but does not by itself establish miscalibration.
Across batches, inspect the case mix, group counts and shared disruptions as well as
the pattern. A larger record need not contain proportionally more independent evidence.

## Practice 4 — Post-mortem your scores

Scoring shows the error on the recorded cases; a **post-mortem** investigates possible
reasons. Keep a **decision journal**: alongside each forecast, record the evidence and
reasoning available at the time. Review confident hits and misses. A surprising miss
is worth investigating, but does not by itself prove the model or probability was wrong.

The trap lurking here is **outcome bias** — judging a decision by how it turned out
rather than by whether it was sound given what you knew. As you learned back in
*Thinking in Probabilities*: **a good decision can have a bad outcome, and a bad
decision can get lucky.** Losing after forecasting a high probability does not establish
that the forecast was poor. Nor does the probability alone establish a good decision:
actions also depend on costs and benefits. Review what the process used, while keeping
the realised outcome in the record for probability evaluation.

A hypothetical example: you said "85% this hire works out," and six months later it
didn't. Two possible findings from the process review:

- **No identifiable information failure:** the candidate looked strong on the recorded
  evidence; an unanticipated family emergency later prevented them from continuing.
  This does not establish that the original probability was correct, or too high.
  Retain the miss and assess comparable forecasts instead of mechanically raising or
  lowering the next candidate's probability.
- **Relevant evidence was ignored:** you discounted two lukewarm references after
  becoming attached to the résumé. Repair that evidence-review process. The omission
  is a reason to investigate the forecast, not a calculation proving that its
  probability should have been a particular lower value.

Process quality, honest reporting and calibration are different questions. Assess
calibration with probabilities and event frequencies over comparable cases, retaining
uncertainty. A flawed rationale or a single bad outcome cannot establish it alone.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The pitfall: only reviewing the misses">
It's tempting to post-mortem disasters and ignore wins. But a lucky win from a bad
decision is just as misleading as an unlucky loss from a good one — it teaches you a
sloppy process "works." Review confident **hits** too, and ask the same question:
was I right for the right reasons, or right by luck?
</Callout>

### When to use it

Post-mortem after any prediction resolves where the stakes or the surprise were
high — and always after a confident miss. The single most valuable habit is the
question *"was my probability honest given what I knew then?"*, asked with the
outcome deliberately set aside.

## Practice 5 — Start from base rates (the outside view)

Before you reach for an inside-view story about why *this* case is special, anchor
on the **base rate**: how often does this kind of thing happen in general? This is
the **outside view** (or **reference-class forecasting**) — find the reference class
your situation belongs to, start from its base rate, *then* adjust for specifics.

A hypothetical example: "How likely is our software project to ship within three
months?" An inside-view story proposes **85%** because the team is strong. A declared
historical reference contains **100 comparable projects**, of which **30** met their
original deadline. Its observed frequency is **30 ÷ 100 = 30%**. This is a starting
point to investigate, not a universal fact about software projects.

Before transferring that frequency, check how the projects were selected, whether
the outcome and horizon match, and whether the process has changed. A proposal of
**45%** for the new project would need evidence justifying the departure; that number
does not become calibrated merely because it lies between the story and the base rate.

A suitable base rate can inform a **prior** in Bayesian updating. It does not guarantee
a calibrated prior: historical frequencies have uncertainty, and the reference class
may poorly represent the new case. Case-specific evidence and its likelihood model
also need scrutiny. Keep subsequent forecasts and outcomes to evaluate the result.

### When to use it

Reach for the outside view first whenever you're forecasting a *kind* of event that
has happened many times before (projects, hires, launches, treatments). It's most
powerful exactly when you feel most special — "this time is different" is usually
the inside view talking, and the base rate is usually right.

## What the superforecasters do

You don't have to take all this on faith. The most rigorous evidence comes from
political scientist **Philip Tetlock** and the **Good Judgment Project** — a
multi-year forecasting tournament in which thousands of ordinary volunteers
predicted real-world geopolitical and economic events, with every forecast scored
against what actually happened. A subset of these volunteers — dubbed
**superforecasters** — were consistently, measurably better than the crowd, and in
some comparisons outperformed subject-matter experts with access to classified
information.

The striking finding, and the reason it belongs in *this* lesson: they weren't
geniuses or insiders. They were ordinary people with **better habits** — the exact
habits you've just read. Drawn from that research, superforecasters tend to:

- **Break big questions into smaller, answerable pieces** instead of reacting to the
  whole.
- **Start from base rates** — the outside view — before layering in specifics.
- **Update in small increments** as evidence trickles in, rather than lurching from
  certain to certain (pure Bayesian updating, done gently).
- **Think in fine-grained probabilities** — they meaningfully distinguish 63% from
  70%, where most of us round everything to "likely," "maybe," or "no chance."
- **Keep score and post-mortem** — they revisit their misses and ask what their
  model got wrong.

<Callout variant="info" title="No magic, no exact numbers">
The honest version of this story is qualitative. Superforecasting is well-supported
research showing that **trainable habits**, not innate brilliance, drive good
forecasting — and that calibration improves with practice and feedback. It is *not*
a claim that anyone can predict anything, or a specific guaranteed accuracy. The
defensible takeaway is the hopeful one: the loop works, for ordinary people.
</Callout>

<details>
<summary>Why does fine-grained probability matter so much?</summary>

Because rounding throws away real information. If you collapse everything into
"likely / maybe / unlikely," you can't tell a strong 70% from a marginal 55% — and a
scoring rule like the Brier score rewards you for the difference. Superforecasters
treat the gap between 63% and 70% as meaningful because, across hundreds of
forecasts, those small distinctions add up to a measurably better track record. It's
the difference between a thermometer with one mark and one with a hundred.
</details>

## Calibration, resolution and sharpness

**Calibration** compares issued probabilities with event frequencies in their forecast groups. **Resolution** measures differences in those outcome frequencies across the groups, relative to the overall frequency. **Sharpness** concerns concentration of the forecast distributions themselves. For a binary event, probabilities nearer the endpoints are sharper; that alone does not make them better calibrated or more informative about outcomes.

Consider a constructed collection of **100 forecasts**, all issued before outcomes. Forecaster P assigns **0.50** to every case. Forecaster Q uses information available beforehand to assign the probabilities in this complete table:

| Q's probability | Cases | Events | Non-events | Observed event frequency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 50 | 5 | 45 | 5 ÷ 50 = 0.10 |
| 0.90 | 50 | 45 | 5 | 45 ÷ 50 = 0.90 |

There are **50 events among 100 cases**, so P matches the overall frequency and has zero empirical resolution. Q matches each group frequency and separates groups whose outcomes occur at different rates. Q's mean Brier is **(5 × 0.90² + 45 × 0.10² + 45 × 0.10² + 5 × 0.90²) ÷ 100 = 0.09**, compared with P's **0.25**.

Both are empirically calibrated in this collection, but Q has the better probability score and greater resolution. P remains a useful constant benchmark; it supplies no case-specific separation. These observed matches do not establish exact calibration in the population.

### When to use the distinction

Inspect both forecasts and outcomes. More extreme probabilities change sharpness; they do not automatically improve resolution. A constant reference can be useful without separating cases. Judge proposed adjustments with a proper score, grouped calibration and later evaluation under a clear protocol. The full Brier decomposition also includes outcome uncertainty. [Original decomposition analysis](https://empslocal.ex.ac.uk/people/staff/ferro/Publications/ferro-fricker2012preprint.pdf)

## Putting it together: where calibration sits in the latticework

Step back and notice what a **calibrated error bar** really is: the honest input
that the rest of your thinking quietly depends on. **Margin of safety** is only as
good as the worst case you're willing to take seriously — set your range too narrow
(overprecision) and the margin is a comforting fiction. **Expected value** weights
possible outcomes by their probabilities; unreliable probability inputs can distort
the result despite correct arithmetic. **Bayesian updating** starts
from a prior and moves with evidence. A relevant base rate can inform that prior,
while an unsuitable likelihood model can still undermine the update. Calibration
checks an important input to these models; it does not guarantee every model or
decision built on it. Use probability scores, outcome records and process review
for their different jobs.

## Recap

These are the practice tools for the next workshop. The arc, in one breath: calibration is
confidence that matches reality (Lesson 1), wrecked by overconfidence and
overprecision (Lesson 2), measured by proper scoring (Lesson 3), expressed as honest
ranges (Lesson 4), and **built through deliberate practice** (this one) — track your
predictions, give ranges and explicit probabilities, run scored calibration
batches, post-mortem your hits and misses, and start from base rates. The
superforecasting research motivates this practice; evaluate whether your own changes
improve later forecasts. And the expert insight is
that humility isn't enough: aim to be **calibrated and informative about outcomes**.
