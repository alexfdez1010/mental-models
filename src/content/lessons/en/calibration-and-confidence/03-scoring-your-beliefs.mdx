You now know what calibration *is* — the match between your stated confidence and
your hit rate — and you've seen the two ways it breaks: overestimation and
overprecision. But "your 80%s should come true 80% of the time" is still a vibe
until you can attach a single, gradeable number to a forecast. This lesson is where
calibration grows teeth. We're going to take a probability — a slippery, deniable
thing — and turn it into a score the future can compute, settle, and rank.

The trick is choosing the *right* score. A naive grading scheme can be gamed: you'd
quickly learn to game it instead of getting calibrated. The scores in this lesson
are designed so that reporting your genuine probability optimizes your **expected**
score. A particular outcome can still reward a different report. The theorem concerns
expectation under your belief; it does not promise that an honest belief is accurate.

## Why score a belief at all

A probability is a **promise**. When you say "70% this ships on time," you're
promising that, across all the times you say 70%, the thing happens about seven
times in ten. The problem with promises is that any single one is easy to wriggle
out of. The project slips, you shrug: "Well, I did say *only* 70%." It rained on
your "20% chance of rain" day; you mutter that 20% isn't zero. Each excuse is
locally valid. Memory does the rest — it files your hits as "I knew it" and your
misses as "bad luck."

A **scoring rule** is the accountant that never forgets. It takes each forecast and
its outcome, hands back a number, and adds it to the pile. One forecast tells you
almost nothing. A recorded collection makes your errors inspectable without relying on flattering
memory. Its size, selection and dependence still matter when drawing wider conclusions.

The hard part is design. We want a rule that gives no expected advantage to
misreporting a belief. That incentive supports honest records; improving the beliefs
themselves also takes relevant evidence and evaluation on subsequent outcomes.

### When to use it

Reach for an explicit scoring rule the moment you're making **repeated** probability
judgments you actually want to improve — forecasting deadlines, diagnoses, market
moves, sports, geopolitics. For a single one-off bet, a score is overkill; the magic
is statistical and only shows up over many calls. If you forecast the same *kind* of
thing again and again, score it.

## The Brier score

Here's the centerpiece. The **Brier score** is the simplest honest grade for a
binary forecast, and it's nothing more than *squared error*.

You assign a probability $p$ to an event. The outcome $o$ is $1$ if the event
happens and $0$ if it doesn't. The Brier score for that single forecast is the
squared distance between what you said and what happened:

$$\text{Brier} = (p - o)^2$$

Over $N$ forecasts you just average them:

$$BS = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i - o_i)^2$$

The range runs from $0$ to $1$, and **lower is better**. A score of $0$ means you
said $1.0$ to everything that happened and $0.0$ to everything that didn't —
flawless prophecy. A score of $1$ means you confidently called every single one
backwards. The "I have no idea" baseline of saying $0.5$ to everything gives
$(0.5 - o)^2 = 0.25$ no matter what. This is a useful benchmark, not a universal threshold for forecasting skill. Compare methods on the same cases with an appropriate reference.

The analogy: think of $p$ as where you throw a dart and $o$ as where the bullseye
turns out to be — at $0$ or at $1$. Brier scores the **squared miss distance**.
Land close to the truth, tiny penalty. Land far, the squaring makes the penalty
balloon. Confidence is wonderful when you're right and expensive when you're wrong,
and the squaring is exactly what makes "expensive" hurt.

Watch what squaring does to a bold call. Suppose you say $p = 0.95$:

- If the event **happens** ($o = 1$): $(0.95 - 1)^2 = (-0.05)^2 = 0.0025$. Almost
  free. You were brave and right.
- If the event **doesn't** ($o = 0$): $(0.95 - 0)^2 = 0.9025$. A near-maximal
  penalty. You were brave and wrong, and the rule made you pay for it.

That asymmetry — $0.0025$ for the brave hit versus $0.9025$ for the brave miss — is
the entire personality of the Brier score. It dares you to be confident, then
punishes confidence that wasn't earned.

### A fully worked Brier example

Let's grade a whole batch. Suppose over a season you made five forecasts. Here's
each one with its probability $p$, the eventual outcome $o$, and the squared error
$(p - o)^2$.

| # | Forecast | $p$ | Outcome $o$ | $(p - o)^2$ |
|---|----------|-----|-------------|-------------|
| 1 | "Project ships on time" | 0.90 | 1 (it shipped) | $(0.90 - 1)^2 = 0.0100$ |
| 2 | "Candidate accepts offer" | 0.70 | 1 (accepted) | $(0.70 - 1)^2 = 0.0900$ |
| 3 | "Rain tomorrow" | 0.30 | 0 (stayed dry) | $(0.30 - 0)^2 = 0.0900$ |
| 4 | "Rival product launches Q3" | 0.60 | 0 (didn't) | $(0.60 - 0)^2 = 0.3600$ |
| 5 | "Server survives the spike" | 0.95 | 1 (survived) | $(0.95 - 1)^2 = 0.0025$ |

Add the squared errors: $0.0100 + 0.0900 + 0.0900 + 0.3600 + 0.0025 = 0.5525$.
Divide by $N = 5$:

$$BS = \frac{0.5525}{5} = 0.1105$$

A Brier score of $0.1105$. That's comfortably below the $0.25$ "coin-flip" baseline,
so it beats that benchmark on this small batch; that does not establish stable forecasting skill. Notice forecast #4 — the confident-ish $0.60$ on
something that didn't happen — did most of the damage, contributing $0.36$ of the
$0.5525$. And forecast #5, the brave $0.95$ that came true, barely cost anything. The
score is telling a story: be confident where you're right, hedge where you're unsure,
and never be loud-and-wrong.

<Callout variant="tip" title="That number from the calibration lab">
Remember the **Brier score** the calibration lab handed you back in lesson 1, after

</Callout>

### Pitfall: lower is better, and the number isn't a percentage

Two traps. First, **lower is better** — it's a penalty, an error, not a grade out of
100. A "Brier score of 0.9" is a disaster, not an A. People who are used to "bigger
is better" scores read it exactly backwards. Second, **the Brier score is not your
hit rate or your accuracy in percent.** A $0.1105$ doesn't mean "11% right" or "89%
right." It's mean squared error in probability space. The only anchors you need:
$0$ is perfect, $1$ is perfectly wrong, and $0.25$ is the do-nothing baseline of
always guessing $0.5$.

## Proper and strictly proper scoring: honesty in expectation

A **proper scoring rule** makes reporting your genuine probability an optimal choice
for expected score, evaluated under that belief. Other reports may tie. A **strictly
proper** rule makes that optimum unique: any different report has worse expected score.
The direction matters: minimize an expected loss or maximize an expected reward.

The binary Brier score is **strictly proper**. If you believe a probability, shading
it to sound bolder or safer increases expected squared error under that belief.
This is an incentive to report honestly, not a test that your belief is the true
probability of the world. [Primary definitions](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jrssb.pdf)

Suppose you genuinely believe an event has a $70\%$ chance: your honest probability is
$0.70$. The temptation is to *report* something bolder — say $0.90$ — because confident
people sound smarter. Does it help your expected Brier score?

Your expected Brier score for reporting some number $r$, evaluated under your belief
$0.70$, is a weighted average over the two ways the world can go. With probability
$0.70$ the event happens ($o = 1$) and you score $(r - 1)^2$; with probability $0.30$
it doesn't ($o = 0$) and you score $(r - 0)^2$:

$$E[\text{Brier}] = 0.70\,(r - 1)^2 + 0.30\,(r - 0)^2$$

Compare three reports:

| Report $r$ | $0.70\,(r-1)^2$ | $0.30\,(r-0)^2$ | Expected Brier |
|-----------|------------------|------------------|----------------|
| Honest: $r = 0.70$ | $0.70\,(0.09) = 0.0630$ | $0.30\,(0.49) = 0.1470$ | $\mathbf{0.2100}$ |
| Bluff: $r = 0.90$ | $0.70\,(0.01) = 0.0070$ | $0.30\,(0.81) = 0.2430$ | $0.2500$ |
| Timid: $r = 0.50$ | $0.70\,(0.25) = 0.1750$ | $0.30\,(0.25) = 0.0750$ | $0.2500$ |

Reporting your honest $0.70$ gives the **lowest** expected Brier ($0.21$). Puffing up
to $0.90$ to look confident *raises* it to $0.25$. Shrinking to a timid $0.50$ also
raises it to $0.25$. Every lie about your belief — in either direction — costs you, on
average under that belief. The minimum sits exactly at your stated belief.

That's not a coincidence of the numbers $0.70$ and $0.90$. A one-line calculus gesture
shows it always holds: treat the expected score $f(r) = q\,(r-1)^2 + (1-q)\,(r-0)^2$ as
a function of your report $r$, where $q$ is your true belief. Take the derivative,
$f'(r) = 2q(r-1) + 2(1-q)r = 2(r - q)$, and set it to zero: $r = q$. The expected
score is a quadratic with positive curvature, so that is its unique minimum on the
probability range. Strict propriety concerns this expectation, not the winner of
every finite batch.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Honest reporting and calibration are separate">
A strictly proper rule removes an expected reward for bluffing. It does not repair a
mistaken belief automatically: an honestly reported probability can still be poorly
calibrated. Keep the original reports, inspect their outcomes and evaluate any proposed
adjustment on later cases. One realised outcome can favour a different report.
</Callout>

## The log score: brutal on overconfidence

The Brier score is the friendly one. Its meaner, more famous cousin is the
**logarithmic scoring rule** — the **log score** — and it has a special talent for
destroying overconfidence.

The idea is even simpler to state. Look at what *actually happened*, find the
probability you assigned to *that* outcome, and take its logarithm:

$$\text{log score} = \log(p_{\text{outcome}})$$

If the event happened, $p_{\text{outcome}}$ is the $p$ you gave the event; if it
didn't, it's $1 - p$. Because the log of a number below $1$ is negative, the log
score is always $\le 0$, and — convention warning — **higher (closer to zero) is
better**. (You'll often see it written with a minus sign as a *loss*, $-\log p$,
where lower is better; same content, flipped sign.) Assigning a high probability to
what actually happened earns a score near $0$; assigning a tiny probability to what
actually happened drags the score toward $-\infty$.

The structural difference is boundedness, not an absolute comparison of penalty
amounts across two scales. Brier's worst possible single penalty is bounded at $1$.
The log reward has **no floor**; its corresponding loss has no ceiling. The closer you push your probability toward $0$ on
something that then happens, the worse your score, with no limit:

The table uses **natural logarithms**, with displayed values rounded.

| You said it would happen with probability... | ...and it happened. Brier $(p-1)^2$ | Log score $\log(p)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $p = 0.5$ | $0.25$ | $\log(0.5) = -0.69$ |
| $p = 0.1$ | $0.81$ | $\log(0.1) = -2.30$ |
| $p = 0.01$ | $0.98$ | $\log(0.01) = -4.61$ |
| $p = 0.001$ | $0.998$ | $\log(0.001) = -6.91$ |
| $p = 0$ | $1.00$ | $\log(0) = -\infty$ |

Watch the two columns diverge. As your probability for the true outcome shrinks from
$0.1$ to $0.01$ to $0.001$, Brier crawls from $0.81$ toward its ceiling of $1$ — it's
running out of room to punish you. The log score, meanwhile, marches off a cliff:
$-2.30$, $-4.61$, $-6.91$, and then, at $p = 0$, straight to negative infinity. Say
something is **impossible** and watch it happen, and the log score declares your
performance infinitely bad. The lesson writes itself: **never say never.** Never
assign $0$ (or $1$) to anything that isn't a logical certainty, because reality only
needs to embarrass you once.

The quantity $-\log p$ measures **surprise**: an outcome assigned a smaller probability
carries more surprise. With natural logarithms its unit is a **nat**; base-two
logarithms measure bits. The binary log rule is also strictly proper, with expected
reward maximized by honest reporting. Its scale and sign differ from Brier's.

### When to use it

Use the **log score** when its sensitivity to tiny probabilities assigned to realised
outcomes suits the evaluation task. It can be dominated by an unsupported near-zero
probability. Brier provides a bounded squared-error comparison. Choose the rule and
sign convention before inspecting results. Neither score, by itself, represents the
real-world cost of a missed event or determines the best action.

## What a good score actually rewards

A Brier score combines several attributes; its full binary decomposition is **outcome uncertainty minus resolution plus a reliability penalty**. The uncertainty term depends on the event frequency in the evaluated collection. The reliability penalty measures squared gaps between probabilities and observed frequencies. Resolution measures how the observed frequencies differ between forecast-value groups.

A constant probability has no resolution across cases. It can still be a useful base-rate benchmark. Its calibration depends on whether that probability matches the event frequency; a constant midpoint is not automatically calibrated on every collection.

Keep **resolution** separate from **sharpness**. Sharpness is concentration of the predictive distribution, a property of the forecasts alone. For binary events, probabilities nearer the endpoints are sharper. Resolution also uses outcomes: merely pushing probabilities away from the midpoint does not establish useful separation. [Original definitions and finite-sample limits](https://empslocal.ex.ac.uk/people/staff/ferro/Publications/ferro-fricker2012preprint.pdf), [calibration and sharpness](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jrssb.pdf)

The workshop later calculates all three terms for complete groups with constant forecast probabilities. Its arithmetic is exact for those records, while conclusions about population attributes still carry sampling uncertainty. A low score on a small batch is a result to report and investigate, not proof of permanent skill.

## Where this goes next

You can now grade a *probability*. But a huge share of real forecasts aren't yes/no
events — they're **quantities**: how long will this take, how much will it cost, how
many users by December? You can't slap a single $p$ on "the project takes 40 days,"
because there are infinitely many possible answers. The honest tool for that is a
**range** — a 90% interval wide enough to actually contain the truth — and, predictably,
your first instinct will make it far too narrow.

Lesson 4, **Thinking in Ranges**, takes the scoring discipline you just built and aims
it at quantities: how to state an honest 90% interval, why overprecision sabotages your
first attempt every time, and how a well-built range connects back to the margin of
safety. Same goal — confidence that tells the truth — pointed at a harder target.
