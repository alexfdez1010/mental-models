You record an eighty-percent interval, then wait for the outcome. What would count as a good forecast: a narrow range, a hit, or the smallest error score? These questions need different information. This lesson makes the evaluation rule explicit before comparing the forecasts.

The example is constructed: six export jobs, their issued ranges and their resolved elapsed times. No interval is fitted to the outcomes below. You will compare the complete paired record, then try a different collection.

## Say what the interval is about

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"

  explanation="A future observation, an unknown fixed value and a population parameter need different interpretations. A label or one hit cannot supply the construction."
  pretest={true}
/>

A **point forecast** gives one value, such as six hours for the next job. It can be a useful summary and can be scored. An **interval** gives lower and upper bounds, but the bounds need a target and an interpretation.

| Statement | Target and meaning |
|---|---|
| “My belief assigns eighty percent to this range for an unknown completed job’s duration.” | A subjective uncertainty statement about a fixed but unseen value. |
| “This range is issued for the next job’s elapsed time.” | A prediction interval for a future observation; state its intended coverage and how it was constructed. |
| “This sampling rule covers the process mean in ninety percent of repeated samples under the model.” | A confidence-interval coverage statement about a fixed population parameter and random bounds. |

A predictive interval can express a subjective model: “subjective” and “predictive” are not opposites. A Bayesian credible interval instead refers to posterior probability under a specified prior and likelihood. Do not claim that a frequentist confidence interval gives that posterior probability merely because its endpoints are now known. Nor does the visual shape of an error bar tell you what it represents; read its legend.

For the forecast audit, record the job ID, the forecast’s issue time, the elapsed-time definition, the horizon, the bounds and the nominal level. Say whether boundary values count as hits. Here they do: the interval is closed, so an outcome on either bound is inside.

### When to use the distinction

Check the target before interpreting a percentage. Uncertainty about a process mean is not the same quantity as variation in its next observation. The next observation may fall outside an issued prediction interval; the endpoints are not absolute limits.

## Give the two tails a meaning

A **quantile** marks a cumulative-probability level. For a central eighty-percent prediction interval, the intended lower and upper levels are one tenth and nine tenths. “Central” refers to tail probabilities, not equal distances from a point estimate or a shortest-width interval.

Use this separate stipulated probability model to see the accounting. It is not a model fitted to the six-job audit that follows.

| Possible elapsed time, hours | Weight out of ten |
|---:|---:|
| 2 | 1 |
| 4 | 2 |
| 6 | 4 |
| 8 | 2 |
| 10 | 1 |

Choose bounds three and nine. One tenth lies strictly below three; one tenth lies strictly above nine. The remaining eight tenths lie inside. These are model probabilities, not observed frequencies from ten experiments.

Discrete probability needs a boundary convention. Here an admissible quantile report $q$ at level $\tau$ satisfies

$$P(Y<q)\le\tau\le P(Y\le q).$$

There may be several such reports. For this law, every lower bound from two through four is a one-tenth quantile, and every upper bound from eight through ten is a nine-tenths quantile. The chosen interval has exactly eighty-percent mass; the closed interval from two to ten has all the mass. Do not assume that every discrete central interval has exactly its nominal inclusive coverage.

### When to use quantiles

Use them when the forecast contract asks for tail probabilities. A skewed distribution can give unequal distances between a median and its two bounds. Skewness by itself establishes neither fat tails nor a particular project-delay mechanism.

## Score width and outside distance

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="A wider issued interval contains an outcome that a narrower one misses. Which result follows without calculating the declared loss?"
  options={[{"text":"The wider one must have the lower loss."},{"text":"The narrower one must have the lower loss."},{"text":"Both must have equal losses."},{"text":"The hit alone does not determine their loss ranking.","correct":true}]}
  explanation="Width and outside distance both enter this score. You need their quantities and the common nominal level."
  pretest={true}
/>

A hit count ignores width and how far a miss fell outside. A range spanning everything can collect hits without giving much detail. A tiny interval can be informative when correct but miss badly. We will use the **interval score**, with lower values better, for central intervals at the same nominal level.

For nominal coverage $1-\alpha$ with $0<\alpha<1$, finite ordered bounds $l\le u$ and a finite outcome $y$:

$$IS_\alpha(l,u;y)=(u-l)+\frac{2}{\alpha}\max(l-y,0)+\frac{2}{\alpha}\max(y-u,0).$$

At eighty percent, $\alpha=1/5$ and the penalty multiplier is ten. The score is width plus ten times the distance outside the nearest bound. Inside or exactly on a bound, that distance is zero. Scores have elapsed-time units; a penalty of thirty is a loss-score contribution, not thirty hours of extra work or observed delay.

For interval $[4,8]$, the width is four. An outcome of six or eight scores four. An outcome of nine is one hour outside and scores $4+10\times1=14$. At eleven, the distance is three and the score is $4+10\times3=34$. Widening can lower an outside penalty while increasing the width term. There is no general instruction to widen every first range.

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  question="Use the worked interval and its eleven-hour outcome. What is the interval score?"
  options={[{"text":"Four, because only width counts."},{"text":"Thirty, because only the penalty counts."},{"text":"Thirty-four, adding width and penalty.","correct":true},{"text":"Fourteen, as for the one-hour miss."}]}
  explanation="Width is 4, outside distance is 3 and the multiplier is 10:4+10×3=34. The score is a loss, so lower is better."
/>

When the predictive law has a finite expected absolute outcome, its corresponding quantile reports minimize the expected score. The score does not validate that model, establish anyone’s motives or guarantee the lowest realized score. In a discrete model, optimal reports can tie. An interval score for two quantiles also does not evaluate every feature of a full predictive distribution.

<details>
<summary>Check the expected-score tie in the stipulated law</summary>

For bounds three and nine in the five-value model, scores are sixteen, six, six, six and sixteen. Weighting them gives

$$\frac{16+2\times6+4\times6+2\times6+16}{10}=8.$$

The intervals $[2,10]$ and $[4,8]$ also have expected score eight. In this example the quantiles are not unique; an expected-score tie is compatible with different inclusive coverages. It does not tell you to report a wider interval for every real task.

For a direct finite proof, write the expected score as $A(l)+B(u)$, where

$$A(l)=-l+\sum_i w_i\max(l-y_i,0),\qquad B(u)=u+\sum_i w_i\max(y_i-u,0).$$

Here $w_i$ are the integer counts in the table. Dividing them by ten gives the probability weights; that denominator cancels the penalty multiplier of ten. On $2\le l\le4$, $A(l)=-2$; below two it is larger, and above four the first two support contributions already give $A(l)\ge2l-10>-2$. On $8\le u\le10$, $B(u)=10$; above ten it is larger, and below eight the last two contributions give $B(u)\ge26-2u>10$. Thus the minimum expected score is eight, attained throughout that rectangle of bounds. This proof concerns this stipulated law, not a unique best real forecasting model.

</details>

### When a score is useful

Declare the score, nominal level, eligible cases and weights before comparing methods. Different loss criteria can rank methods differently. A forecast score is not automatically the operational cost of acting on it.

Three rules issued central eighty-percent intervals for the same six jobs. N used $[4,8]$, M used $[3,10]$, W used $[2,12]$. The elapsed times were four, five, six, seven, nine and eleven hours. They are a fictional complete evaluation panel, not draws claimed to follow the separate five-value law. Every record has equal weight; no rule changes after the outcomes.

| Rule | Total width over six | Total outside penalty | Hits | Total score | Exact mean score |

M has the lowest total and mean score in this panel. W has the most hits. Neither claim implies that M has exact eighty-percent population coverage or that W is necessarily too wide. The inspector keeps issued bounds fixed; selecting another record reveals a different part of the same audit.

Six cases do not fix a population’s coverage. As a separate calculation, suppose six hit indicators are independent and each has true probability $0.8$. All six hit with probability $0.8^6=0.262144$: about twenty-six percent. With probability $0.9$ per independent hit, all six hit with probability $0.9^6=0.531441$. Thus all hits in a small batch can be quite ordinary. These numbers require the stated independence and common-probability assumptions; the panel alone does not establish them.

Record misses and inspect where they occur: horizon, task type, changing conditions and dependence can matter. A persistent pattern on comparable later cases is evidence to investigate, not a license to multiply widths by a miss-count ratio. If you tune a rule using these outcomes, label that development and freeze it before a new evaluation. Keep every eligible case and compare candidates on the same weights.

### When to repeat the evaluation

Repeat it with later comparable outcomes and after relevant process changes. Report the observed count, sample size, score direction and limits. Do not infer professional competence, honest intent or future coverage from one finite table.

A hits three of four; B hits four of four. Those fractions are descriptive. They do not prove the next interval’s probability or a population calibration result. Changing endpoints after seeing these outcomes would create a new fitted rule, not improve the recorded forecast retroactively.

Communicate a forecast with its target, horizon, bounds, nominal interpretation and supporting assumptions. A decision also needs consequences, feasible actions and any service or loss criterion. An eighty-percent interval is not a universal safety buffer or a guarantee that its upper endpoint cannot be exceeded.

### Sources and scope

[Gneiting and Raftery](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf), sections 6.1–6.2, discuss quantile forecasts and the interval score. Their continuous quantile assumptions are not silently imposed on our discrete example; its expected-score ties are checked directly. [Bracher and colleagues](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1008618), section 2.2, explain the width and miss components for central predictive intervals. No empirical health data, forecast fit or intervention result is used here.

[NIST’s confidence-interval interpretation](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda352.htm) and its [prediction discussion](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmd/section5/pmd512.htm) distinguish uncertainty about a parameter from a future observation. All job times, bounds and weights here are constructed. The calculations establish finite score comparisons, not guaranteed coverage or safety.
