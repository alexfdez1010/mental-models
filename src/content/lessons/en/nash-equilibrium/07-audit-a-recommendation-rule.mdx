Two archive teams share a scanner. A device sends each a private message: **Request** or **Defer**. Nobody has to obey. The decision is whether each team can improve by ignoring its message while the other follows.

You already know how to check a payoff cell. This workshop adds a different condition: *what can I infer about the other team after seeing my own recommendation?* The device's random draw is shared, even though the messages are private.

## Specify the game before trusting the advice

This is a constructed, one-encounter game. Each team chooses Request or Defer simultaneously, after receiving its message. A **payoff** is a value in points: higher is better. The teams evaluate uncertain outcomes by their expected payoff, the probability-weighted mean. Points already include every consequence modeled here; there is no further fee, effort cost, future encounter, retaliation or enforcement. These are stipulated preferences, not measured scanner throughput.

Read A's possible switch. Against B's Request, A prefers Defer: zero beats minus six. Against B's Defer, A prefers Request: three beats one. B has the same comparison with its own payoffs. Thus Request/Defer and Defer/Request are the two pure Nash cells; Defer/Defer is not one.

The device is trusted to draw from a **known joint law**, the probabilities of the two messages together. Each team sees only its own message, not the ticket number, the other message or a side channel. Think of two sealed envelopes from one draw: opening yours can reveal something about what is in the other. The limit of the analogy matters: an envelope is advice, not an enforceable assignment.

A **deviation** changes one team's response to what it sees. Throughout the audit, the other team still follows its recommendation. We are testing a specified plan under a specified information structure, not predicting that actual teams will use it.

### When to use this specification

Before evaluating a recommendation system, write down the choices, payoffs, draw law, timing and observations. A missing observation can change the best response even when every payoff stays fixed.

## Joint probabilities are more than matching frequencies

The private rule draws one of four **equally likely labelled tickets**. Two different tickets carry Defer/Defer; they remain distinct copies. This describes a probability law, not a promise that four actual days will contain every ticket once.

| A's message | B's message | Private ticket copies | Independent ticket copies |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Request | Request | 0 | 1 |
| Request | Defer | 1 | 3 |
| Defer | Request | 1 | 3 |
| Defer | Defer | 2 | 9 |
| Total | | 4 | 16 |

A **marginal probability** ignores the other component: add every cell where A receives Request. The private rule gives A Request with probability $1/4$; B's marginal is also $1/4$. The independent rule gives each team Request with probability $4/16=1/4$, but its pair probabilities are the products of those marginals. For example, Request/Request has probability $1/4\times1/4=1/16$, rather than zero.

The common error is to reuse the marginal $1/4$ after receiving an informative message. Before seeing anything, all four copies are possible; after A sees Defer, only three are. Use this filter whenever advice is correlated. Independent advice would not change B's marginal when A opens its envelope.

## Check following against every unilateral switch

The relevant expectation is now conditional on **your message**. When A sees Request, B must have Defer. Following gives A three; switching to Defer gives one. When A sees Defer, the compatible copies have B Request once and B Defer twice:

$$u_F=(0+1+1)/3=2/3,\qquad u_S=(-6+3+3)/3=0.$$

Here $u_F$ means the conditional payoff from following and $u_S$ from switching. The complete audit includes B rather than hiding half the test behind symmetry.

| Team | Own advice | Other Request weight | Other Defer weight | Compatible total | Follow | Switch |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | Request | 0 | 1 | 1 | 3 | 1 |
| A | Defer | 1 | 2 | 3 | 2/3 | 0 |
| B | Request | 0 | 1 | 1 | 3 | 1 |
| B | Defer | 1 | 2 | 3 | 2/3 | 0 |

Following wins every comparison. This joint law is a **correlated equilibrium**: with the law known and only one's own recommendation observed, no team gains by unilaterally changing its response, assuming the other follows. The recommendation is voluntary. Neither a referee nor a penalty has been slipped into the game.

Why are these checks sufficient? With two actions, after each possible message the only alternative is the other action. A plan to ignore, flip or selectively follow messages consists of these same comparisons. Randomizing between following and switching averages their payoffs; it cannot exceed their maximum. Check every team and every positive-probability message. If a message has zero probability, its compatible total is zero and this law supplies no conditional distribution for it; do not manufacture one.

This definition concerns a **law of advice**, not each realized cell viewed with hindsight. Defer/Defer occurs with probability $1/2$ even though it is not a pure Nash cell of the original game. A team told Defer cannot distinguish that case from the one where its partner was told Request. That uncertainty changes the available incentive calculation.

## Change the disclosure and repeat the test

Now keep the payoffs and private rule, but publish the **whole message pair before acting**. If it is Defer/Defer, each team knows the other was told Defer. Following pays one; switching to Request pays three. There is a profitable deviation. The earlier conditional average is no longer the right comparison because the team knows which of its previously compatible cases occurred.

Publication breaks this particular recommendation arrangement. It does not establish that secrecy is generally valuable, that public information always harms coordination or that deception is required. The device's law was public all along; **knowing a distribution and observing its realized draw are different information**.

A different failure can occur without publishing anything. Try a uniform private rule with one ticket for each of the four message pairs. After Request, the other team is equally likely to have Request or Defer. Following yields $(-6+3)/2=-3/2$; switching yields $(0+1)/2=1/2$. Switching gains two points. Random advice alone is no certificate of incentive compatibility.

### When to repeat the audit

Repeat it after a new side channel, an extra message, a disclosed draw index or a payoff change. A certificate for one information policy does not carry over just because the action labels remain the same.

## Separate obedience from average payoff and insurance

Before receiving any message, A's expected payoff under the private rule is:

$$0(-6)+(1/4)3+(1/4)0+(2/4)1=5/4.$$

Under independent recommendations with the same marginals, A instead gets:

$$(-6+9+0+9)/16=3/4.$$

The independent rule also passes its obedience checks here: after either message, the other team still requests with probability $1/4$. Request gives $(-6)/4+3(3/4)=3/4$ and Defer gives $0/4+1(3/4)=3/4$. This is the original game's independent mixed Nash profile, with each team requesting with probability $1/4$. Equal marginals neither guarantee equal outcomes nor imply that one candidate must fail.

A **maximin guarantee** is a different test: choose a strategy for its worst payoff against arbitrary opposing strategies. A's same independent request probability $1/4$ gives $(-6)/4+0(3/4)=-3/2$ against B always Request. Its Nash payoff $3/4$ was conditional on B's equilibrium mix, not an insurance floor against every opponent. The special zero/constant-sum examples in lesson 05 support their own maximin guarantee; do not extend it to every general-sum Nash profile.

Nor is the private rule globally best. A public fair lottery between Request/Defer and Defer/Request uses weights **[0,1,1,0]**. Each announced pair is already a pure Nash cell, so full disclosure leaves both teams best-responding. Each team gets $3/2$, above the private rule's $5/4$. We have audited given proposals, not optimized every possible device.

| Law in the original game | Row-major weights | A's expected payoff | B's expected payoff | Private obedience |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Private rule | [0,1,1,2] | 5/4 | 5/4 | Passes |
| Same-marginal independent rule | [1,3,3,9] | 3/4 | 3/4 | Passes with ties |
| Uniform rule | [1,1,1,1] | -1/2 | -1/2 | Fails |
| Lottery over the pure Nash cells | [0,1,1,0] | 3/2 | 3/2 | Passes, also after full disclosure |

“Passes the incentive test”, “has a higher mean in this comparison”, “is globally optimal” and “will be used by people” are separate claims. An unconditional average cannot replace the message-by-message deviation test.

Keep the simultaneous choices, known law, private individual advice, voluntary responses and absence of other costs. First audit the old rule; then consider the proposed repair. Both candidate laws are given before the questions.

<details>
<summary>Check the complete transfer solution</summary>

Under the old rule, Defer still leaves three compatible copies. Following earns $2/3$; switching earns $(-1+3+3)/3=5/3$. The gain is one point. The pure Nash cells of the terminal game have not changed, but the old recommendation law fails.

For the repair, work through all four checks:

| Team | Own advice | Other Request weight | Other Defer weight | Compatible total | Follow | Switch |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | Request | 0 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| A | Defer | 2 | 1 | 3 | 1/3 | 1/3 |
| B | Request | 0 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| B | Defer | 2 | 1 | 3 | 1/3 | 1/3 |

After Defer, following gives $(2(0)+1)/3=1/3$; switching gives $(2(-1)+3)/3=1/3$. The exact tie is enough for **weak** obedience: following is a best response, but switching is another best response. A convention to follow when tied is compatible with the model; the arithmetic does not force it. The repair's unconditional payoff is $(2(3)+2(0)+1)/5=7/5$ for each team.

</details>

### Sources and scope

The private-information distinction builds on Robert J. Aumann's [Subjectivity and Correlation in Randomized Strategies](https://ma.huji.ac.il/~raumann/pdf/Subjectivity%20and%20Correlation.pdf), originally published in 1974; the linked file is an author-hosted reprint. MIT's [lecture on correlated equilibrium](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/correlated.html) gives the voluntary-mediator interpretation and separates zero-sum maximin results from general-sum Nash analysis. Every scanner payoff and ticket law here is constructed. These calculations establish conditional incentive comparisons under the stated assumptions, not real compliance, convergence, privacy policy or a globally optimal mechanism.
