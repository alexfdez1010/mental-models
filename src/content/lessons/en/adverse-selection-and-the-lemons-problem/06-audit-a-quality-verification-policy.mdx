A quality check can create more total surplus and still be a policy the buyer declines. To see why, keep three accounts separate: who enters, who gains, and when the inspection fee is paid.

This workshop uses one fictional archive file offered to one buyer. You will audit specified quotes and inspection policies, then test a different case. The prices and values are stipulated utility credits, not estimates of a real market.

## Fix the opportunity and the outside options

A **reservation value** is the seller's value of keeping the file. At price $p$, trading adds $p-r_t$ for a seller of type $t$ with reservation value $r_t$. The seller accepts whenever that surplus is nonnegative; acceptance in an exact tie is an explicit rule of this model. Keeping the file is the seller's outside option, with incremental surplus zero.

The buyer's surplus from a completed trade is $v_t-p$, where $v_t$ is the buyer's value. Declining gives zero before any fee. If H could be identified for free, a trade would create $22-18=4$ credits jointly; L would create $8-6=2$. The price divides that gain: it does not create it.

All values use a common stipulated cardinal scale. Both parties are risk-neutral, so they compare expectations on that scale. There is no other buyer, resale, liability, borrowing, entry cost or repeated trade. A common quote commits the buyer to pay that price if the seller accepts. Later we will introduce a different policy with an inspection and a decision after its report.

### When to specify the boundary

Write the outside options and timing before calling a trade attractive. A positive purchase payoff is not enough if an available policy pays more. These accounts describe this one-buyer model; they do not identify a competitive price or a bargaining equilibrium.

## Condition on the sellers who accept

At a quote of six, H declines because six is below eighteen. L accepts at its reservation value. Acceptance therefore occurs with probability one half. Given acceptance, the seller is L with probability one; the buyer's conditional value is eight.

That is **adverse selection** in this case: a quote filters privately known types. It changes the composition of the accepted pool without changing anyone's file. Conditional value and trade probability have different denominators. At six, the buyer gains two per completed trade but only one per potential opportunity:

$$P(\mathrm{accept})=\frac12,\qquad E[v\mid\mathrm{accept}]=8,\qquad U_B=\frac12(8-6)=1.$$

### When acceptance is information

Recompute the pool under the actual terms. If both types accept, acceptance need not change their proportions. If just one type accepts, ignoring the filter can misprice the opportunity. Neither observation by itself reveals a real seller's motive.

## Find the buyer's best common quote

<MCQ client:visible
  question={"Must a risk-neutral buyer pay its entire expected value whenever a seller accepts?"}
  options={[{"text":"Yes; otherwise the prior probabilities are inconsistent."},{"text":"Yes; otherwise the seller necessarily prefers no trade."},{"text":"No; a valuation ceiling alone does not choose a price.","correct":true},{"text":"No; conditional expectations cannot inform prices."}]}
  explanation={"The buyer may gain at a price below its conditional value. A competitive or bargaining price requires additional rules, not just an expectation."}
  pretest={true}
/>

The common quote can be any real $p\ge0$. We call it viable here when it attracts at least one type and gives the buyer nonnegative expected surplus. The buyer can also make no offer, receiving zero. This definition is a test of the stated policy, not an equilibrium prediction.

For $p<6$, nobody accepts. For $6\le p<18$, only L accepts, so

$$U_B(p)=\frac12(8-p).$$

It is nonnegative exactly up to eight and falls as the quote rises. For $p\ge18$, both types accept and

$$U_B(p)=\frac12(22-p)+\frac12(8-p)=15-p<0.$$

Thus the viable common quotes are exactly $6\le p\le8$. The buyer's maximum is one credit per opportunity at six. At seven, the buyer and seller each get one half; the joint gain remains one. Choosing seven to share the gain is a different objective from maximizing the buyer's surplus.

The mean value of a pool is a risk-neutral buyer's ceiling under a zero-surplus comparison, not a requirement to pay the whole mean. Competition or bargaining can add a price rule; this model stipulates a buyer who can commit its quote.

Change only the prior to H with probability three quarters and L with one quarter. If both accept, mean buyer value is now

$$\frac34\times22+\frac14\times8=18.5.$$

Quotes from eighteen through eighteen and a half retain both types with nonnegative expected buyer surplus. Hidden quality alone therefore does not force all good types out in every configuration. This counterexample changes neither the reserves nor the information available to the buyer.

### When a price rule matters

Separate an expectation calculation from the rule that chooses a payment. State whether a price is a committed quote, an auction payment, a competitive outcome or a stipulated update rule. An average is not all four.

## Audit an inspection policy

Return to the original half-and-half prior. The buyer now offers prices contingent on a perfect, truthful inspection: nineteen for H and seven for L. The seller chooses whether to enter before the buyer pays the nonnegative inspection fee $k$. Both types expect one credit above their reservation values and enter. The inspection reveals the type without changing quality; after the report, the buyer can buy at the promised contingent price or decline.

This inspection uses resources costing $k$ for each entered file. The buyer funds it, the inspector supplies it at cost, and there is no additional inspector rent. Count that real resource once in the joint account. Payments between buyer and seller are internal transfers and cancel when adding their surpluses.

Before the inspection fee, the buyer gets $22-19=3$ in H and $8-7=1$ in L: mean two. In either report, purchasing adds a positive amount relative to declining after the same fee. This continuation supports the entry calculation; the inspection itself is not an automatic reason to trade.

That fee threshold depends on the price split. Keeping the fee at one and a half but changing the contingent prices to eighteen and a half/six and a half leaves each seller one half. Buyer surplus becomes one; joint surplus remains one and a half. Both sellers strictly prefer entry, while the buyer ties its best common-quote alternative:

$$U_B=\frac12(22-18.5-1.5)+\frac12(8-6.5-1.5)=1.$$

Repricing redistributed surplus; it did not reduce inspection resources.

### When to compare private and joint accounts

Use the joint account to see resources created or consumed. Use each participant's account to check whether that participant wants the proposed policy, given its alternatives. The one-credit fee threshold belongs to one price schedule and prior; it is not a universal value of information or a best-mechanism theorem.

That does not contradict the earlier comparison: before paying, this policy had expected surplus one half, below the available common-quote policy's one.

The fee belongs in the earlier policy choice and in a complete historical account. It cancels only when comparing the two continuation options that share that already-paid fee. **Sunk** means unchanged by the present choice, not imaginary or harmless.

For a common quote below four, nobody enters. From four up to fourteen only L enters, so buyer surplus is $\frac34(10-p)$, nonnegative exactly when $p\le10$. From fourteen onward both enter, but their mean value is $\frac14\times20+\frac34\times10=12.5$, below that quote. The buyer's best common quote is therefore four, yielding $\frac34(10-4)=4.5$; seller surplus is zero.

At inspection prices seventeen/seven, the buyer gets three before the fee in either type and two afterward. Each seller gets three, so joint surplus is five. The buyer prefers its no-inspection alternative, despite the larger total.

At fourteen and a quarter/four and a quarter, buyer surplus after the fee is four and three quarters in either type. Each seller gets one quarter:

$$20-14.25-1=4.75,\qquad10-4.25-1=4.75.$$

Joint surplus is still five. Now the buyer exceeds its best common quote, and both sellers strictly prefer entry.

### When this audit travels

For another setting, define the hidden characteristic, prior, outside options, information technology, timing and feasible actions again. Inspection errors, strategic reports, risk preferences, rival offers, entry costs and repeat dealings can change the calculation. A real proposal needs evidence about those inputs; eagerness to trade does not supply them.
