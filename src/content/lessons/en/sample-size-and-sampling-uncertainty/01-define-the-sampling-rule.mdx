A sample size is a property of a collection protocol. Without that protocol, “two observations” leaves nearly everything open.

## Make the repeated experiment explicit

Our first model concerns a fixed set of eight inspection cards during one frozen audit period. Their classification is measured without error and does not change during collection. IDs A, B, C and D are marked; E, F, G and H are unmarked. The **target population** is all eight cards. Its fixed marked proportion is $P=4/8=1/2$. We reveal the full population here so that you can check a procedure; a real investigator may not know this target.

| ID | A | B | C | D | E | F | G | H |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Marked value | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

The **sampling frame** is the list from which selection is possible. Here it contains all eight IDs once. Select one unordered pair of distinct IDs uniformly: there are $8\times7/2=28$ such pairs, each with probability 1/28. No replacement means an ID cannot be drawn twice. This is a **simple random sample without replacement** of size $n=2$.

The **estimator** is a function of the collected values. Write $K$ for the marked count and $\hat P=K/n$ for the estimated proportion. The hat marks a data-dependent quantity. AB gives $K=2,\hat P=1$; AE gives $K=1,\hat P=1/2$; EF gives $K=0,\hat P=0$. These are possible results of the same protocol, not different target populations.

The three counts have probabilities 6/28, 16/28 and 6/28. Why 16 mixed pairs? There are four choices for the marked ID and four for the unmarked ID. A particular pair and a particular count have different denominators.

### When to use the specification

Write the protocol before attaching a probability to a sample result. Include the unit, time window, eligibility, replacement rule, selection probabilities and estimator. Uniformity is a feature stipulated or justified by the design; it is not a synonym for “we picked something”. Nonresponse or changing measurements would require additional rules.

## A precise answer to the wrong population

Now change the model. The target contains 12 fixed cards, six marked. Only six are accessible: five marked and one unmarked. The excluded six contain one marked and five unmarked. We measure the accessible frame completely, with the same error-free fixed criterion.

| Set | Marked | Total | Proportion |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Accessible frame | 5 | 6 | 5/6 |
| Excluded cards | 1 | 6 | 1/6 |
| Target population | 6 | 12 | 1/2 |

The frame census always reports 5/6. Relative to the target 1/2 it has **bias** $5/6-1/2=1/3$: its expectation minus the target. Its sampling variance is zero under this census rule. Its **mean squared error**, the average squared distance from the target, is $(1/3)^2=1/9$. Precision within the frame has not repaired the target mismatch.

### Transfer before the answer

A new fixed target has 20 cards: ten marked. A frame census measures eight cards containing six marked. Under the stated error-free census, calculate the reported proportion, its bias relative to the target, and its sampling variance. What would make this a census of the target?

<details>
<summary>Work through the frame audit</summary>

The report is $6/8=3/4$. The target is $10/20=1/2$, so bias is $1/4$ and squared error is $1/16$. The sampling variance is zero because every repeat of this fixed frame census returns 3/4. Measuring all 20 target cards would remove this particular frame mismatch. Merely duplicating the eight measured records would not. In practice, the unmeasured cards' classifications may be unknown; these counts are supplied to make the constructed audit reproducible.

</details>

### Limits and use

A small, correctly randomised sample can still vary widely. A large, systematically restricted frame can still miss the target. Neither observation proves that every convenience sample is biased by a particular amount. To transport a frame result, state and defend an additional relationship between frame and target; its truth is not supplied by $n$.
