An interval procedure is a machine: feed it data, receive a set of parameter values. Its coverage claim concerns the machine across repeated data collections.

## Construct first, audit separately

For this lesson, replace the finite-card design with $n$ independent, identically distributed Bernoulli trials of unknown fixed probability $p$. The marked count is $K$. Its **binomial law** assigns probability $\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ to count $k$; $\binom nk$ counts which $k$ positions are marked. This is not sampling distinct IDs from eight fixed cards.

An interval constructor receives only the observed count $k$, sample size $n$ and chosen method. It does **not** receive the true $p$. In our teaching audit we separately supply $p$ to determine which possible intervals include it, then add their sampling probabilities. That sum is **coverage** at $p$.

The first method is the familiar **Wald interval**, using the sample proportion $\hat p=k/n$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_W(k)=\Big[&\max\left(0,\hat p-\frac{49}{25}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n}\right),\\
&\min\left(1,\hat p+\frac{49}{25}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n}\right)\Big].
\end{aligned}
$$

We fix $49/25=1.96$ exactly in this recipe and call its nominal level 95%. This approximation is not an exact finite-coverage guarantee. For $n=4,p=1/2$, only counts 1, 2 and 3 cover the target. Their probabilities sum to $(4+6+4)/16=7/8$, or 87.5%.

At $k=0$, Wald reports [0,0]. Under $n=4,p=1/4$, that observation still has probability $(3/4)^4=81/256$. A zero plug-in standard error does not prove that $p=0$. [Brown, Cai and DasGupta’s technical report](https://www.stat.purdue.edu/~dasgupta/publications/tr99-19.pdf) documents the poor coverage behaviour of this approximation; our finite calculation stands on its displayed law.

## Exact equal tails and what the guarantee means

The second recipe is the closed **Clopper–Pearson equal-tail interval**, with nominal coverage $19/20$. For a candidate $p$, retain it after observing $k$ exactly when both inclusive binomial tails satisfy

$$\Pr_p(K\le k)\ge\frac1{40},\qquad\Pr_p(K\ge k)\ge\frac1{40}.$$

“Inclusive” includes the observed count. Equality is retained. This inverts two tail conditions to construct a set of candidate parameter values. The set is a closed interval. At $k=0$ its lower endpoint is 0; at $k=n$ its upper endpoint is 1. For $n=4,k=0$, the upper endpoint solves $(1-p)^4=1/40$, giving $1-(1/40)^{1/4}\approx0.6023646$.

The table rounds bounds outward to four decimal places. For example, [0.0675, 0.9325] encloses the exact endpoints for $n=4,k=2$. Membership is evaluated from the exact tail inequalities, not these rounded numbers. A candidate at a tail equality must not be discarded by display rounding.

| Audit model | Wald coverage | Exact equal-tail coverage |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $n=4,p=1/4$, independent | 81/128 | 255/256 |
| $n=4,p=1/2$, independent | 7/8 | 1 |
| $n=4,p=3/4$, independent | 81/128 | 255/256 |
| $n=8,p=1/2$, independent | 119/128 | 127/128 |

### Why the exact method covers at least its nominal level

Fix any $p$ in [0,1] and keep the independent binomial model. Counts excluded because their lower cumulative probability is below 1/40 form an initial block of counts. The probability of that whole block is its final cumulative probability, less than 1/40. Counts excluded because their upper tail is below 1/40 form a final block with probability less than 1/40. The probability of their union is at most the sum of those two probabilities, at most 1/20. Therefore the retained interval covers this fixed $p$ with probability at least 19/20.

This argument applies to every $p$, including the boundaries; it is not inferred from the five cases in the selector. The union bound does not require the two exclusion events to be independent. Discreteness can make the procedure **conservative**: actual coverage exceeds nominal coverage. [R’s official documentation](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/binom.test.html) states the coverage guarantee and warns that these intervals are not generally the shortest available.

Under the copied law, only $k=4$ covers $p=1/2$ for either recipe; it has probability 1/2. The exact binomial guarantee did not fail on its own assumptions: the data law changed. The same interval endpoints need not retain the same coverage under a new collection process.

### A width trap and a transfer

More observations need not make **every realised interval** narrower. Four initial zero outcomes give exact interval width approximately 0.6024. If four later outcomes are all ones, the eight-outcome count becomes four; its exact interval width is approximately 0.6860. These outcomes can occur under a fixed independent Bernoulli law. The first interval started near a boundary, while the second is centred.

Before opening the solution, audit this claim: “Our copied eight-row file gave count four, and the exact interval contains one half; therefore its procedure has at least ninety-five percent coverage for copied files.”

<details>
<summary>Separate this hit from the whole procedure</summary>

This realised interval does contain one half. Across copied files, however, counts zero and eight each occur with probability one quarter and miss one half. Only count four covers, with probability one half. One successful realised interval does not establish coverage. A repair requires a procedure justified under the actual two-bit collection law, not relabelling the same eight-row binomial method.

</details>

### When to use a coverage audit

Use it before interpreting an interval label as a reliability claim. State the parameter domain, sampling law, constructor and stopping rule. Equal-tail exact intervals offer a finite binomial guarantee under their assumptions, with potentially wide or conservative results. They do not cover arbitrary dependence, target shifts or data-dependent stopping automatically.
