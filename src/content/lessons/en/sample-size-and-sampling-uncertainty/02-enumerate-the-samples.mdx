The population is fixed; the estimate can move. To measure that movement, first list what can happen under the collection rule.

## Build the whole sampling distribution

Keep the eight IDs and uniform unordered pair rule from lesson one. There are six unmarked pairs: EF, EG, EH, FG, FH and GH. There are six marked pairs: AB, AC, AD, BC, BD and CD. Each of the four marked IDs pairs with each of the four unmarked IDs, giving 16 mixed pairs. That accounts for all 28.

A **sampling distribution** lists an estimator's values and their probabilities under repeated collection. It is a probability catalogue, not a promise that the next 28 collections will realise each pair once.

| Marked count $K$ | Sample proportion $\hat P$ | Pair count | Probability | Squared departure from 1/2 | Probability × squared departure |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 0 | 6 | 3/14 | 1/4 | 3/56 |
| 1 | 1/2 | 16 | 4/7 | 0 | 0 |
| 2 | 1 | 6 | 3/14 | 1/4 | 3/56 |

The **expectation**, written $E(\hat P)$, is the probability-weighted mean: $0(3/14)+(1/2)(4/7)+1(3/14)=1/2$. The estimator is **unbiased** for this target because its expectation equals $P$. A realised estimate can still miss.

The **variance** is $E[(\hat P-E\hat P)^2]=3/56+3/56=3/28$. It averages over the sampling law; there is no extra division by the number of rows in this table. The **standard error** is the standard deviation of the estimator, $\operatorname{SE}(\hat P)=\sqrt{3/28}\approx0.3273$. It is measured in proportion units. The variance uses squared proportion units.

## Why sampling more of a fixed population changes the rule

<MCQ client:visible
  className="not-prose"
  pretest={true}
  question={"Moving from two distinct cards to all eight, what happens to this estimator’s sampling variance?"}
  options={[{"text":"It becomes zero because every selection contains the whole fixed population.","correct":true},{"text":"It stays positive because the classifications are binary.","correct":false},{"text":"It becomes one eighth because there are eight rows.","correct":false}]}
  explanation={"With error-free measurement and the population fixed, every census returns exactly four eighths. There is no selection variation left."}
/>

For $n=4$, counts 0 through 4 arise from 1, 16, 36, 16 and 1 of the 70 equally likely sets. For example, $K=2$ chooses two of four marked IDs and two of four unmarked IDs: $6\times6=36$. Computing the weighted squared deviations gives variance $1/28$, three times smaller than the pair variance. The standard-error ratio is $1/\sqrt3$, rather than $1/\sqrt2$.

Here is the general finite-population result and why its conditions matter. A fixed population has size $M>1$ and marked proportion $P$. Sample $n$ distinct units uniformly. Let $X_i$ be the binary value at draw position $i$. Each draw has mean $P$ and variance $P(1-P)$. Distinct draw values are negatively associated in the following precise sense: their **covariance**, $E(X_iX_j)-E(X_i)E(X_j)$, is

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)=-\frac{P(1-P)}{M-1}\quad(i\ne j).$$

To verify it, write $H=MP$ for the integer marked population count. The probability that two distinct positions are both marked is $(H/M)((H-1)/(M-1))$. Subtract $P^2$ and simplify. Expanding the squared sum gives $n$ variance terms and $n(n-1)$ covariance terms. Dividing by $n^2$ gives

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat P)=\frac{P(1-P)}{n}\frac{M-n}{M-1}.$$

The last factor is the **finite-population correction** for the variance. For $M=8,P=1/2,n=2$ it gives $(1/8)(6/7)=3/28$. For $n=4$ it gives $(1/16)(4/7)=1/28$. At the census, $n=M$, it is zero.

### When the square-root rule applies

For independent, identically distributed binary draws with fixed probability $P$, covariance terms are zero, so $\operatorname{Var}(\hat P)=P(1-P)/n$. Quadrupling $n$ then halves the standard error. Without replacement from a small fixed population, the correction also changes. With dependence, the covariance terms must be checked. This is a claim about a specified estimator and law, not a universal reward for adding rows.

## Transfer to a different finite population

Two of ten fixed cards are marked. Draw two distinct IDs uniformly. Before opening the solution, find the complete count probabilities, expected sample proportion and variance.

<details>
<summary>Enumerate and verify the new catalogue</summary>

There are $10\times9/2=45$ pairs. An unmarked pair chooses two of eight unmarked IDs: $8\times7/2=28$. A mixed pair chooses one of two marked and one of eight unmarked: 16. The two marked IDs form one pair. Thus the weights are 28, 16 and 1.

$E\hat P=(1/2)(16/45)+1/45=1/5$. The weighted second moment is $(1/4)(16/45)+1/45=1/9$. Variance is $1/9-(1/5)^2=16/225$. The finite-population formula independently gives $(1/5)(4/5)(1/2)(8/9)=16/225$. Standard error is $4/15$.

A complete finite catalogue supports exact calculations. A large simulated sample would only approximate that catalogue and would add simulation variation. Use enumeration when the state space is small enough; use a justified model or approximation when it is not, while preserving the distinction between model uncertainty and simulation error.
